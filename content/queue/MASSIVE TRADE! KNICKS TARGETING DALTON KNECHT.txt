## THE REPORTS

Here's what we know. According to BasketNews, after clearing waivers on Wednesday, former Lakers wing Dalton Knecht is drawing interest from a handful of teams as a potential two-way target — something Jake Fischer said during his latest Bleacher Report live stream.

Then Ian Begley confirmed the Knicks' piece of it: the Knicks are among the teams with interest in former Laker Dalton Knecht, per league sources familiar with his market. Begley also noted that Knecht was waived before the regular season and is eligible to sign a two-way deal. And The Stein Line reported earlier that Minnesota has interest as well.

So this is a real market, with real competition, for a player who just two seasons ago was one of the most exciting rookies in the league.

## WHO IS DALTON KNECHT?

Let's refresh the memory. Knecht was the 17th overall pick in the 2024 NBA draft after a breakout season at the University of Tennessee that put him on every draft board. He's 6-foot-6, a wing with a quick, confident shooting stroke and the ability to score in bunches.

And early in his rookie year, he looked like a steal. Over a nine-game stretch from mid-to-late November, Knecht averaged 18.9 points while shooting 55.4 percent from the field — and an incredible 52.2 percent from three — along with 4.9 rebounds and 1.3 assists per game. That's not a fluke night. That's two-plus weeks of scoring like a starter in the Lakers' rotation.

When he gets hot, he can catch fire from deep faster than almost anyone his age. That's the skill everybody remembers.

## THE TRADE THAT BROKE HIS MOMENTUM

So what happened? Here's the part of the story that matters most. Knecht's play started to decline after his name got pulled into trade talks — and one trade in particular.

In February 2025, the Lakers agreed to trade Knecht, along with Cam Reddish and draft picks, to the Charlotte Hornets for center Mark Williams. And then the trade was rescinded — after Williams failed his physical. Knecht was sent back to the team that had just tried to move him.

Think about that from a young player's perspective. In the middle of your rookie season, your team tells the world it's willing to trade you. Then the deal falls apart for reasons that have nothing to do with you, and you have to walk back into that locker room. That left Knecht in a precarious spot within the organization. And ever since, his play got worse. The confidence, the rhythm, the role — all of it slipped. Eventually, the Lakers waived him before this regular season.

Sometimes a player needs a fresh start more than anything else. A new environment. A team that actually believes in him. And it's worth remembering how young he still is in NBA terms — he's only in his third professional season. Plenty of shooters have lost their touch for a year and found it again once the pressure lifted and the role became clear.

## WHY THE KNICKS? THE RECLAMATION TRACK RECORD

And honestly, it's not surprising that New York is one of the teams interested. This front office has a long track record of taking chances on players who couldn't find a home — and turning them into contributors.

Josh Hart bounced around before he became the heart of a championship team. Isaiah Hartenstein built his value in New York before turning it into a big contract with Oklahoma City. Landry Shamet came in on an Exhibit 9 deal and became a $24 million champion. This season, Andre Drummond came in leaner and dominated the first half of the opener, and James Wiseman — a former number two pick nobody wanted — won the final roster spot.

Knecht fits that profile perfectly: talented, young, overlooked, and in need of a new environment.

## WHY THE KNICKS? THE SHOOTING NEED

And the timing could not be more on point. Look at the shooting in the opener against Philadelphia. The Knicks shot 37.2 percent from the field and 33 percent from three. OG Anunoby went one-of-nine from deep. Mikal Bridges scored four points on two-of-eight shooting. Landry Shamet went one-of-four from three. Deuce McBride went one-of-five from the floor. Jalen Brunson shot six-of-19.

One game is one game. But it's a reminder that the Knicks could use another wing who can get hot from three. That's exactly Knecht's calling card. On a two-way deal, he wouldn't be expected to play every night — but as an emergency shooter off the bench, or a spark on nights when the shots aren't falling, he could be a real weapon.

## THE SKILL PROFILE: WHAT KNECHT DOES WELL

Let's get specific about the player, because "shooter" doesn't tell the whole story.

**Catch-and-shoot range.** His best skill. When he's in rhythm, he doesn't need much space — a quick release and deep range make him a threat the moment he catches the ball.

**Shooting on the move.** Knecht isn't just a spot-up guy. He can come off screens and handoffs and fire, which is exactly the kind of action Mike Brown runs with KAT as a high-post hub.

**Attacking closeouts.** When defenders fly at him, he can put the ball on the floor and get to the rim or into a pull-up. That's what made his November stretch so dangerous: teams had to respect the three, and then he'd drive by them.

**Transition scoring.** At 6-foot-6, he runs the floor well and finishes in the open court.

**The concerns.** Defense has been the big question — staying in front of quicker wings and making the right rotations. And his confidence clearly took a hit after the rescinded trade. Those are fixable, but they're real.

## HOW HE'D FIT NEXT TO BRUNSON AND KAT

Now imagine him in the Knicks' system. Brunson draws two defenders on almost every drive. KAT pulls bigs away from the rim and runs the offense from the top of the key. What does that offense need most around those two? Wings who punish help defense by making open threes.

That's Knecht. On the nights the Knicks' wings go cold — like OG and Bridges did in the opener — a two-way shooter who can come in and hit a couple of threes changes the math. He wouldn't be a rotation player every night. But as a change-of-pace scorer, he's the type of weapon a deep team can keep in its back pocket.

## THE HARTENSTEIN LESSON

There's another angle worth mentioning. Even when the Knicks' reclamation projects leave, the team often benefits along the way. Isaiah Hartenstein came to New York, played a key role, built his value, and turned it into a big deal in Oklahoma City. The Knicks got the best years of his development when they needed them most.

With Knecht, the same logic applies. On a two-way deal, if he rebuilds his value in Westchester and New York, the Knicks either get a cheap shooter for their bench or a developed young player they could eventually convert to a standard deal — or even use as an asset. Low risk, multiple ways to win.

## WHAT THIS SAYS ABOUT THE FRONT OFFICE

And the interest itself tells us something. The season has started. The roster is set. The Knicks just played their opener. And yet the front office is still scanning the market for value. That's a group that knows exactly where its weak spots are.

Before the season, the Knicks wanted another wing — that's why they nearly kept Bruce Brown. They had to choose size with Wiseman because of the apron. Mohamed Diawara's role is still uncertain. Deuce McBride's minutes shrank on opening night, and his name keeps coming up in trade speculation. If the Knicks can find a wing who can shoot without touching the apron, they're going to look. Knecht is that kind of opportunity.

## THE PROBLEM: THE SECOND APRON

Now, here's where it gets complicated. The Knicks can't simply sign Knecht to a standard contract. They're right up against the second apron, and adding another standard or veteran-minimum deal would push them over James Dolan's line. That's the same math that cost them Bruce Brown before the season.

Could they open room by moving Pacôme Dadiet? In theory, yes — through a salary-dump trade. But according to this reporting, the Knicks tried that before the season, and nothing happened. Moving Dadiet would likely require attaching multiple second-round picks, and even then there's no guarantee a team would take him. That's why the Knicks kept him and preserved his contract as a trade tool. So for now, the standard-contract route is basically closed.

That leaves one realistic path: a two-way contract.

## THE TWO-WAY PUZZLE

And this is where it gets tricky. The Knicks' three two-way spots are already filled: Tyler Nickel, Jaden Akins, and Kevin McCullar Jr. To sign Knecht to a two-way, one of them would have to be waived. So let's go through each one.

**Tyler Nickel.** He's on a two-year two-way deal, and the Knicks have invested in him as a shooter. He just got that contract. It's very hard to see the Knicks waiving him this quickly.

**Jaden Akins.** Ian Begley just reported that the Knicks have been impressed by Akins — his play and his approach to his craft haven't gone unnoticed, and the organization is optimistic about his future as a cheap, team-friendly piece under the apron. He's not going anywhere.

**Kevin McCullar Jr.** By process of elimination, he's the most likely candidate. If you watched our "McCullar Crossroads" video, you know we flagged this exact risk: the two-way deal that makes him easy to keep also makes him easy to replace. He's 25, in his third season with the organization, and the Knicks traded Oso Ighodaro's draft rights to get him. The organization clearly believes in him — they brought him back for a reason. But if a former first-round pick with Knecht's shooting upside becomes available, the front office would have to at least think about it.

## THE OTHER TWO-WAY CANDIDATE: A CENTER

And there's another layer to the puzzle. Remember, the Knicks still have zero centers on two-way deals. After Drew Eubanks' torn pectoral muscle and Tony Bradley's release, their center depth is Karl-Anthony Towns, Andre Drummond, and James Wiseman. Former Knick Trey Jemison was waived by Toronto and is two-way eligible. So if one two-way spot opens, the Knicks have to choose: a shooting wing like Knecht, or insurance at center like Jemison?

After opening night — when KAT's foul trouble forced Drummond into heavy minutes and he faded in the second half — center depth looks like the bigger need. But Knecht's talent level is higher. That's a real dilemma, and how the front office answers it will tell you a lot about how they see this roster.

## THE COMPETITION

And the Knicks aren't alone. Minnesota has shown interest, and Fischer says a handful of teams view Knecht as a two-way target. That matters. A player in Knecht's position will likely choose the team that offers the clearest path to minutes and development. Some rebuilding teams can promise more playing time than a defending champion. The Knicks' pitch would be different: a winning environment, a proven development pipeline, Westchester, and a front office with a track record of turning overlooked players into real contributors.

## WHAT A TWO-WAY KNECHT WOULD LOOK LIKE

If it happens, here's what to expect. On a two-way deal, Knecht would split time between the Knicks and Westchester, with limits on how many NBA games he could be active for. He'd likely get big minutes in the G League to rebuild his confidence and rhythm, then serve as an emergency shooter in New York when injuries or cold nights hit. If he gets back to anything close to his November rookie form, he could force his way into a bigger role — and potentially, down the line, a standard contract.

And at his age, the upside is long-term too. Alongside Akins, Dink Pate, Tyler Kolek, and Mohamed Diawara, Knecht could become part of the young, cheap core the Knicks need to stay competitive under the apron.

## THE RISKS

Let's keep it honest. Knecht isn't a perfect player. His defense has been a question. His consistency disappeared after that early hot stretch. And the fact that the Lakers waived a former first-round pick tells you they'd lost faith. There's real work to do: rebuilding confidence, tightening his defense, and proving the November version wasn't a mirage.

But on a two-way deal, the cost is basically zero. Two-way contracts don't count against the apron. If it doesn't work, the Knicks can move on. If it does, they've found a shooter for almost nothing. That's the kind of low-risk, high-upside bet this front office loves — the same logic behind Wiseman, Shamet, and every other overlooked player who found a home at the Garden.
