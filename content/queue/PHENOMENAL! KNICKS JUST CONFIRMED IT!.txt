THE CERTAINTY: ANOTHER BIG IS COMING

Let's start with the one thing we can say with total confidence, because we've opened a hundred videos this way and the story hasn't changed: the front office IS adding another big man. It's happening. The only questions are who and how — a two-way deal, a trade, a veteran-minimum signing, a training camp invite. Every route remains on the table, and new names keep cycling through the machine. But today's name is different, because today's name just spent June trying to break our hearts.

ENTER KELLY OLYNYK — THE FINALS RIVAL

Per NBA insider Jake Weinbach, the Knicks will likely fill their 14th roster spot by signing a big man to a minimum contract — and Kelly Olynyk is among the top remaining centers on the market who should interest New York. The cap math checks out perfectly: the Knicks sit roughly $3.3 million below the second apron, and a minimum deal slides in WITHOUT crossing that forbidden line. Yahoo's framing was even stronger, calling it a potentially strong addition because Olynyk "can do a little bit of everything" — bang inside when needed, stretch the floor from deep, and yes... he just LOST to the Knicks in the NBA Finals with San Antonio. Read that twist again: the veteran who watched the confetti fall on someone else's championship could walk into the champion's locker room weeks later. The man has earned over $121 million across a long, respected career — at this stage, he's not chasing checks. He's chasing the one thing New York just proved it can provide.

And don't sleep on the intel angle either, because it's real. Olynyk just spent a season inside the San Antonio Spurs organization — the team every projection has circled as New York's most likely Finals rematch opponent. He knows how that locker room thinks, how Wembanyama likes to be attacked and how he does NOT, what the coaching staff drilled all year, which buttons rattle that young core. We made this exact point in reverse when Hukporti took our playbook to Philadelphia — institutional knowledge travels, and it matters. Signing Olynyk isn't just adding a body; it's downloading a scouting report on your biggest rival, delivered by a smart, experienced pro who's been in the league long enough to know exactly which details are worth sharing.

And the fit is more interesting than a typical minimum signing, because Olynyk brings the one ingredient this center search hasn't offered yet: positional FLUIDITY. Think about the lineup toys this hands Mike Brown. Olynyk can play the five, obviously. But he can also slide to a legitimate power forward next to Andre Drummond — a twin-big look with actual spacing, because Olynyk's jumper keeps the paint open in a way most backup bigs never could. Or flip it: Olynyk at center, OG Anunoby taking the toughest frontcourt assignment, and suddenly you've recreated the KAT-style five-out spacing with the SECOND unit. We know Mike Brown lives for these laboratory lineups — he spent all of last season proving that flexibility wins playoff rounds. A big man who can wear three different hats on a minimum contract is exactly the kind of tool this coaching staff knows how to weaponize.

There's also a dimension of Olynyk's game that the box score has never captured: the man can PASS. For over a decade he's been one of the league's sneaky-good playmaking bigs — the dribble-handoff maestro, the guy who reads the backdoor cutter a half-second before it happens, the connective tissue that makes second units flow instead of stagnate. That skill is quietly enormous for this roster. When Brunson sits and the bench mob takes over, offense can turn into isolation soup — but run it through a big who can catch at the elbow, survey the floor, and deliver on time, and suddenly Shamet's off-ball movement, Clarkson's cutting, and Alvarado's chaos all get easier. High-IQ veterans who make everyone around them smarter are exactly what the minimum-contract shelf almost never offers. Olynyk is the exception.

THE HONEST FILE — BOTH SIDES

Now, we keep it a hundred on this channel, so here come the concerns, and they're real. Olynyk barely played last season. When he did, the three-ball didn't fall the way his reputation promises. He is NOT a shot blocker — never has been. And he's not a great defender, which stings, because rim protection and defensive presence are supposedly the exact traits this front office has been hunting since Mitchell Robinson walked. If the mission statement is "young, cheap, shot-blocking," Olynyk is one-for-three: cheap. That's the case against.

But here's the case FOR, and it's built on receipts. When Kelly Olynyk gets real minutes, Kelly Olynyk PRODUCES — that's been true for a decade. The Houston stretch where he looked reborn. The Utah run: 12 points a game on 40% from three. Solid, professional basketball in Toronto just a few seasons back. And with the Pelicans: nine a night on a scorching 42% from deep. This isn't a guy whose skills evaporated; it's a guy whose OPPORTUNITIES did. Veterans with a defined skill — and floor-spacing at seven feet is one of the most valuable defined skills in basketball — tend to snap right back to form when a real role reappears. On a team whose entire offensive identity is five-out spacing, a career 3-point-shooting big fits the scheme like he was grown in a lab for it. No, he doesn't check the defense box. But he checks the system box harder than almost anyone left on the market.

And let's paint the actual rotation picture, because abstractions are boring. Imagine a February back-to-back: KAT logs 30 the night before, Drummond's knee is barking, and Mike Brown needs 15 frontcourt minutes he can trust. Option A tonight is Olynyk at the five with Alvarado-Shamet-Clarkson-Diawara around him — four willing movers orbiting a big who spaces to the arc and keeps the ball humming. Option B is the jumbo look: Drummond parked at the five eating glass, Olynyk at the four dragging the opposing backup center twenty-five feet from the rim, driving lanes wide open for whoever attacks. Option C, closing lineups against small-ball teams: Olynyk the nominal center, OG on the opponent's biggest threat, and five shooters on the floor at once. None of those are fantasy — they're Tuesday-night solutions, and Tuesday-night solutions are what 82-game titles are quietly built on.

THE FULL MARKET MAP

And speaking of the market, let's zoom out and run the entire remaining board, because the picture is clarifying fast. Kevin Love? Almost certainly headed to Philadelphia or Minnesota for one final season — cross him off. Dwight Powell? No. Bismack Biyombo? Doubtful at this point. Drew Eubanks — as we broke down before — is the prime training-camp-deal candidate, and expect exactly that kind of offer. Xavier Tillman? Another camp-invite profile. Mason Plumlee? Probably not happening. Olivier Sarr and a couple of the younger developmental bigs? Two-way territory. Tony Bradley? A camp look wouldn't shock anyone. Slide over to the power forward shelf and it thins out even faster — Guerschon Yabusele appears headed back overseas, and Olivier-Maxence Prosper is a pure four who'd be a scary-small small-ball five. That's the whole store, folks. And when you inventory it honestly, Olynyk stands out as arguably the most PROVEN offensive player left standing — which is exactly why his name surfaced now, with camp bearing down and the front office likely preparing multiple training-camp invites to battle it out. Don't be surprised if Eubanks, Olynyk, and a young center or two all get thrown into the same October cage match. May the best big win.

One more strategic note on that market map: notice how the tiers have sorted themselves. There's a camp-invite tier, a two-way tier, and a "not happening" tier — and almost nobody left commands a guaranteed deal outright. That's LEVERAGE for New York. When no remaining free agent has a strong market, the champs get to run auditions instead of negotiations, dangling the most valuable currency in basketball — a real chance at a ring on a title favorite — without guaranteeing a dime. Expect the front office to milk that advantage all the way to opening night.

THE OG PROBLEM — LET'S TALK ABOUT IT

Now for the thread running underneath ALL of these options, because it's the part I care about most. Notice the common theme of nearly every name on that market map: none of them are real defensive anchors. Which means, in lineup after hypothetical lineup, the plan quietly becomes "...and OG Anunoby guards the center." And look — OG CAN do it. He's been the best defender on this team since the moment he arrived, he's switched onto bigs plenty, and he's coming off an All-Defensive season with legitimate All-Star buzz building for this year. But there's a massive difference between OG taking big-man assignments in SPURTS and OG absorbing that punishment as a nightly job description. You are not parking 6'7" OG on Nikola Jokić for 35 possessions. You're not feeding him to Joel Embiid or letting Bam Adebayo bludgeon him on the block for a full series. Those are heavyweight fights, and asking your most valuable perimeter defender to moonlight as a heavyweight all season is how you wear down a cornerstone before the games that matter.

Last year, the math worked because Mitch existed — the big-body insurance that let OG's switches stay situational. That insurance policy now plays in Boston. Yes, KAT rose to the occasion magnificently in the Finals against Wembanyama, and his history against Jokić and Embiid is genuinely strong — the man gets UP for marquee matchups. Yes, Drummond is a big body whose defense has aged better than his reputation. But "KAT gets up for big games" and "Drummond is fine" cannot be the entire defensive plan at the center position for an 82-game title defense. That's the deeper reason this third-big decision keeps dragging on: the front office isn't just filling a roster spot, it's deciding how much burden lands on OG's shoulders from October through June. Sign a pure spacer like Olynyk and you're implicitly betting MORE on OG's versatility. That bet might win — but let's at least name it out loud.

THE BOTTOM LINE

So here's where we land. Kelly Olynyk to the champs is a real, sensible, second-apron-proof option — a proven system fit with a poetic Finals twist, likely as one contestant in a training camp battle royale rather than a handed job. He solves the offense side of the third-big equation brilliantly and leaves the defense side exactly as unsolved as it is today. And hovering over everything: the McBride trade chip still sits in the vault for February, when a REAL defensive big could still arrive the expensive way. The minimum signing buys time. The deadline buys the answer. Watch this front office run both plays at once — because that's exactly what they've done all summer.
