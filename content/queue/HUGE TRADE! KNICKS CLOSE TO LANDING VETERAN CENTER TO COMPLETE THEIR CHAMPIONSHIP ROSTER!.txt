SIMMONS PICKS SACRAMENTO — AND WHY THAT'S PERFECTLY FINE

It's done: Ben Simmons has signed a one-year deal with the Sacramento Kings. The 30-year-old chose the situation that had been circling him for weeks — remember, we told you about Scott Perry personally flying to Florida for that workout — and the window we discussed slammed shut exactly the way we warned it might. But here's the detail that matters for us: The Athletic's Sam Amick confirmed the Knicks were among the small group of teams with GENUINE interest right until the end. New York was in the room. They evaluated. They just didn't overextend.

And honestly? That's not a loss — that's a window into how this front office thinks. Simmons at his best checks fascinating boxes: a long, switchable defender who can guard one through five, initiate offense in transition, and impact winning without ever touching the scoring column. Next to elite scorers like Brunson and KAT, the theoretical fit was real, and we made that case ourselves on this channel. But the questions never went away either — durability, consistency, whether he could hold up across a full season of championship-level demands. Those concerns followed him through his Clippers stint and they didn't vanish this summer. So the Knicks did exactly what a confident champion should do: they kicked the tires hard, set their price, and let him walk when the math didn't clear the bar. No panic. No reaching. On to the next.

And spare a thought for what Simmons himself chose, because it's revealing too. In New York he'd have been a fifteenth man on a stacked champion — minutes uncertain, role defined by others, every mistake magnified under the brightest lights in sports. In Sacramento he gets runway: a rebuilding roster, a front office that flew across the country to court him, and the space to rebuild his career at his own pace. For a player coming off a year away from the game, that's a rational choice, and we genuinely wish him well. But it also confirms something about the Knicks' pitch: they were never going to promise what they couldn't deliver. This roster doesn't hand out roles as recruiting gifts — you EARN your minutes here, champion's rules. Simmons wanted certainty. New York sells competition. Both sides read the situation correctly.

THE 24 HOURS THAT REVEALED EVERYTHING

Now here's where it gets genuinely interesting, because look at the reporting timeline. Less than 24 hours before the Simmons news broke, Ian Begley of SNY reported two OTHER names on New York's radar: three-and-D wing John Konchar and veteran big man Drew Eubanks. Now hold those three players up next to each other. Konchar: a perimeter shooter and off-ball defender. Eubanks: an old-school, physical, paint-dwelling energy center. Simmons: a playmaking, transition-running point-forward. Three players who could not possibly be more different — all surfacing in the same news cycle.

That's not a front office confused about what it wants. That's a front office refusing to be pinned down. There is no fixed profile for this last roster spot, no predetermined skill set, no price point they're locked into. They're casting the widest possible net and waiting — because they CAN wait. Remember the rule: teams must carry between 14 and 15 standard contracts, and New York sits at exactly 14. They're compliant. There is zero obligation to fill that fifteenth chair before opening night. And as Begley noted weeks ago, if the Knicks truly loved anyone left on this market, the signing would already be done. The silence IS the evaluation. When you're the defending champs with a top-heavy juggernaut already built, a fifteenth man who can't genuinely contribute is just a name on a spreadsheet — and going into the season with 14, preserving flexibility for a midseason addition or a deadline move, might flat-out be the smarter play.

THE EUBANKS FILE — AND THE CAMP BATTLE STRATEGY

Let's zoom in on Eubanks though, because his name tells a bigger story. The profile: 29 years old, eight NBA seasons, and exactly the kind of motor coaches fall in love with — relentless on the boards, competitive defensively, happy to do the invisible dirty work that never trends on social media but wins Tuesday nights in February. On paper, a natural theoretical fit for the third-big role, which is why his name has floated around New York for months.

But the honest file has red flags too. Last season with a 22-win Sacramento team, Eubanks managed just 13 minutes a night — around five points and three rebounds — and his year was wrecked by injury twice: first a fractured thumb, then a torn UCL in the same thumb that ended his season in March. Full health at camp is the non-negotiable starting point for any conversation.

And here's the strategic read between the lines — the part I find genuinely smart. The Knicks spent the whole summer chasing bigger fish: Jonas Valančiūnas, Moussa Diabaté, Yves Missi. None of it landed. So with camp weeks away and the market picked clean, the pivot is taking shape: bring in MULTIPLE candidates on non-guaranteed or minimum-level deals, throw them into a genuine training camp competition, and let the floor decide who earns the backup-big minutes. Eubanks fits that framework like a glove — experienced enough to push everyone around him, hungry enough after an unsigned summer to empty the tank every practice, and cheap enough that there's zero financial risk in the look. Best case, he wins the job and you found your guy for free. Worst case, he raises the bar for whoever beats him. Either way the standard goes up.

And history says never dismiss the camp-battle route, because championship benches get built this way all the time. Around the league, some of the most valuable role players of recent years walked in through exactly this door — unsigned in September, indispensable by May. A hungry veteran fighting for his NBA life in October often gives you more than a comfortable name signed in July ever would. If the Garden crowd falls in love with whoever claws his way onto this roster, remember: that's not luck, that's design.

One more honest observation, though — and this one the front office probably doesn't want said out loud. A team quietly investigating stopgap centers is NOT a team with a blockbuster trade sitting in the chamber. If a Missi-level deal were close, nobody's calling Drew Eubanks. Which means we should brace for the real possibility that camp, not a trade, answers the backup-big question — at least until February 11th rolls around and the Deuce McBride chip comes back into play. Is that cause for alarm? No. KAT remains one of the most skilled offensive centers alive, this championship was built on collective effort, and the third-big role needs to be reliable and physical, not spectacular. But let's see this clearly for what it is: patience with a plan B, not a plan A in motion.

THE RIVALRY OF THE DECADE: KNICKS-SPURS IS REAL

And now, the main course — because while the roster margins get sorted, something much bigger is brewing on the horizon. The New York Knicks are the reigning champions. The San Antonio Spurs are the team that pushed them hardest on the way there. And the basketball world may already be watching the birth of one of the great rivalries in league history.

Here's what makes it special: this one wasn't born from geography or division standings or decades of playoff bad blood. No shared border, no bragging rights, no ancient grudge. What exists between these teams is rarer and honestly more compelling — a PURE basketball rivalry, built on competition, mutual respect, and two players seemingly destined to spend the next decade trying to outdo each other on the biggest stages the sport can offer: Jalen Brunson and Victor Wembanyama.

And the receipts go back further than people remember. March 2024 — the night that announced everything. Brunson hangs SIXTY on the Spurs. And Wemby, refusing to be a footnote, answers with 40 points and 20 rebounds — a stat line that headlines any other game ever played. The Knicks took the win, and then came the moment that defined the whole thing: Wembanyama took the game ball and sent it into the stands — a gesture of respect for Brunson's night, and maybe, without knowing it, the opening ceremony of a rivalry where neither man would ever accept the last word belonging to the other. Then Christmas Day. Then the Emirates NBA Cup championship round in Vegas in the fall of 2025. Every meeting stacking another chapter onto a story that already felt bigger than any single game.

And then... the 2026 NBA Finals. The grandest stage, and it delivered EVERYTHING. Remember the full arc of that run: Mike Brown's team went down 2-1 in the very first round, overhauled its whole approach, and rode total collective buy-in to a rampage through the East with victory margins that put them in all-time-great playoff company. But the Finals demanded one last act of resilience — and Game 4 gave us the largest single-game comeback in Finals history, the night that saved the series and broke San Antonio's heart. Five games. Brunson, Finals MVP on a wrecked wrist. A city's 53-year wait, over.

And think about why this particular matchup cooks the way it does — because styles make fights. Brunson is everything scouts said couldn't headline a title team: barely 6'2", no elite athleticism, a craftsman who wins with footwork, angles, and a basketball IQ that borders on precognition. Wembanyama is the opposite pole of the sport — a 7'5" being who breaks the geometry of the game itself, the kind of talent that arrives once in a generation and rewrites what's possible. The chess master versus the cheat code. Old-school craft versus new-world physics. Every time they share a floor, basketball gets to argue with itself about what greatness even means — and THAT is the ingredient no marketing department can manufacture.

Now look forward, because this thing has lost NONE of its heat. San Antonio returns one of the scariest young cores in basketball — Wemby flanked by Dylan Harper and Stephon Castle, a group that only gets better — plus the veteran ballast of Tobias Harris and a serious head coach in Billy Donovan. Those aren't rebuilding moves; those are "we're coming back for the belt" moves. And New York? Almost entirely intact, chemistry deepened by the fire of that playoff run, extensions in the works to keep the core together. Sure, other rivalries exist — the Pacers ended back-to-back Knicks runs before the breakthrough, and OKC lurks in the Spurs' own conference. But none of them carry this mix: cross-conference drama, generational star power on both sides, and championship stakes already banked. Wembanyama arrives next season with new teammates, a new coach, and new ideas for the Brunson problem. The Knicks arrive as champions with a roster built to answer. This rivalry is real. It's organic. And it is only getting started.
