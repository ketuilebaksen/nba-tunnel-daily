FILE ONE: MOUSSA DIABATÉ — THE POSSESSION MONSTER

Start with my guy, because the deeper you dig on Diabaté, the more the obsession makes sense. The headline numbers: 8 points, 9 rebounds, 2 assists, and a block a night on a scorching 63% from the field. Now the percentile profile, because this is where scouts start drooling: elite true shooting, 93rd percentile in rebounding, 92nd percentile in blocks. And then the quote that should be printed on the front office wall — Basketball University flat-out called him "the BEST player in the entire NBA at winning the possession battle last season. Truly a world-class offensive rebounder. An absolute chore to play against. Extremely high-impact hoops." The best in the league at winning possessions. Read that again and think about what it means: every offensive rebound is a stolen possession, every stolen possession is a bonus three for a team that shoots them — and no team weaponizes extra possessions like New York.

And here's the tactical wrinkle from his own season that proves the fit. Remember what actually unlocked Diabaté's run in Charlotte: when Ryan Kalkbrenner went down injured, Diabaté stepped in — and the Hornets' entire three-point bombing offense suddenly WORKED, because as one viral breakdown put it, "it was either going in, or Moussa was getting the offensive rebound." That's the eraser effect: shoot without fear, because the big man turns your misses into second chances. Now transplant that role to the Garden. The Knicks live and die by the five-out three-ball. Attach the league's premier possession thief to that offense and every cold stretch gets a safety net. Diabaté doesn't need plays called — the man even flashed a corner three and a developing mid-range this year, but that's garnish. The meal is the dirty work: 63% finishing, glass domination, rim protection, and a motor that never idles.

One more stat that explains EVERYTHING about why the Knicks want him now: on the league's talent-over-salary rankings — the players producing furthest above their contracts — the list runs Wembanyama, Jalen Duren, Donovan Clingan... and Diabaté fifth, despite playing CONSIDERABLY fewer minutes than everyone above him. Adjust for opportunity and he might top that list. He's in the final year of his deal, his role in Charlotte is genuinely unclear behind their crowded frontcourt, and his market value hasn't caught up to his impact yet. That's the definition of a closing window: acquire him now, keep the minutes modest, and — just like the Hornets fans fear — sign him below market before the league fully wakes up. That's not a trade. That's a heist with paperwork.

And if the Mitch comparison feels like hype, put the profiles side by side honestly. Robinson's superpower was turning misses into possessions — the offensive glass, the tip-outs, the demoralizing second chances. That's EXACTLY the crown Diabaté now wears, per the possession-battle data, except he pairs it with softer hands as a finisher and flashes of range Mitch never developed. Robinson's weakness was availability — the injury file that ultimately priced New York out of keeping him. Diabaté has no such file. Same archetype, younger body, cleaner medical history, and a skill curve still pointing up. Nobody replaces a beloved champion emotionally. Statistically? This might be the rare case where the replacement out-profiles the original.

FILE TWO: YVES MISSI — THE OLD FLAME

Now the other file, and this one comes with history. The Knicks have wanted Yves Missi for YEARS — I mean that literally. When they made the Alvarado trade with New Orleans, they tried to pull Missi into that same deal. Rejected. They've come back since — rejected again, and again. This is the longest-running pursuit on the board, and the affection has never cooled.

The profile explains why: 6 points, 6 rebounds, and 1.5 blocks in 19.6 minutes a night — and savor that minutes number for a second, because 19.6 is the EXACT figure Mitchell Robinson played last season. Same role, same workload, nearly identical output, except Missi is years younger and still climbing. We've quoted his defensive metrics before — the 84th percentile rim protection — and everything about his game screams the archetype: vertical spacing, mobility, shot-blocking instincts, an engine on the glass. And the opportunity squeeze in New Orleans is real: between Derik Queen's arrival and Zion Williamson's minutes, Missi's runway keeps shrinking on a team going nowhere. He's in year three of his four-year rookie deal — cheap now, extension leverage later — and every month the Pelicans stall, his situation gets more awkward and New York's patience gets more valuable.

Now the honest tiebreaker between the two files, since you're going to ask. Diabaté wins on offensive impact — the possession-stealing is a genuine superpower, and his motor turns every Knicks miss into a coin flip. Missi wins on pure rim protection — the points-saved data we've covered puts him a tier up as a defensive anchor, and his extra year of team control is worth real money under the apron. Diabaté's path is cheaper to acquire (expiring deal, murky role) but dicier to keep (free agency looms). Missi costs more in the trade (New Orleans' stubbornness has a price) but locks in longer. If you're building for THIS June, lean Diabaté. If you're building for the next five Junes, lean Missi. And if you're Leon Rose? You keep both lines warm and let the market pick your answer — which is exactly what the reporting says he's doing.
