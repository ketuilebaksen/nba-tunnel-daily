## WHO IS TREY JEMISON?

If you only know the name from the end of the Knicks bench, let's fill in the picture. Trey Jemison III is a 26-year-old center from Birmingham, Alabama, listed at 6-foot-10 and 270 pounds. He played college ball at Clemson and then at UAB, where he became a rebounding and shot-blocking anchor — Conference USA All-Defensive honors, and as a senior, a school single-season record of 329 rebounds.

He went undrafted in 2023, and his NBA journey since then has been the definition of grinding. New Orleans signed him and waived him. He went to the G League. He got a 10-day contract with Washington. Then a 10-day with Memphis that turned into a two-way deal. Then New Orleans claimed his two-way contract off waivers. Then the Lakers. Then, in September 2025, the Knicks.

That's a lot of stops. But every one of those teams saw the same thing: a big, strong, physical center who rebounds, protects the rim, sets screens, and does the dirty work. Not a floor-spacer. Not a polished post scorer. A role player who knows exactly what his role is.

## THE MEMPHIS FLASHBACK: WHAT HE CAN DO WITH MINUTES

Want to know what Jemison looks like with real playing time? Look at his run in Memphis during the 2023–24 season. In 23 games with the Grizzlies, he averaged about 7.5 points and 6 rebounds, with an assist and a block per game, shooting around 55 percent from the field. He set a career high of 24 points against Washington. And here's a fun one: he also set a career high at that point — 12 points — against the New York Knicks.

That's the key with Jemison. When he's buried as a fourth or fifth center, the numbers are tiny. When he's given a real role, he produces like a legitimate NBA backup big. For his career, he's played 76 NBA games with 14 starts.

## HIS KNICKS SEASON: SMALL MINUTES, BIG RING

Now let's talk about his time in New York. The Knicks signed Jemison to a two-way contract on September 16th, 2025. He spent most of the year as emergency depth behind Karl-Anthony Towns, Mitchell Robinson, and Ariel Hukporti. In 13 games with the Knicks, he played about 6.3 minutes a night, averaging around one point and 1.4 rebounds. His best night came on December 18th, 2025, when he played 18 minutes and put up five points and five rebounds.

But the real work happened in Westchester. In 21 G League games, Jemison averaged 8.0 points, 8.4 rebounds, 3.0 assists, and 1.4 blocks. Three assists from a center in the G League tells you he understands the game, not just the paint.

And then June 13th, 2026. The Knicks beat the San Antonio Spurs in five games and won their first championship in 53 years. Now, one honest detail: because Jemison was on a two-way contract, he wasn't eligible to play in the playoffs. But he was part of that team all season long — in practices, in the locker room, on the road. He's a champion. And champions remember who was in the room.

## WHY HE LEFT — AND WHY HE COULD COME BACK

So why didn't Jemison just stay in New York? On August 4th, Chris Haynes reported that he agreed to a two-way deal with the Toronto Raptors. The thinking at the time was that Toronto offered him a clearer path — their last open two-way spot, with a better chance at real minutes than he'd have behind New York's centers.

Fast forward two months, and that path in Toronto has reportedly closed. And look at the timing — the Knicks just played the Raptors twice to close out the preseason. Jemison went from wearing a Knicks ring to being released by the team New York just faced. And now he's a free agent again, right as the Knicks have a hole in exactly his position.

## THE HOLE: ZERO TWO-WAY BIGS

Here's the problem that makes this so interesting. Every NBA team gets three two-way contract slots. The Knicks' three two-way players right now are Tyler Nickel, Kevin McCullar Jr., and Jaden Akins. Two wings and a guard. Not a single big man.

Now look at the center depth chart. Karl-Anthony Towns starts. Andre Drummond backs him up. And the 14th roster spot is likely going to James Wiseman — a talented 25-year-old who's appeared in only five NBA games over the last two seasons. That's three centers, and the third has very little recent experience.

Last season, the Knicks' third center was Ariel Hukporti, a player they drafted and developed. Hukporti is gone now — he's with Philadelphia, and sadly, he suffered a torn Achilles in the preseason opener against the Knicks. Mitchell Robinson is in Boston. So the depth that carried this team through a title run has been completely rebuilt.

If KAT gets into foul trouble and Drummond tweaks something on the same night — or if Wiseman's body doesn't hold up — who's the emergency center? Right now, the answer is nobody.

## WHY THE CAMP BIGS CAN'T FILL THAT SPOT

And here's a rule a lot of fans don't know. You might ask: why not just keep Eubanks or Bradley on a two-way deal? Or sign Cissé to one if Dallas releases him? The answer is that two-way contracts are only available to players with limited NBA service time. Veterans like Eubanks and Bradley have too many years in the league to qualify. Same goes for most of the bigs we've been talking about. Wiseman, too. Even if the Knicks love them, they can't stash them on a two-way.

Jemison, on the other hand, just signed a two-way contract with Toronto. That means he still qualifies. He's one of the few experienced NBA centers who can legally fill that exact slot.

## THE BEST PART: ZERO APRON IMPACT

And this is why the move could make so much sense. Every conversation about this Knicks roster eventually crashes into the same wall: the second apron. James Dolan won't cross it. KAT's extension is frozen because of it. The 14th spot is down to one minimum contract because of it. Bruce Brown might need a Dadiet salary dump because of it.

But two-way contracts don't count against the salary cap the way standard contracts do. That means bringing Jemison back on a two-way would add a fourth center without touching the apron math. No salary dump. No trade. No second-round picks. Just a phone call to a player who already knows the system, the coaching staff, and the city.

For a front office that has spent all summer hunting for size under strict financial limits — Diabaté, Kalkbrenner, Ware, Cissé — this might be the cheapest center they'll find all year.

## SO WHO WOULD LOSE A TWO-WAY SPOT?

Of course, there's a cost. To sign Jemison to a two-way, the Knicks would have to release one of their current two-way players. Let's go through the three.

**Tyler Nickel.** He's on a two-year two-way deal and has been getting real preseason minutes, including in the bench-heavy games. He's a shooter, and the Knicks invested in him with that two-year commitment. He feels like the safest of the three.

**Kevin McCullar Jr.** If you watched our "McCullar Crossroads" video, you know we flagged this exact risk: the two-way deal makes him easy to keep — but also easy to replace. He's 25, in his third camp, and the Knicks traded Oso Ighodaro's draft rights to get him. But the organization clearly still believes in him. He re-signed in late September, and the Knicks tend to keep their two-way guys around when they can. I don't think he's the first one out.

**Jaden Akins.** A young guard with real defensive credentials. At Michigan State in 2024–25, he averaged nearly 13 points, 3.5 rebounds, and 1.5 assists and made the All-Big Ten defensive team. But he shot only about 40 percent from the field and 29 percent from three. And here's the roster problem: the Knicks are loaded with guards and wings — Brunson, Alvarado, Deuce, Shamet, Clarkson, Kolek, plus Bruce Brown possibly coming. What they don't have is center depth.

So if I'm the front office, Akins is the most logical swap. Not because he's a bad prospect — he's a good defender with a future — but because positional need matters. A third two-way guard is a luxury. A two-way center is a necessity.

## THE CLOCK IS TICKING

And here's why the Knicks can't wait around. Jemison is good enough that someone will sign him. Remember his history: back in 2024, the New Orleans Pelicans claimed his two-way contract right off waivers. Teams have shown they're willing to jump on him quickly. And a waived two-way player can go through waivers again — where the Knicks, as defending champions, are last in line. If another team claims him, the opportunity disappears. If he clears, the Knicks would have to move fast before someone else signs him.

And look at the calendar. Thursday was the final preseason game against Toronto. Monday, October 19th, is KAT's player option deadline and roster cutdown day. Tuesday, October 20th, is ring night. The front office has a lot on its plate this week. But adding a two-way center is one of the easiest decisions on the list.

## HOW HE'D FIT INTO THE PLAN

Let's be clear about his role. Jemison wouldn't be competing with Wiseman. Wiseman is fighting for the 14th standard roster spot, and all signs point to him getting it. Jemison would be a fourth center — insurance on a two-way deal.

His job would be simple. Screen. Finish inside. Rebound. Protect the rim. Cover rest nights and injuries for KAT and Drummond, who are both going to carry heavy loads. Play big minutes in Westchester, stay ready, and step in when the Knicks need a body in the paint. He's done that before. He knows Mike Brown's terminology, he knows the Westchester staff, and he knows the Knicks' locker room.

And there's one more benefit: it gives the coaching staff a four-deep center rotation game to game — KAT, Drummond, Wiseman, and Jemison — with different looks for different opponents. Against big, physical teams like Minnesota with Rudy Gobert or Cleveland with Jarrett Allen and Evan Mobley, having a 270-pound body available could matter.

## WHAT A FOURTH CENTER REALLY MEANS OVER 82 GAMES

Some fans will say: it's a fourth center, who cares? Here's why it matters more than you think.

An NBA regular season is 82 games, including back-to-backs, long road trips, and stretches of four games in five nights. KAT is coming off a long championship run, he turns 31 next month, he's in the middle of a contract standoff, and the coaching staff has made keeping him fresh a priority. Drummond is a veteran who's dealt with health issues before. Wiseman is coming back from a torn Achilles and has barely played over the last two seasons. That's three centers, and each one comes with a question mark.

Now imagine a night in January. KAT picks up two early fouls. Drummond is resting on the second night of a back-to-back. Wiseman is day-to-day with a sore ankle. Without a fourth center, Mike Brown would have to go small — maybe OG or Josh Hart at the five for long stretches. That works for a few minutes. It doesn't work for a whole game against Gobert or Allen.

Last season, Jemison appeared 13 times for the Knicks. That might not sound like much. But every one of those appearances was a night when the Knicks needed a body in the paint and had one ready. That's the job. You don't notice it when it's filled. You notice it when it's empty.

## THE WESTCHESTER ANGLE

There's another underrated piece here: Westchester. The Knicks' G League affiliate is where Jemison did his real work last season — and where he'd likely spend most of his time again. He already knows the staff, the schemes, and the way the organization wants its bigs to play.

And look again at that stat line: 3.0 assists per game. For a center in the G League, that's notable. Mike Brown's system relies heavily on dribble handoffs and quick decisions from the big — we've seen KAT running the offense from the high post all preseason. A backup big who can make the right pass out of a handoff or a short roll fits that style much better than a big who can only dunk. Jemison isn't KAT, obviously. But he's shown he can make simple, smart plays, and that matters in this system.

A two-way player spends most of his time with the G League team anyway. So bringing back someone who already knows Westchester means zero adjustment period. He could walk in on day one and do his job.

## THE KNICKS' GRINDER BLUEPRINT

And finally, this move would fit a pattern that's become part of this franchise's identity. The Knicks under Leon Rose have built a champion partly by trusting players other teams overlooked.

Landry Shamet came in on an Exhibit 9 deal in September 2024, fighting for a roster spot. Two years later, he's a champion on a four-year, $24 million contract and one of the hottest shooters in the preseason. Bruce Brown — a champion who's been on eight teams in nine years — came into camp on a tryout and has impressed everyone internally. Mohamed Diawara, a second-round pick, earned a contract over a former first-rounder. Kevin McCullar Jr. has been developed for three seasons.

Jemison fits that mold perfectly. Undrafted. Waived multiple times. Never given a long-term deal. And yet he keeps getting signed, because he does the things that help teams win. This front office values guys who know their role, work in the dark, and are ready when called. That's exactly who Trey Jemison is.

## MY CALL

Here's my prediction. I think the Knicks will at least look into this — the fit is too obvious to ignore. Zero apron impact, a player they know and trust, and a clear positional need. I'd swap Akins for Jemison if I were the front office. But the biggest danger is the clock. If a team claims him off waivers or signs him first, this window closes fast. If the Knicks want a two-way center who's already been part of a championship locker room, this is the week to make the call.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

Here's where it stands. Trey Jemison III — a 6-foot-10, 270-pound center who spent last season with the Knicks on a two-way deal and was part of the championship team — has reportedly been waived by the Toronto Raptors, two months after leaving New York for a clearer path. In 13 Knicks games he played sparingly, but in Westchester he averaged 8.0 points, 8.4 rebounds, 3.0 assists, and 1.4 blocks, and in Memphis he showed he can produce with real minutes. The Knicks currently have zero centers on two-way deals — Nickel, McCullar, and Akins are all perimeter players — while their center depth is KAT, Drummond, and likely Wiseman. Camp veterans can't sign two-way contracts, but Jemison can, and a two-way deal wouldn't touch the second apron. Akins looks like the most logical swap. The clock is ticking before another team grabs him.
