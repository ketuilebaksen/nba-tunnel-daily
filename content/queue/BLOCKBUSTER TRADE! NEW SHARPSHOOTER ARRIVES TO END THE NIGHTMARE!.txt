## THE HONEST FRAMING

Quick clarity, the NBA Tunnel way. There was no blockbuster trade. ## HOW IT SLIPPED AWAY

This one stings because the Knicks controlled it. They came out on fire and jumped to an early 12-point lead. They led after the first quarter, at halftime, and after three. With a little over nine minutes left, OG Anunoby dropped in a soft floater to make it 86–76. The Garden was loud. It felt like the first win of the year was on the way.

Then Orlando closed the game on a 35–16 run and won the fourth quarter 40–27. Franz Wagner, who struggled from the field early, scored 21 of his 23 points in the final period alone and finished with 13 rebounds and nine assists. Tristan da Silva hit five threes and scored 19. Anthony Black, back from an ankle injury, added 18.

And the Magic did all of this without their leading scorer, Paolo Banchero — and they even lost Jalen Suggs to a knee injury in the fourth quarter. Orlando also came into the night without a win, which made it even harder to swallow. With the loss, the Knicks became the first defending champion to open a season 0–3 since the 2011–12 Dallas Mavericks.

## THE NUMBERS OF THE COLLAPSE

**Three-point shooting:** Knicks nine-of-38. Magic 18-of-42. That's a 27-point swing from deep.

**Turnovers:** 14 total — eight of them in the fourth quarter.

**Free throws:** Orlando made 17-of-21. New York made 17-of-24.

In a game decided late, those missed chances added up fast. And for the season, the Knicks are shooting just 32 percent from three. For an offense built on Brunson and KAT collapsing defenses and kicking out to shooters, that's the nightmare.

## WHO STOOD OUT

Let's be fair — there were real positives.

**Karl-Anthony Towns:** Three games, three double-doubles. Against Orlando: 22 points and 14 rebounds. He picked up two early fouls again — a pattern — but after his second foul early in the second quarter, he didn't commit another all night. That kept him on the floor. When the Knicks were in control, he was attacking his defender off the dribble again and again. And he moved the ball well: four assists, after totaling just four in his first two games combined.

**OG Anunoby:** Maybe his most complete game of the season. Twenty-two points on six-of-11 shooting, three-of-six from deep, seven-of-nine at the line. He worked in the post to create open corner threes for Josh Hart, drove for an and-one, and scored on his own during an 8–0 burst that put the Knicks up 10. That's OG as a true three-level threat — and the Knicks need a lot more of that version.

**Josh Hart:** Hart did what Hart does. Thirteen points, eight rebounds, three made threes, two assists, two steals — and one of the few Knicks with a positive rating on the night. Steady, physical, reliable. The glue.

**Jalen Brunson:** Twenty-eight points, but on 28 shots. One-of-seven from three and three missed free throws. Shots that usually fall kept rolling out. He still made big plays late, including a successful challenge on a loose-ball foul against Wagner. The effort is there. The touch looks rusty. History says Brunson doesn't stay cold for long.

## THE BENCH: MOMENTS AND QUESTIONS

**Andre Drummond** played only seven minutes but made them count — a tip-in and a strong drive past Nikola Vučević. His best stretch since the first half of the opener.

**James Wiseman** logged a season-high seven minutes. He crashed the glass and used his length to change shots at the rim, even though he missed all five of his attempts. A real first step after Mike Brown said only five rotation spots are locked.

**Landry Shamet** knocked down a couple of threes.

**Deuce McBride**, who exploded for 20 points against Boston, couldn't find the same stroke this time. A hot Deuce would go a long way toward fixing the team's shooting.

**Mikal Bridges** had the toughest night. In 35 minutes, he took only two more shots than Wiseman did in seven. When the ball found him, he often swung it right away instead of attacking. Bridges has gone through slumps before and worked his way out. But right now, the Knicks need more.

## THE BRIGHTEST SPOT: TYLER KOLEK

And then there's the player who might have been the brightest spot of all. Before the game, Jose Alvarado was listed as questionable with a left ankle sprain — and he didn't play. That opened the door for Tyler Kolek, and he walked right through it.

Context first: Kolek hadn't seen meaningful minutes in a competitive game in more than seven months. During last season's playoff run, the only games in which he played at least 10 minutes were blowouts the Knicks won by 30, 39, and 51 points. For a 25-year-old in his third NBA season, whose future in New York isn't locked in, this was the chance he'd been waiting for.

In 15 minutes, Kolek dished out nine assists without a single turnover. The moment he checked in, he was creating for others. He drove and dumped it off to Mikal Bridges for an easy layup. He found Karl-Anthony Towns cutting down the lane. He drove and kicked to Landry Shamet for a three. He kept a loose ball alive among Orlando's bigs and found Shamet again for another three. He set up a KAT layup and a Josh Hart three that restored a 75–69 lead. On defense, he held his ground against the bigger Anthony Black and forced a missed turnaround.

And maybe the most important stretch came in the third quarter. Brunson went to the bench just after the five-minute mark with the Knicks up four. Kolek ran the offense while the captain rested for about nine minutes of game time. When Brunson came back, the lead had grown to six. For a backup point guard, that's the job: keep the ship steady and give the star a breather without giving anything back.

## THE SHOOTING CASE FOR KOLEK

To be fair, his own shot didn't fall — zero-of-four, including zero-of-two from three. But his history tells a more encouraging story. Last season, Kolek shot 39 percent from three, compared to 33 percent for Alvarado. That matters, because spacing is the engine of this offense, and right now the engine is sputtering. A guard defenses have to respect from the perimeter opens the floor for Brunson and KAT.

There was also a matchup reason Kolek fit this game. His defense has never been his strength, but Orlando's backcourt didn't carry much shooting threat — Jalen Suggs is a below-average outside shooter, and Jevon Carter is known more for defense. Desmond Bane is Orlando's real shooter, and Kolek wasn't assigned to him. So it was a comfortable spot. But he made the most of it.

## KOLEK'S FUTURE: ROTATION PIECE OR TRADE CHIP?

So what comes next? Kolek's performance earned him a stronger case for more minutes. Mike Brown has a deep group of guards and wings, but a pass-first point guard who protects the ball and keeps the offense organized has real value — especially for a team still finding its rhythm.

But there's a bigger picture. Kolek was one of the Knicks who didn't get an extension when talks were tabled. His future isn't locked in. And if the Knicks decide to make a move during the season, he could be one of their most attractive trade pieces.

Leon Rose has a track record of adding help midseason. Three years ago, he made a deadline deal for Bojan Bogdanović and Alec Burks. Two years ago, he added P.J. Tucker in March. Last season, Alvarado himself was the finishing piece of the championship roster. The rotation in October is rarely the rotation in April. And with few draft picks to trade and very little cap room, a trade is the most realistic way to upgrade.

If that time comes, the Knicks have only a couple of young, low-cost players with upside to offer: Kolek and Mohamed Diawara. Diawara is in the first year of a four-year contract and played more than twice as many minutes as a rookie as Kolek did. Three-and-D wings are highly valued around the league, so Diawara looks more like a keeper. That makes Kolek — a proven point guard on a small salary — a natural candidate if New York needs to bring in a key veteran. Add Pacôme Dadiet's contract and Deuce's expiring deal, and those are the Knicks' realistic trade tools after December 15th.

My take: I'd keep Kolek unless the return is a clear upgrade at center or wing. Sunday showed he can run an NBA offense under pressure. Either way, his value just went up — and every team in the league saw it.

## THE NEW SHARPSHOOTER: DALTON KNECHT

Now, the name in our title. When the Knicks added Dalton Knecht on a two-way deal this week, a lot of fans got excited — and after a nine-for-38 night, it's easy to see why.

On paper, Knecht looks like a perfect fit. He's a 6-foot-6 wing who has been a strong three-point shooter at every level — 37.6 percent from deep as a rookie with the Lakers, on more than four attempts a game. He hit 40.7 percent of his corner threes that season. He tied the NBA rookie record with nine threes in a single game. He has the instincts to slip behind defenders for backdoor cuts, and enough bounce to finish above the rim when he gets there. And 92 percent of his made threes as a rookie were assisted — he's a catch-and-shoot wing.

Now picture him on the floor next to Brunson and KAT. Brunson drives, the defense collapses. KAT draws a big out to the perimeter. The ball swings to the corner — and Knecht lets it fly. That's exactly the "spray three" Mike Brown loves. That's exactly what the Knicks didn't have on Sunday.

## THE REALISTIC EXPECTATIONS FOR KNECHT

But let's be honest about the climb. Knecht is on a two-way contract, so he'll split time between the Knicks and Westchester, with a limit of 50 NBA games. His second NBA season was rough — his minutes were cut almost in half after the rescinded Mark Williams trade, and his three-point percentage dropped to 34.2. His defense has graded out negatively both years. And Mike Brown has made it clear: minutes are earned, not given.

So he's not going to walk in and start. But on nights when the Knicks' shooting goes cold, Brown now has an emergency shooter he can call on. If Knecht gets his rhythm back in Westchester — or in a few spot minutes in New York — he could become the kind of bench weapon that turns a 9-for-38 night into a 13-for-38 night. And in a game the Knicks lost by nine, that's the difference.

## TONIGHT: DETROIT

The next test is tonight: the Knicks head to Detroit to face the Pistons, one of the toughest, most physical teams in the East. The keys are clear. Hit open shots. Protect the ball in the fourth quarter. Keep KAT on the floor and running the offense — if Sunday was KAT finding his groove, Detroit will have a lot to handle. Keep feeding OG in the post and on the drive. And if Alvarado is still out, give Kolek the keys again.

## THE PERSPECTIVE

It's late October. Last spring, this same group lost only a handful of playoff games on the way to a championship. The talent hasn't disappeared. KAT has three straight double-doubles. OG just played his most complete game. Hart is Hart. Brunson is creating looks even when they don't fall. And the young players — Kolek, Wiseman, and now Knecht — are getting chances. The rhythm simply hasn't arrived yet. When the shots start falling, this looks like a very different team.

## WHY SPACING IS THE WHOLE STORY

Let me explain why the shooting matters even more than the raw numbers suggest. This offense is built on gravity. Brunson gets into the paint, two defenders collapse. KAT pops out to the arc, and the opposing big has to follow him. That creates open looks — but only if the guys on the weak side make defenses pay. When they don't, the floor shrinks. Defenders start cheating off shooters, Brunson sees bodies in every gap, and those tough twisting finishes he usually makes become contested heaves.

That's exactly what happened in the fourth quarter on Sunday. As the threes kept missing, Orlando packed the paint, the Knicks forced the ball inside, and eight fourth-quarter turnovers followed. Missed threes and turnovers aren't two separate problems — one feeds the other. Fix the first one, and the second one gets easier too.

The good news? This is a roster full of proven shooters. Hart hit three on Sunday. Shamet hit a couple. OG went three-of-six. Deuce just had a 20-point night in Boston. The open looks are coming. They just have to fall. And when they do, everything else opens up again.

## WHAT MIKE BROWN CAN ADJUST

So what can the coaching staff actually control? Here are three ideas — my analysis, not reporting.

**One: stagger Brunson and Kolek more deliberately.** Sunday showed that the offense didn't fall apart when Brunson sat. If Kolek can keep running things for stretches, Brunson stays fresher for the fourth quarter — and that's when the Knicks have been fading.

**Two: get Bridges involved early.** When a player is struggling, the best fix is often a simple one: run a couple of early sets for him. Get him a catch on the move, a cut to the rim, an easy one at the line. Confidence builds from there. A passive Bridges hurts this team on both ends.

**Three: protect the ball late.** Eight fourth-quarter turnovers is the stat that should bother this staff most. Slow it down, run the offense through KAT at the elbow, and stop forcing passes into crowded lanes. You don't need hero ball to close games — you need clean possessions.

**Bonus: trust the young guards when the matchup fits.** Kolek earned it Sunday. Wiseman is earning it in small doses. And when Knecht is ready, a few minutes of pure shooting could change the math on a cold night.

None of these are radical changes. They're adjustments. And that's the point: this team isn't broken. It's simply out of rhythm right now.

## MY CALL

Here's my take. The Knicks get their first win tonight against Detroit. The shooting regresses to the mean — nobody shoots nine-for-38 forever. Kolek keeps earning minutes until Alvarado returns, and maybe beyond. And Knecht gets his first Knicks minutes sooner than people expect, because this team needs shooting right now.
