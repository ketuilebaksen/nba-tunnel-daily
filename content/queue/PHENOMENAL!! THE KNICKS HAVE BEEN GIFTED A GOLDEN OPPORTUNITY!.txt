STORY ONE: THE KONCHAR PREDICTION

Remember the surprise name we broke down last episode? It just got warmer. John Konchar — the seven-year veteran glue guy — is now being PREDICTED to sign with the Knicks on a minimum deal after being waived by Minnesota. And the backstory is deliciously absurd: the Timberwolves churned him through their cap machinery and cut him loose purely to clear room for the Jonathan Kuminga signing — the same Kuminga domino, by the way, that crushed our Donte dreams. So Minnesota's cap crunch takes away one reunion... and might hand us a rotation player for FREE. The basketball gods have a sense of humor.

And look, we made the case already, but it bears repeating with the signing now looking likely: Konchar is the archetype this championship was BUILT on. Twenty-one million in career earnings, zero headlines, all substance — a strong rebounder for his position, a genuinely elite perimeter defender, a capable (not great, capable) open-three shooter, and an offensive connector who understands how to play inside a system instead of demanding the system bend to him. He won't give you twenty a night. He'll give you 15-20 minutes of high-IQ, zero-maintenance winning basketball and go home happy. Championship teams stay fresh through an 82-game grind on the backs of exactly these guys. If the minimum gets it done — and it looks like it will — sign him yesterday. Minnesota's loss, New York's gain.

STORY TWO: THE McBRIDE BOARD — THREE NAMES, THREE DEALS

Now the main event, and fair warning — this one's bittersweet. James L. Edwards of The Athletic doubled down on his reporting: the Knicks and Deuce McBride are likely heading toward a separation, either at the February deadline or next offseason. We've walked through the WHY for weeks — Deuce gets PAID next summer, the second apron is a hard wall, and the front office that let Mitchell Robinson walk rather than cross that line isn't changing philosophy for a backup guard. We won a championship with Deuce. He mattered. And the cap does not care. What's NEW today is the specificity: three concrete targets the front office is reportedly monitoring. Let's grade all three.

TARGET ONE: RYAN KALKBRENNER, CHARLOTTE HORNETS. The name that refuses to die on this channel — and notice the wrinkle: the door we thought slammed shut earlier this summer was for a KOLEK-based deal. With Deuce as the centerpiece, it creaks back open. The profile is exactly our wishlist: 24 years old, nearly made All-Rookie, and the per-minute production is filthy — 7.6 points, 5.5 rebounds, and 1.5 BLOCKS in just 21.4 minutes... while shooting an NBA-BEST 75.3% from the field. That is elite, elite efficiency. Charlotte's frontcourt is still a traffic jam — Naz Reid in the fold, the rookie Steinbach drafted, young centers stacked on young centers — while their perimeter scoring is starving. The reported framework: Kalkbrenner to New York; McBride plus multiple second-rounders — the 2027 and 2029 picks from New York and the 2029 via Phoenix — to Charlotte. My grade? This is the HOME RUN. A young, cheap, shot-blocking big on a rookie deal is the exact archetype we've hunted since June. If Charlotte engages, you run to the table.

TARGET TWO: SCOTTY PIPPEN JR., MEMPHIS GRIZZLIES. The sneaky-value play. The undrafted kid with the Hall of Fame surname has quietly become one of the league's premier backup point guards: 10.6 points, 4.5 assists, 3.2 rebounds across three Memphis seasons on 48/39/72 splits — efficiency across the board. And the beautiful part is the contract: a $2.7 million team option for 2027-28, which hands New York flexibility AND softens the tax math. The framework here is clean — Pippen Jr. for McBride, essentially straight up. What you'd get is a true table-setter who doesn't need the ball, can genuinely run the second unit next to Alvarado, and gives Brunson real breathers. What you would NOT get is a center — and that's my hesitation. This deal solves a want, not the need.

TARGET THREE: KRIS DUNN, LOS ANGELES CLIPPERS. The safe floor. We covered Dunn weeks ago and the case hasn't changed: a decade of elite, disruptive point-of-attack defense, an expiring $5.6 million contract, and a contract-year motor that'll run white-hot. Yes, the career 33% from three makes him an offensive downgrade from Deuce — but he started 80% of Clippers games the last two seasons and hit a respectable 37.4% from deep last year under Ty Lue. The reported framework is heavier: McBride AND Pacôme Dadiet to L.A. for Dunn. In New York he'd play 15-20 minutes as the designated backcourt jailer, anchoring the second unit with Alvarado, Clarkson, and Kolek. My grade: solid basketball, steep price. Two young assets for a 30-something specialist only makes sense if the front office has fully given up on Dadiet — which, given the "final warning" reporting we covered, might not be far-fetched.

And before anyone melts down in the comments about "trading a champion" — let's handle the emotional math honestly, because we've lived this story all summer. Nobody WANTS Deuce gone. He's the longest-tenured Knick, a homegrown success story, a guy who bled for this jersey through the lean years and the golden one. But run the alternative: keep him past the deadline, watch a rival back up the Brinks truck in July — the exact Boston-and-Robinson play we already suffered once — and lose him for NOTHING. Zero return on the most tradable asset in the vault. Championship front offices don't do sentimental bookkeeping. They convert value before it evaporates, and they do it while the asset is at its peak. The Quickley-into-OG trade taught this fanbase that lesson forever: the goodbye that hurts in January can be the cornerstone you're hugging in June.

THE VERDICT ACROSS ALL THREE: notice that none of these is a like-for-like swap. Every deal pivots Deuce's value into a DIFFERENT skill set — rim protection, table-setting, or on-ball defense. That's not a downgrade; that's roster surgery, converting a surplus into a scarcity. If you're asking me to rank them: Kalkbrenner by a mile, Dunn second, Pippen Jr. third — because the center hole remains THE hole, and only one of these trades fills it. But all three confirm the headline that matters: the chip is in play, and the front office is actively shopping. February 11th is circled in red ink.

The schedule makers essentially said: "Congratulations on the championship. Now prove it, immediately, in the loudest possible room." And here's the counterintuitive truth — good teams WANT this. Soft openings breed complacency; every championship hangover in league history started with a cushy first month and a team still smelling its own confetti. Boston in week one is a forced reset button: emotional catharsis Monday, cold-blooded business Thursday. By the end of that first week, Mike Brown will know exactly what his title defense looks like away from the Garden — and remember Brunson's own words from the Post interview: the trophy goes out of sight, the mindset gets TOUGHER. The schedule is about to test whether the team actually lives that. I think they do.

One more schedule note worth savoring: October 20th itself. Ring night against PHILADELPHIA — the LeBron-and-Jaylen-Brown superteam we've been tracking all summer, the roster built specifically to dethrone New York, forced to stand on the Garden floor and watch our banner rise before the season's first tip. The league didn't just schedule a game; it scheduled a statement. The two heavyweights of the East, night one, with fifty-three years of pent-up celebration raining down on the challengers. If you're not already counting the days, check your pulse.

STORY FOUR: THE NOTEBOOK — DIRK'S CONFESSION

And we close with something special. Dirk Nowitzki — the greatest Maverick who ever lived — just publicly admitted he doubted Jalen Brunson. His words: "I have to admit I was skeptical when he went to New York to become the franchise player in a market like that. He's someone quite calm, rather reserved, so I was a bit worried to see him arrive in that media environment." And then the deeper cut: "I already knew during his rookie season that he was very solid... but I didn't think he had a FRANCHISE PLAYER ceiling, let alone Finals MVP."

Sit with that. The GOAT Maverick, who watched Brunson in that Dallas gym before and after every practice, who saw the work ethic firsthand, still couldn't project this. Because in Dallas, Brunson was permanent second fiddle to Luka — and nobody extends the ceiling of the guy standing in a superstar's shadow. Then New York happened. The stage Dirk worried would swallow a quiet, reserved kid turned out to be the exact stage he was built for. And Dirk, to his eternal credit, finished with grace: "He's been incredible on and off the court... winning the title, bringing a championship to New York after more than 50 years, being Finals MVP — his journey is incredible. His footwork, his changes of pace, his way of going exactly where he wants on the court — it's really great to watch."

And there's a lesson inside Dirk's confession for how we evaluate OUR young guys, too. The greatest Maverick ever watched Brunson daily and still capped his ceiling at "very solid" — because context hides ceilings. A role suppresses what opportunity reveals. Keep that in mind next time someone writes off Kolek behind Brunson, or Diawara in spot minutes, or whichever kid is buried on a loaded champion: the evaluation isn't finished until the opportunity arrives. Sometimes not even legends can see it coming.

That's the whole Brunson story in one confession: doubted by legends, out-worked the doubt, and answered with a parade. He never got defensive, never demanded the keys in Dallas, never complained — he just prepared, waited, and detonated when the opportunity came. Winning the respect of a legend by proving him wrong might be the purest championship validation there is.
