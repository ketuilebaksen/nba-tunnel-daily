## THE BRUNSON COMEBACK LAB

Now let's open the lab. Here are the five data-driven signs that the version of Brunson who carried New York to a championship has returned.

## SIGN #1: HE'S MAKING HIS SHOTS AGAIN

Start with the simplest one. Early in the season, Brunson's struggles matched those of almost everyone on the roster not named Karl-Anthony Towns. He had surgery on his left shooting wrist in the offseason, and the rust showed.

Look at the before-and-after on his signature shots:

| Shot type | Early season | Since then |
|---|---|---|
| Floaters | 24% | 42.8% |
| Pull-up jumpers | 33.3% | 53.8% |
| Above-the-break threes | 34% | 44.1% |
| Paint shots outside the restricted area | 22.2% | 47.6% |

For a player whose whole game is built on those exact looks, the early numbers were alarming. The new numbers are vintage Brunson.

**And the overall picture** over his last 10 games, compared with that rough opening stretch:
- True shooting percentage: **up 8.8 points**
- Two-point percentage: **up 4.9 points**
- Three-point percentage: **up 12 points**
- Free-throw percentage: **up 14.3 points**

That's not a small correction. That's a star getting back to his normal level.

## SIGN #2: CAPTAIN CLUTCH IS BACK

Brunson's nickname was earned over years of big shots in big moments. He's long ranked among the league leaders in clutch scoring and playoff points. Early this season, some wondered whether that edge had faded, as the Knicks dropped several close games.

Those doubts are gone. Brunson currently leads the NBA in:
- **Clutch points**
- **Clutch three-pointers made**
- **Clutch assists**

And the Knicks have started winning the tight games they were losing in October. For a team defending a title, that matters, because the biggest games of the year are often decided in the final minutes.

## SIGN #3: HE'S MOVING WITHOUT THE BALL

This is the one most people miss. Brunson has always been a three-level scorer who uses strength, a high basketball IQ, and excellent ball security to get to his spots. But he's also one of the best off-ball players in the league — arguably second only to Stephen Curry. He keeps moving, uses screens, cuts with purpose, and punishes defenses as a catch-and-shoot threat. He sharpened that skill playing next to Luka Dončić in Dallas.

And the Knicks are using it more. Towns is finding Brunson more than he did at this point last season:
- **Towns has assisted Brunson 19 times** — 11 more than any other teammate.
- **14 of those assists** have come in the last 10 games.
- **42.6% of Brunson's made field goals** over that span have been assisted — up from 31.1% last season.

That's huge. Brunson doesn't have to create everything himself, and that saves energy for the moments that matter. Remember Mike Brown's two-minute tracking data — the staff is constantly managing his workload. Easier baskets are part of that.

## SIGN #4: THE HART SPACING MATH

This is the most debated one. Josh Hart's outside shooting remains a hot topic. He made 41.3% of his threes last season, but he's been far less willing to let it fly this year, and that affects spacing.

For the second season in a row, Brunson has been more productive with Hart on the bench:
- **With Hart on the floor:** 25.8 points per 36 minutes on 54.8% true shooting
- **With Hart off the floor:** 30.2 points per 36 minutes on 57.2% true shooting

Hart missed three games with the ankle injury and has seen his minutes move up and down since returning, so Brunson has spent more time on the floor without him lately — and thrived.

**But here's the important part:** That doesn't make Hart a problem. His rebounding, energy, and playmaking are a huge part of who this team is. It simply means the spacing math changes when Hart isn't looking for his own shot. And as we'll see in a minute, Hart just had one of the most historic nights of his Knicks career.

## SIGN #5: THE PLAYMAKING IS BACK

In the early part of the season, Brunson averaged 5.3 assists per game — and part of that was out of his control. OG Anunoby, Mikal Bridges, and Hart all struggled to make shots, so a lot of good passes never turned into assists.

Over his last 10 games, Brunson is averaging **6.4 assists per game** while keeping his turnover rate the same. He's creating more for others without giving the ball away more often. That steady decision-making is a big reason the offense looks smoother.

## THE LAB'S HONEST NOTE

To be fair, it's not perfect. Brunson still over-dribbles at times and forces plays he doesn't need to make — the finish of the win in Minnesota, when the Knicks didn't score over the final 3:26, was a clear example. And his two-man game with Towns is improving but hasn't reached its full potential yet.

That's actually good news. It means there's still room to grow.

## THE INDIANA REVENGE: KAT AND HART MAKE HISTORY

Now let's talk about Sunday's 113–106 win over the Pacers — revenge for the Cup loss in Indianapolis nine days earlier.

**Karl-Anthony Towns:** Dominant. **30 points and 13 rebounds**, back from his illness. Late in the third quarter, he outscored the Pacers 10–2 by himself, capping the burst with a three that pushed the lead to 86–74. That's the stretch that broke the game open.

**Josh Hart:** And here's the history. Hart posted **10 points, 11 assists, and 10 rebounds** — a triple-double. It was his **18th triple-double as a Knick**, tying **Micheal Ray Richardson for second** on the franchise's career list. Only **Walt Frazier, with 23**, has more.

Think about that. Josh Hart is chasing Clyde Frazier in the Knicks record books. And it was a perfect example of what Hart gives this team, even on nights when his own shot isn't part of the plan. That's why the Brunson-Hart spacing numbers don't tell the whole story.

**Landry Shamet:** 17 points against the Pacers, continuing a strong stretch that's made his four-year, $24 million contract look like a bargain.

**The bench guards:** Jose Alvarado and Tyler Kolek have provided energy and timely sparks in recent wins, giving Brown more options when the starters need a breather.

And all of this came without OG Anunoby, who missed his second straight game with tightness in his left calf. OG has also had a tough shooting start, so Bridges isn't the only wing searching for his touch.

## THE BRIDGES UPDATE

If one problem stands out right now, it's Mikal Bridges' slow start. And honestly, if that's the biggest issue, the Knicks are in very good shape.

**The season numbers:**
- 7.8 points, 3.4 rebounds, 2.2 assists, and 1.1 steals per game
- 1.3 turnovers in 20.4 minutes per game
- 40.3% from the field and 31.7% from three

Those numbers are well below what the Knicks expect from a core starter on a four-year, $150 million deal. And against Indiana, he went just three-of-12 from the field.

**But that's not the full story.** When his shot isn't falling, Bridges still guards the opponent's best perimeter player, plays heavy minutes, and stays involved. He's played at least 28 minutes in each of the last six games — a clear sign the staff still trusts him.

**Mike Brown's stance:** Brown has given more late-game minutes to Landry Shamet at times, but he's made it clear he isn't planning a major change. He said pulling Bridges out of the starting lineup would be "unnecessarily dramatic."

**Why the trust is justified:**
- He's one of the most reliable defenders on the roster — his defense helped close out the 102–99 win in Minnesota.
- He has a long track record of rising in big moments, in the playoffs and in the clutch.
- **Saturday against Portland** proved it. The Knicks built a 26-point lead, then watched it slip away. With the game on the line, Bridges hit a three at the buzzer for a 97–96 win.

One make doesn't erase weeks of struggles. But it showed that his confidence is intact. If he finds his shot, a team that's already 8–2 over its last 10 becomes even more dangerous.

## THE TEAM IDENTITY: WINNING IN DIFFERENT WAYS

Put it all together, and this is a team that has stopped losing close games and is winning in different ways:
- **Some nights, it's Towns** — 30 and 13 against Indiana.
- **Some nights, it's Brunson** — the league's best closer.
- **Some nights, it's the bench** — Shamet, Kolek, Alvarado.
- **Some nights, it's a role player's moment** — a Bridges buzzer-beater, a Hart triple-double.
- **Some nights, it's a key defensive stop** — like the finish in Minnesota.

That's what a championship team looks like.

## WHAT "UNNECESSARILY DRAMATIC" TELLS US ABOUT MIKE BROWN

That quote from Brown deserves a closer look, because it explains how he's coaching this season.

**He protects confidence.** Moving a veteran starter to the bench is a public statement. For a player already fighting his shot, it can make things worse. Brown would rather keep the routine and adjust minutes quietly.

**He manages the fourth quarter instead.** Bridges starts, but Brown has given more late-game minutes to Shamet when the matchup or the hot hand calls for it. That's a subtle way to reward production without a dramatic demotion.

**He trusts track records.** Brown has seen what Bridges can do in big moments. That trust paid off in a big way on Saturday.

**He's done this before.** Last season, Brown waited until the end of November to move Josh Hart into the starting lineup, and only when an injury to OG Anunoby forced the issue. He doesn't make lineup changes because of noise. He makes them when there's a clear basketball reason.

So don't expect a Bridges benching anytime soon. Expect patience, a short leash late in games, and a coach who believes the shot will come back.

## THE CISSÉ QUESTION — AND THE RULE THAT BLOCKS IT

Now, the bigger roster question isn't on the wing. It's the depth behind Towns in the frontcourt. And a new trade rumor out of Dallas has put it back in the spotlight.

**The player:** Moussa Cissé, a 24-year-old, 6-foot-11 center who's still developing. He's known for his athleticism, rebounding, and shot-blocking — young, inexpensive, and plays above the rim. For a Knicks team searching for the right center mix since the offseason, it's easy to see why his profile stands out.

**The news:** Cissé has emerged as a possible trade candidate in Dallas, and the Knicks have been linked to him as a team that could use center depth.

**The history:** And here's the twist. The Knicks liked him so much that they signed him to a two-year, $4.7 million offer sheet on July 18th, when he was a restricted free agent. Dallas matched the offer and kept him. His contract pays $2.2 million this season, with a non-guaranteed $2.6 million for next season.

**The catch:** Under NBA rules, when a team matches an offer sheet, it can't trade that player to the team that signed him to the offer sheet for one year. So Dallas can't trade Cissé to the Knicks until next summer. That's the rule that makes a deal impossible for now.

We actually mentioned this back in October, when Dallas first matched — and it still applies. The Knicks can't go back to the well on Cissé this season, no matter how good the fit is.

## WHAT THE CISSÉ STORY TELLS US

Even though the trade is blocked, the story is still useful. It confirms what the Knicks want:
- **Young:** Cissé is 24.
- **Athletic:** He plays above the rim.
- **Cheap:** About $2.2 million.
- **A rim protector and rebounder:** Exactly the job description behind KAT.

So expect the Knicks to keep looking for that same profile elsewhere. We've talked about names that fit: Charles Bassey, Karlo Matković, Yanic Konan Niederhäuser, Goga Bitadze, and even waived bigs like Lawson Lovering. And of course, James Wiseman is already on the roster with that exact athletic profile. With only about $89,000 of room, according to ESPN's Bobby Marks, any trade has to bring back no more salary than it sends out — which is why cheap young bigs keep coming up.
