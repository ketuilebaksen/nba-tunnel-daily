THE KD PRECEDENT: BROWN'S REDISTRIBUTION DOCTRINE

Because Mike Brown's answer to the Mitch question might be the smartest thing said at any podium today. Asked how Drummond replaces Robinson, the coach REJECTED the premise — and reached for a heavyweight precedent: his years on the Warriors bench when Golden State lost Kevin Durant in 2019. His words: "Everybody's like, losing KD, you guys could be in trouble — first-ballot Hall of Famer. We didn't go out and replace him with a CLONE. You can't do that." Then the Mitch respect, fully paid: "Mitch is a great young player... one of the best rebounders, if not THE best offensive rebounder in the league. He protects the rim and all that. We're not going to have ONE person step up and replace him. We're ALL going to have to do a little bit more, on both sides of the floor."

That's not coach-speak, folks — that's a philosophy with a championship receipt. You don't replace elite specialists one-for-one; you REDISTRIBUTE their production across the roster and let the system absorb the loss. And notice: it's exactly the blueprint we laid out in the changing-roles video — Hart's rebounding graduating to load-bearing, KAT holding the discipline gains, the wings crashing harder, the whole roster inheriting Mitch's job in slices. The coach just confirmed the homework. Nobody wears number 23's shoes. Everybody carries one of his boxes.

THE DRUMMOND PLAN: THE X'S AND O'S

And then Brown got specific about the new big fella, and this is where my ears really perked up. First, the gym report — and remember, this coach doesn't hand out compliments: "Watching him in the gym, you see he's a little QUICKER than what you thought, a little more EXPLOSIVE than what you thought. Got some size to him, some physicality." At 6'11" and 279 pounds, that size is the plan's foundation, and Brown laid out the deployment with real tactical detail: Drummond as a pick-and-roll weapon that defenses simply cannot disrespect. His words: "He's a guy teams aren't going to just switch or disrespect in the pick-and-roll game, which is going to help Jalen, Mikal, OG and stuff like that. You switch, and he's going to KILL you on the glass — or he's going to sit in the paint."

Unpack the chess there, because it's beautiful: last season, teams switched small onto the Knicks' screeners and lived with it. Try that with Drummond and you've put a guard on a 279-pound rebounding machine two feet from YOUR rim — he either mauls the offensive glass over the mismatch or camps in the paint as a dump-off target. Which means defenses have to play pick-and-rolls straight... which is exactly the coverage Jalen Brunson has butchered his entire career. Drummond's gravity isn't about his own scoring; it's about forcing defenses back into the shapes our stars feast on. And on the other end, Brown addressed the elephant honestly: no, Drummond is not the shot-blocker Mitch was — nobody is. But: "He's been through this thing a million times. His veteran understanding of the officials, the game flow — I could go on and on — is going to be a positive." Rim protection by TIMING instead of hops, positioning instead of pogo. Different tool, same job description. And there's a subtle officiating dividend in that veteran-savvy point people miss: a big who knows the referees — how they call verticality, when they swallow whistles, which crews let you bang — stays OUT of the foul trouble that plagued this frontcourt for years. Remember the KAT discipline arc, the Robbins foul file, the whole channel obsession with whistle management? Drummond arrives having already graduated that course a decade ago. Sometimes the best rim protection is simply being allowed to stay on the floor.

THE HONEST WORRY — BECAUSE WE ALWAYS DO IT

Now my sleepless-night section, because faith without scrutiny is just fandom. The Brown gym report — quicker, more explosive — I want it to be true, badly. But the fear is specific: PACE. How does Drummond run the floor for 82 games? How does he move laterally when the Sixers or the Raptors spread him out and hunt him in space? Because we watched this movie in flashes last season with early-season KAT: one big trailing the play, and suddenly it's four-on-five every transition, the opponent's offense sets up clean, and the possession bleeds out before it starts. If gym-quick Drummond is real, the plan sings. If January-back-to-back Drummond lags... the redistribution doctrine gets stress-tested fast, and it's on everybody — Hart cleaning extra glass, wings covering extra rotations — to carry those slices Brown talked about. The coach said it himself: this is a TEAM assignment now. The team better mean it.

And the counterweight, to keep the file fair: offensively, there's a real argument Drummond is an UPGRADE on what Mitch gave this offense. He'll knock down the occasional three when left alone — a sentence never once typed about Robinson — his free-throw shooting, while not pretty, doesn't invite the hack-a-big circus, and that veteran feel means smarter short-roll reads and fewer dead possessions. Plus the wrinkle Brown now has in his pocket: the DOUBLE-BIG look — KAT sliding to the four with Drummond at the five, situationally, matchup-permitting. And here's a media day observation to file: KAT showed up looking noticeably LEAN this year — a slimmer, quicker version of the champ. A trimmed-down KAT playing minutes at the four next to a 279-pound anchor? That's a completely different geometry than anything the league scouted last season. Not an every-night look — but one more club in a bag that keeps getting deeper.
