WHO IS TYLER NICKEL? THE SAFEST BET IN THE SECOND ROUND

Here's a truth about the modern NBA draft that smart front offices have figured out: one of the SAFEST bets you can make in the second round is the elite three-point specialist. Athleticism is a lottery ticket. Playmaking is a projection. But a repeatable, high-volume jumper? That translates. There's always a role for it, there's always a market for it, and the guys who have it tend to stick for a decade. Tyler Nickel is exactly that bet.

The college résumé: 13 points per game on 40% from three at Vanderbilt — lights-out production in the toughest conference in America. He's got real size for a shooter, and the comparisons circling him are the exact archetypes you want: Duncan Robinson and Sam Hauser — comps Nickel himself has embraced. Those aren't stars; they're something arguably more valuable for a contender — SPECIALISTS with ten-year careers, playoff minutes, and defenses that have to account for them every second they're on the floor. Miami built entire offensive packages around Robinson. Boston won a title with Hauser spacing the floor. That's the template.

And then came Summer League, where Nickel didn't just meet the hype — he broke the scale. Tommy Beer dug up the stat that should be framed in the Knicks' front office: Nickel shot 41% from three on 9.2 ATTEMPTS per game in Vegas. That makes him just the SECOND player this entire decade to average over nine threes a game and hit over 40% of them at Summer League. The other name on that list? Sam Merrill — who parlayed that exact profile into becoming a genuinely important rotation piece for Cleveland. Nine-plus attempts is the part that matters, by the way. Anyone can get hot on four shots a game. Hunting NINE threes a night and still hitting 41% means the volume, the conditioning, the movement shooting, the confidence — the entire specialist toolkit — is already there. This is not a project. This is a weapon waiting for a contract.

And appreciate the honesty of the archetype for a second. Nickel isn't pretending to be something he's not — he's compared HIMSELF to Robinson and Hauser, which tells you he understands his NBA identity perfectly. Self-aware specialists succeed; delusional ones wash out chasing a game they'll never have. A rookie who already knows his job description — sprint the arc, shoot without conscience, compete on defense, repeat — skips the identity crisis that eats most second-rounders alive.

One more detail that actually works in his favor: Nickel is an OLDER rookie. He spent years in college, which means he arrives polished, physically mature, and ready for real minutes NOW — no three-year redshirt required. For a championship team with zero patience for long development arcs, that's a feature, not a bug. Mike Brown loves throwing young guys into regular-season minutes while he tinkers with rotations — it's exactly how Diawara went from afterthought to trusted piece — and an NBA-ready shooter is the easiest possible plug-in.

And picture what the weapon actually looks like in THIS offense, because the fit is almost unfair. Every set we watched Diawara feast on in that pro-run breakdown — the guard draws two, the weak-side shooter relocates, the kickout arrives — is a set designed for a player like Nickel. Now surround him with the most double-teamed player in basketball in Brunson, the gravitational pull of KAT, the drive-and-kick chaos of Hart and Alvarado... and understand that Nickel might see more WIDE-OPEN threes per minute in New York than he saw in his entire college career. Specialists don't need volume of touches; they need quality of looks. No roster in the league manufactures quality looks like the champs. Drop a 40% movement shooter into that machine and the machine gets scarier.

THE DECISION: THREE ROUTES ON THE TABLE

So why hasn't he signed anything yet? Because Nickel's contract is the keystone of the entire endgame — and per the reporting from the Knicks beat, the third-center CAMP BATTLE will more than likely determine his fate. Names like Drew Eubanks are already bubbling up for that competition with opening night about six weeks away, and it also explains the thing sharp fans have noticed all summer: all THREE two-way slots are still sitting untouched. That's not indecision. That's a front office keeping every chip in hand until the whole board resolves at once. Here are the three routes:

ROUTE ONE: SIGN NICKEL OUTRIGHT TO THE 14TH SPOT. The full commitment. You decide the shooting is real, you lock him onto a standard deal now, and you protect yourself long-term — because here's the wrinkle casual fans miss: parking him on a two-way makes him a restricted free agent as early as NEXT summer. We nearly learned that lesson the hard way with Diawara; winning a championship conveniently smoothed over what could've been an awkward negotiation. Sign Nickel outright now, and you own his rights on your terms. The cost: your 14th spot is spent, and the third center has to come from the two-way pool or the minimum market.

ROUTE TWO: NICKEL ON A TWO-WAY, CENTER GETS THE 14TH SPOT. The likelier path, honestly. Nickel takes a two-way, spends the year splitting time between Westchester and the big club, and the 14th standard spot goes to whichever big man survives training camp. Clean, simple, addresses the roster's actual hole — and if Nickel balls out, you do exactly what you did with Diawara: convert him later and re-sign him cheap. The risk is that RFA clock, but a front office this sharp can manage it.

ROUTE THREE: THE GALAXY-BRAIN OPTION. And this is where the contract mechanics get delicious. Sign a center to a NON-GUARANTEED deal for camp. If he earns it, great — he's your guy. If he doesn't? Waive him before January 1st and eat ZERO cap hit. Meanwhile, load the two-way slots with a center or two, keep Nickel in the mix, and then — here's the kicker — wait deep into the season for the PRORATED minimum to shrink, and sign a proven big (a buyout guy... a certain 7'1" grandmaster we discussed recently, perhaps?) for pennies when the market ripens. Maximum flexibility, minimum commitment, every door left open until February. The catch: this route banks heavily on KAT and Drummond staying healthy through the waiting period — a real risk for a title defense. But it's also EXACTLY how last season played out, when the final spot stayed fluid until the Jeremi Sochan-type and veteran PJ Tucker-style pieces arrived late. This front office has run this play before. And won a ring doing it.

WHY NO GUARDS NEED APPLY

One structural note that clarifies everything: the Knicks do NOT need to spend a single two-way on a guard. Count the room — Brunson at the top, Alvarado behind him, Kolek developing, Deuce still on the roster even amid the trade noise, Shamet and Clarkson on the wings. The backcourt is a fortress. Which means the math gets simple: two-ways go to CENTERS (and Nickel, in route two), and every remaining resource points at the frontcourt. Even the two-way market has names — Vlad Goldin is a plausible two-way target, though Miami would have matching rights to complicate it. The blueprint is visible from space: shooter secured, bigs auditioned, flexibility preserved.

THE EUBANKS REALITY CHECK

And a quick honest word on Drew Eubanks while we're here, because his name keeps circulating and expectations need calibrating. The stats from his injury-shortened season — 5.2 points, 3 boards, 60% from the field before the torn UCL in his thumb ended his year — describe exactly what he is: a SERVICEABLE depth big. Fine. Useful, even. But run him through the doctrine we've established all summer: young? No. Cheap long-term growth asset? No. A player who develops INTO a big-time backup role on a multi-year bargain deal? Not at this stage of his career. Eubanks checks precisely one box — warm body depth — which is why he profiles as a camp-battle contestant, not an answer. Same for the rest of that free-agent shelf: Xavier Tillman, Olivier Sarr, Tony Bradley — training camp deals, every one of them. The UNINSPIRING nature of that list is the whole reason the real solution still lives in the trade market and the buyout market, and why the front office refuses to spend real resources on the shelf. They're not settling. They're stalling — intelligently.

And zoom out to what Nickel represents in the bigger doctrine we've mapped all month, because he's the perfect case study. Remember the filter: young enough to grow, cheap enough to keep, one clearly identifiable NBA skill, visible development curve. McCullar failed it. Dadiet is on his final warning against it. Diawara set the curve. And Nickel? He might be the cleanest pass yet — an identifiable ELITE skill (the shot), acquired via one of those trade-back maneuvers fans grumbled about, arriving exactly when the bench lost shooting depth this summer. If he hits, the draft-and-develop machine will have produced rotation pieces in back-to-back classes for pennies — which, in the second-apron era, is the difference between a one-banner team and a dynasty.
