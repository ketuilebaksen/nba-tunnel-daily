## PART ONE: THE TWO-MINUTE STOPWATCH

Here's what Brown explained, as reported by Begley on November 5th.

**What they track:** Players are measured in two-minute chunks. The staff looks at shooting percentages, movement, and physical signs of fatigue.

**What they're looking for:** The moment a player's efficiency starts to dip. Instead of guessing when someone is tired, the staff gets data.

**What they've found:** Some players show a decline after about six consecutive minutes. In other words, three two-minute intervals in, the drop-off begins.

**The Brunson exception:** According to Brown, Brunson's offensive pressure actually increases with each interval. He doesn't show the drop-off other players do. He keeps up his intensity even through long stretches.

## WHY THIS IS A WEAPON

Think about how this connects to everything we've talked about this season.

**It explains the spread-out rotations.** Remember Chicago, when 10 players logged at least 13 minutes and the starters shared the floor for only 14 of 103 possessions? Or the Bulls blowout, when 14 players got in and 12 played at least 10 minutes? That isn't random. If the data says most players fade after six minutes, you sub more often. You keep fresh legs on the floor. That's a big reason Brown's bench units have been winning their minutes.

**It answers the fatigue question.** After the Philly loss, Josh Hart said, "We all feel the fatigue. We have to fight through it." The two-minute system is how the staff manages that. It's not about resting guys for whole games. It's about pulling them before the drop-off.

**It changes how we read Brunson's minutes.** If Brunson gets better the longer he plays, the logic flips. You don't cut his stints short. You let him cook. That's why he can have nights like Chicago, with 14 points in the final seven minutes of a 38-point game, or the Bulls rematch, with 22 points in the first quarter.

**It settles the clutch debate, at least partly.** A couple of videos ago, we discussed whether KAT should take over late-game possessions while Brunson's shooting recovered. This data is a strong argument for Brunson in crunch time. The longer the game goes, the more pressure he applies.

## THE CAVEAT: GETTING BETTER ISN'T THE SAME AS NOT GETTING TIRED

Let's be careful here, because this is important. "His offensive pressure increases" is not the same as "he never gets tired."

Brunson is 30. He's a smaller guard who drives into contact on almost every possession. He had surgery on his left shooting wrist in July. And since he arrived in New York, only Mikal Bridges and Anthony Edwards have logged more combined regular-season and playoff minutes than he has. That's a lot of mileage.

We also saw a real-world exception in Philadelphia: after his go-ahead three with 1:32 left, he went one-for-five in the final stretch and missed a key free throw. Data in two-minute chunks is powerful, but it doesn't capture everything, like the second night of a long week or the cumulative wear of a season.

So the smart use of this weapon is to let Brunson play long stretches inside games, and still manage his total load across the season. Those are two different things.

## WHAT THE DATA MIGHT SAY ABOUT OTHERS

Brown didn't name the players who fade after six minutes, and I won't guess. But it's easy to see how this kind of tracking explains some rotation decisions we've questioned. Clarkson playing short, explosive stints. Shamet coming in fresh and hot. Kolek running the second unit in bursts. If the staff sees exactly when efficiency drops, a nine-minute Clarkson stint might be by design, even if we fans want more.

That's my analysis, not Brown's words. But it adds a layer to the "play Clarkson more" argument. Maybe the answer isn't longer stints, but more of them.

## AND WHAT ABOUT KAT?

Here's an interesting angle on the big man. Karl-Anthony Towns came into this season noticeably slimmer, quicker, and more mobile. He's eight-for-eight in double-doubles. And his defensive rating was on pace to be the second-best of his career, per the early-season numbers we covered recently.

If the two-minute data rewards players who can hold their efficiency through long stints, a lighter, fitter KAT could be a big winner. The thing that has cut his minutes isn't fatigue. It's foul trouble. In the Detroit win, when he stayed on the floor without early fouls, he put up 27 points and 20 rebounds.

So for KAT, the two-minute lens says something simple: keep him on the floor and out of foul trouble, and he can carry big minutes. That's another reason the backup center question matters. When KAT has to sit because of fouls, the drop-off to the next big man is where the Knicks bleed points.

## PART TWO: THE MIKAL BRIDGES PROBLEM

Now to the hardest topic on the roster. According to an analysis published on November 4th, Bridges' numbers through seven games were:

- **Points:** 6.1 per game
- **Field-goal percentage:** 30.8
- **Three-point percentage:** 26.9

Those are some of the worst marks of his career. And the same analysis says Bridges ranks last on the roster in production among players who have logged more than 50 minutes this season.

**The minutes have followed.** Brown has kept Bridges on the bench for long stretches of the second half in several games, going to Landry Shamet and Jordan Clarkson in key moments. He played a career-low 13 minutes in Chicago and 16 against Brooklyn. In the Bulls blowout, he was scoreless in the first quarter and had seven points at halftime. I haven't seen his final line, so I won't guess.

**The message from Brown:** Performance on the court matters more than salary or what the team gave up to get you. No spot is guaranteed. That's consistent with everything he's said this season.

## WHY A BRIDGES TRADE IS HARDER THAN IT SOUNDS

We've covered the trade talk in our last two videos: the SI column about OKC, and the New York Post's Stefan Bondy asking whether trading Bridges could be the Knicks' escape hatch. Here's the reality check.

**The contract:** Bridges is in the first year of a four-year, $150 million extension, an average of $37.5 million per year. A team taking that on would want to see production first. Right now, his trade value is at its lowest.

**The picks:** The Knicks sent several first-round picks to Brooklyn for Bridges in 2024. That's one reason they're so short on draft capital now: their only tradable first-rounder is in 2033. So they can't attach picks to sweeten a deal.

**The cap:** With only about $370,000 under the second apron, per Daily Knicks, any deal would have to be close to salary-neutral or cheaper for New York.

So the irony is real. The trade that built the championship team is now the reason the Knicks have so few tools to move Bridges if they wanted to.

## SHOULD BRIDGES LOSE HIS STARTING SPOT?

This is the next question being debated, so let's do it honestly.

**The case for moving him to the bench:** Shamet just scored 22 against Brooklyn and started well in place of Hart against Chicago. Clarkson leads the team in plus-minus. Coming off the bench might take pressure off Bridges and let him rediscover his shot against second units.

**The case against:** Bridges is still one of the Knicks' best perimeter defenders. Starting lineup changes can shake a player's confidence even more. And the starting five that won the title, with Bridges in it, was plus-19.9 in the Finals.

**My take:** If anything changes, I'd do it quietly and temporarily. Something like: "Shamet starts while Hart is out, and Bridges plays a bigger bench role." Let him play against second units, get easy looks, and get to the free-throw line. He had zero free-throw attempts through his first six games. Then move him back when his confidence returns. That's my analysis, not a report.

## PART THREE: THE CENTER SHOPPING LIST

Now the frontcourt. In an analysis published on November 4th, Dan Favale laid out the Knicks' center problem: KAT is excellent, but Andre Drummond's minutes have been inconsistent, and James Wiseman hasn't consistently delivered, although his eight points, eight rebounds, and three blocks against the Bulls were his best night yet.

He named three under-the-radar targets. Let's go through each one with an honest cap check. Remember the rule: with about $370,000 of room under the second apron, the Knicks can't take back meaningfully more salary than they send out. Their best salary-matching piece is Pacôme Dadiet's roughly $3 million, which is now effectively expiring.

## TARGET 1: CHARLES BASSEY, GOLDEN STATE

**The profile:** A 6-foot-10 big man who rebounds, finishes around the rim, and could be an interesting pick-and-roll partner for Brunson.

**The salary:** About $2.45 million, a minimum-level deal.

**The cap check:** ✅ Works on paper. Dadiet's salary is higher than Bassey's, so New York would be sending out more money than it takes back.

**The catch:** He can't be traded until December 15th, and the Warriors might want to keep him for their own frontcourt needs.

**My read:** A realistic, low-cost option. He's not a game-changer, but he fits the "boring big who does the dirty work" profile we talked about last video.

## TARGET 2: KARLO MATKOVIĆ, NEW ORLEANS

**The profile:** A mobile big who can finish above the rim and has some potential as a three-point shooter. He's been getting more minutes while Yves Missi recovers from knee surgery. He isn't a defensive specialist, but his skills could allow lineups with him next to Towns.

**The salary:** About $2.3 million.

**The cap check:** ✅ Works on paper. A Dadiet-plus-second-round-pick package is the obvious structure.

**The catch:** New Orleans would have to want to move him, and with Missi out, he's useful to them right now.

**My read:** An intriguing fit, especially for spacing. But the defensive questions matter, because the Knicks' biggest frontcourt need is rim protection and toughness.

## TARGET 3: JAYLIN WILLIAMS, OKLAHOMA CITY

Quick reminder: that's Jaylin Williams, the big man, not Jalen Williams, the injured star wing. Different players.

**The profile:** He's 24 and has a rare combination of defensive rebounding, three-point shooting, and passing IQ. Since 2022, he's been part of an exclusive group of players with at least 10 defensive rebounds and three made threes per 100 possessions, a list that includes Luka Dončić, Jayson Tatum, Kevin Love, and Victor Wembanyama.

**The salary:** About $7.77 million.

**The cap check:** ⚠️ Difficult. Dadiet plus Deuce adds up to only about $6.9 million, which is still short of $7.77 million, and that's more than the Knicks' $370,000 of room covers. They'd need to add more salary, like Drummond, Alvarado once he's eligible, or Wiseman, or bring in a third team.

**The catch:** With Chet Holmgren out eight to 10 weeks, Williams is more important to OKC than ever. And as we covered last video, OKC is the team that might be shopping for bigs, not selling them.

**My read:** The most talented of the three, and the least realistic. Losing both Deuce and Alvarado for him would also gut the guard rotation.

## HOW IT ALL CONNECTS

Here's the through line. The two-minute data tells us Brown is managing fatigue more precisely than ever. That only works if he has enough reliable bodies to rotate. Right now, the weak spots are a slumping Bridges at the wing and a thin backup center spot.

Fix those two things, without giving away the depth that makes the system work, and Brown's system gets even stronger: fresher legs, more winning bench minutes, and more room to let Brunson do what the data says he does best, which is keep attacking.
