THE ONE-SKILL AUDIT: FIVE MEN, FIVE CALLING CARDS

JOHN KONCHAR — the skill: REBOUNDING. We've praised the defense for weeks, but the Bball Index numbers reveal the true superpower: 91st percentile at his position in defensive rebounding percentage AND in crashing the offensive glass off teammates' misses. Ninety-first — from a GUARD. He attacks the boards from the corners, hunts loose balls like they owe him money, and plays so much bigger than his frame that he can survive as a small-ball FOUR — the Josh Hart trick, performed by a camp invite. The trade-off is honest: the three-ball isn't trusted, which might park him in the dunker spot on offense. But rebounding travels every night, needs zero plays called, and is the single most Knicks-coded skill in existence. Of the three wings, the analysis says he's the likeliest to grab a seat if one opens — and the glass is why.

OCHAI AGBAJI — the skill: RIM PROTECTION... from a WING. This one surprised me, folks. At 6'5", Agbaji profiles as a small forward who's guarded mostly guards, and the perimeter file is honestly mixed — he does NOT navigate ball screens well, which caps his on-ball value. But watch what happens near the basket: blocks in the 80th percentile over two seasons, opponents shooting a full 2.0 points WORSE at the rim against him, 87th percentile in rim deterrence, 80th in rim protection among off-ball wings. That's a weak-side eraser wearing a shooting guard's body. In a scheme that demands as much from its help defenders as Mike Brown's does, that's a real, specific calling card — the low-man rotation that saves the possession. The catch: at 26, everything else needs work, and one niche skill has to beat four other men's niches.

BRUCE BROWN — the skill: SCREEN NAVIGATION. The ring gets the headlines, but here's the data-driven reason the front office has chased him for years: 89th percentile over three seasons in fighting through ball screens — which would instantly make him the SECOND-best screen navigator on the entire roster, behind only Jose Alvarado. And the detail that separates him from the other good navigators in the building: he holds up one-on-one afterward. Kolek and Clarkson chase over screens fine but leak in isolation; Brown's profile sits in the Hart-and-McBride tier, just behind OG himself. Translation: he's not just bench depth — he's PLAYOFF infrastructure, the guy you throw at the Brunson-hunters and pick-and-roll surgeons that June basketball is made of. Champions scout for April in September. This is what that looks like.

JAMES WISEMAN — the skill: VERTICALITY. And Tunnel fam, allow me a small smile, because the analysis landed exactly where this channel planted its flag: neither KAT nor Drummond is a lob threat — KAT stretches, Drummond crashes, nobody SOARS. Wiseman's career number at the rim: 75%, the 79th percentile among centers. A healthy version rolling into the acres of space this offense creates is a dimension the roster simply does not own — the vertical spacing we described as Brunson's missing toy. The audit's caveat is the audit's caveat: meniscus, Achilles, barely any basketball in two years, and nobody knows what's left in the legs. The whole bet compresses to one word: LIFT. If it's still there, he's the most interesting player in this camp. If it's gone, the calling card is a memory.

DREW EUBANKS — the skill: FEEL. The least flashy card on the table and maybe the most usable: a screening center who functions as an offensive release valve. The numbers sketch a specific player: 47% from the short mid-range on real volume — 73rd percentile among bigs — a reliable floater, and short-roll decision-making that keeps possessions alive when the defense blitzes Brunson. The wart is real: a passer turnover rate in the 18th percentile — the feel occasionally outruns the hands. But here's the tactical wrinkle worth watching: in the short-roll game specifically, he might offer something even Drummond doesn't, which makes a Brunson-Eubanks two-man pairing the single most interesting preseason experiment on the schedule. When Brunson gets trapped — and he gets trapped every night of his life — the man catching at the elbow decides everything.

Five men. Five distinct answers to five distinct problems. And one seat — unless the domino falls. That's not a camp battle, folks. That's a design. And notice the meta-lesson in how this shelf was built, because it's a masterclass in roster theory: the front office didn't sign five versions of the same insurance policy — they signed five DIFFERENT policies against five different disasters. Wing injury crisis? Konchar and Brown. Rim protection emergency? Agbaji's help defense, Wiseman's size. Brunson-blitz problem in a playoff series? Eubanks' short roll. Whatever this season decides to break, one of these five is the specific spare part. Most teams stock depth. This one stocked ANSWERS — and camp is just the process of learning which question the season will ask first.

THREE CHAMPIONS, THREE NEW JOB DESCRIPTIONS

Now flip to the men already wearing the rings, because the biggest changes this season may happen on the court, not the transaction wire. Three roles are quietly being rewritten.

KAT: FROM ADJUSTMENT TO IDENTITY. Remember the honest arc — during the regular season he openly admitted the new role was HARD. Then the playoffs arrived and the struggle turned into a masterpiece: fewer shots, better shots, the point-center operating 25 feet out, a problem four straight opponents never solved. The record in that stretch: FIFTEEN AND ONE. Read that again — 15-1 through the entire playoff gauntlet. And the underrated engine of it all was DISCIPLINE: he traded the bulldozer drives that used to buy offensive fouls for floaters and eurosteps, stopped biting on pump fakes, and watch these numbers — block percentage up from 1.5 to 4.4, his highest since he was twenty years old, and loose-ball fouls stretched from one every 58 minutes to one every 72, a 24% improvement. The 2026-27 assignment is brutal in its simplicity: be THAT — the playoff version — from opening night, for 82 nights, because until the center domino falls, there's no understudy for the leading man. Last spring can't be a cameo. It has to be the character now.

And one more beat on the KAT discipline story, because those foul numbers deserve their own sentence: fouls were the tax this man paid his entire career — the pump-fake bites, the reach-ins, the offensive charges that put him on the bench in third quarters his team needed him. What the playoffs proved isn't that KAT got lucky for two months; it's that the tax is OPTIONAL. He rewired a decade-old habit under the brightest lights in the sport, at age 30, mid-title-run. Players almost never do that. And a coaching staff that just spent a year performing that surgery on its own superstar? That's the same staff about to inherit a foul-prone Liam Robbins in Westchester and a raw Wiseman in camp. The KAT rewiring isn't just a feel-good stat — it's the organization's proof of method. They fix this exact disease here. It's the house specialty now.

JOSH HART: THE GLASS GETS HEAVIER. If KAT facilitates more, Hart's rebounding graduates from valuable to LOAD-BEARING, for three stacking reasons: the point-center spends possessions away from the glass, Robinson's offensive rebounding left in the divorce, and the big-man shelf is thin until further notice. But here's the fun wrinkle: Drummond changes the geometry. He even flashed a THREE-POINT shot last season — a genuinely new wrinkle — but he's no KAT out there, so when Drummond anchors bench units, expect Mike Brown to hit the gas: grab-and-go, secondary transition, easy baskets before the defense breathes. And who's the ignition for that engine? The man who's been grabbing-and-going his whole career. Hart doesn't need to change a thing — the season is changing around him, straight into his strengths.

DIAWARA: THE WIDEST RANGE IN THE BUILDING. And the third name carries the most fascinating fork: some corners of the league view Mohamed Diawara as one of New York's most valuable TRADE chips; this channel has documented all summer why he might instead be the season's key energy-saver. The need is mathematical: OG, Bridges, and Hart carry monster minutes, the roster tilts toward small guards, and SOMEBODY has to absorb wing minutes so the starters reach June with legs. The résumé says he's earned the audition: 36.9% from three last season, the Donovan Mitchell film-session legend, the Brickley-certified summer. The next step is specific: attack closeouts off the bounce, so defenses pay for running him off the line. And understand why that one skill is the hinge: right now, when Diawara catches in the corner, defenders sprint at him with abandon because the worst outcome is a made three — there's no counter. Add one hard dribble into a pull-up or a dump-off, and suddenly the closeout has to be honest, the drive lane opens, and a role player becomes a rotation player. It's the smallest possible upgrade with the largest possible consequence — the same single-skill leap that turned Deuce from a two-way afterthought into a champion's sixth man. This organization has the receipts on exactly this development. Mo's just next in the assembly line. If that comes, the wing rotation gets deep enough to rest champions without losing the plot. The formula for the whole roster, in one breath: Hart stays Hart, Diawara takes the step, KAT proves last spring was a preview and not a fluke — and this team gets SCARIER than the one that paraded.
