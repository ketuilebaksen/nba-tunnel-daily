OFFENSE 2.0: THE CHAMPIONS REFUSE TO STAND STILL

Here's Mike Brown, in his own words, on what changes this season: "Obviously playing with KAT more at the elbow, more than we did." Stop right there — the elbow-hub experiment that won a CHAMPIONSHIP is getting EXPANDED, not preserved. Then the sentence that made me rewind: "Being with this group for a year now, you get a better feel for what players can do. I didn't know OG could be as effective as he was in the pick-and-roll game. Same for Mikal Bridges. So to try and continue to find ways to get those guys involved at the top of the floor is something we'll take a look at." And he kept going: "We have great cutters that have tremendous size. We'll continue to mix that in. We all know KAT is a really good facilitator at the elbow. Maybe Andre is too — I don't know. Maybe we move OG to the five a little bit more than we did last year. There's a lot of different ways we can go... the biggest thing is knowing that NOTHING is set in stone." Begley confirmed all three pillars in his own reporting: more KAT elbow actions, more pick-and-roll for OG and Bridges, more OG minutes at backup center.

Now step back and appreciate what you're watching, because this is the difference between champions and former champions: the coach of the best offense in basketball spent his summer looking for what he MISSED. Most title-winning staffs laminate the playbook and pray. Brown instead admits — publicly — that year one taught him things he didn't know about his own stars, and year two will cash those lessons in. And connect the OG piece to the file we've been building all month: the All-Star case rested on KAT's sacrifice handing OG a 20-point platform. Now add BALL-HANDLING reps — actual pick-and-rolls, actions at the top, downhill decisions — to a man who was almost exclusively a finisher last season. That's not a tweak, folks; that's a dimension. Begley himself flagged how rare it was for OG or Bridges to have the ball last year outside bench units. The offense that won it all is about to have MORE weapons pointed in MORE directions. Sleep on the champs at your own risk.

And don't skip the throwaway line hiding in Brown's quotes, because it might be the sneakiest experiment of all: "We all know KAT is a really good facilitator at the elbow. Maybe ANDRE is too — I don't know." Andre Drummond... as an elbow hub. Folks, that's not idle musing — that's a coach wondering if the bench unit can run a diet version of the championship offense instead of a completely different one. Remember the media day plan: Drummond's veteran feel, the smart reads, the occasional three. If even a fraction of the KAT elbow package translates — the dribble-handoffs, the backdoor feeds to cutting size — then the second unit stops being a stylistic reset and becomes a CONTINUATION, and opponents' bench minutes stop being their breathing room. Add Brown's point about "great cutters with tremendous size" — Hart, Bridges, the wings diving off those elbow touches — and you see the full vision: one offensive language, spoken at two volumes, for forty-eight minutes. That's how you grind the league down, possession by possession, night after night.

THE OG-AT-FIVE DEBATE: MY HONEST PUSHBACK

But now the sentence that needs a full segment, because "maybe we move OG to the five a little more" deserves both its credit and its caution. The credit first: small-ball OG WORKS — we've seen it. Despite the frame, the man rebounds capably at the five, and a switch-everything lineup with OG at center is a legitimate playoff WEAPON: five defenders, zero hiding spots, matchup chaos for four minutes at a time. Picture the closing-lineup version: Brunson, Deuce or Shamet, Bridges, Hart, OG — five shooters, five switchers, and an opposing center dragged to the perimeter with nowhere to hide his feet. That's the lineup that ends third quarters and steals playoff games. As break-glass basketball, I love it. The coaches clearly see it as one answer to the third-center question — Begley connected those dots directly — and having that club in the bag lowers the desperation everywhere else. But here's my honest pushback, and I feel strongly: as a REGULAR-SEASON DIET, no. Absolutely not. Playing the five in this league means eating 279-pound rolls, boxing out monsters, absorbing forty collisions a night — and OG carries an injury history we all know by heart. You do not spend your newly-minted All-Star candidate's body on a job description you employ THREE actual centers to perform. This roster has Towns. It has Drummond. It will, by all indications, have Wiseman. Let the bigs be big, and save small-ball OG for the playoff nights when it breaks a series open. Use it like hot sauce — not like a meal.

And read WHY Brown is even floating it, because the subtext is the real story: he knows exactly what he has at center. KAT's whistle history — reformed but never cured. Drummond's pace questions — the honest worry we flagged after media day. A camp battle at third center that hasn't declared a winner. OG-at-five is the coach's insurance policy against all three — and, if you're thinking like a front office, it's quiet LEVERAGE too: the less desperate this team looks for center help, the better every trade conversation goes in February. Sometimes a coach's throwaway line is a message to twenty-nine other teams: we have in-house answers, so don't call us with bad offers. Chess, as always. But my prayer stands: let OG be OG — a power forward, a wing destroyer, a first-time All-Star. The five should be his costume, not his job.
