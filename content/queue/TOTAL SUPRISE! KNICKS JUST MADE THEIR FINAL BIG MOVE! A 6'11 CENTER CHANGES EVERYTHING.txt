## THE FINAL MOVE: THE 6'11" CENTER

Let's start with the decision. The Knicks brought five veterans into training camp on non-guaranteed deals: James Wiseman, Bruce Brown, Drew Eubanks, Tony Bradley, and Ochai Agbaji. Eubanks suffered a torn pectoral muscle and was waived. Bradley and Agbaji were released. That left Wiseman and Bruce Brown. And because James Dolan refuses to go into the second apron, keeping both would have required a trade. So the Knicks chose size.

Wiseman, 6-foot-11, will open the season as the third center behind Karl-Anthony Towns and Andre Drummond.

His story is fascinating. He was the number two overall pick in the 2020 draft, selected by Golden State. Injuries have wiped out huge parts of his career — he tore his left Achilles in the 2024–25 season opener with Indiana and played just five games over the last two seasons combined. Across five NBA seasons, he's averaged 9.0 points and 5.5 rebounds. But he has a strong connection in New York: Mike Brown was an assistant on Steve Kerr's staff in Golden State during Wiseman's first two seasons, and reports say Brown is a big fan.

**Why it "changes everything":** Because of what Wiseman brings that nobody else on the roster does — a vertical rim-runner who can catch lobs and finish above the defense. That's exactly the weapon Jalen Brunson lost when Robinson left. Is it a surprise? It is to anyone who expected the Knicks to protect a veteran wing like Bruce Brown. The front office looked at the hole in the middle and decided size mattered more. And with Eubanks out long-term and zero centers on two-way deals, there's no margin left behind KAT and Drummond — which is exactly why the third center had to be a real big, not a wing.

## THE HOLE: WHAT MITCH LEFT BEHIND

To understand the plan, remember what's missing. Mitchell Robinson spent his entire NBA career in New York before signing a three-year, $47.4 million deal with Boston on the first day of free agency. Last season, he averaged 8.8 rebounds in 60 regular-season games and finished eighth in Defensive Player of the Year voting. He was one of the best offensive rebounders in the league.

And second-chance points are part of the Knicks' identity. New York has ranked in the top 10 in grabbing its own misses in each of the last five seasons. That identity helped carry them to the 2026 championship. So the question isn't just "who plays center?" It's "who keeps the offensive glass alive?"

## THE SURPRISE ANSWER: OG ANUNOBY ON THE GLASS

And here's where the preseason gave us something nobody expected. OG Anunoby is crashing the offensive glass like never before.

In his four exhibition appearances, OG played close to 86 minutes and pulled down nine offensive rebounds. That works out to roughly 10 percent of New York's missed shots while he was on the floor.

Now, preseason numbers come with a warning label. Small sample, coaches testing lineups, games that don't count. But look at his history and you'll see why this matters. OG has never finished a full regular season with an offensive rebounding rate above 5 percent. His best mark in any playoff run sits around 7 percent. His previous preseason numbers were even lower. So 10 percent isn't a small bump. It's a completely different level of activity.

And it's rare for a player his size. Last season, only two players listed at 6-foot-7 or shorter finished with an offensive rebounding rate of 10 percent or higher: Justin Champagnie and Jabari Walker. Neither played anywhere close to the minutes OG plays for New York. If he holds onto even part of this habit, he becomes a unique weapon at the forward spot.

## LUCK OR INTENT? THE FILM SAYS INTENT

To be fair, some of those nine boards were luck. One came off a tip that happened to bounce his way. Another fell right into his hands after a missed three. But the film also shows real effort and real intent.

Against Washington, OG won inside position on almost every defender after a Brunson corner three, then grabbed the miss. Against Philadelphia, he used his strength to take away a rebound that looked like it belonged to Jabari Walker. In another sequence, he physically moved Dean Wade out of the way to secure the ball after a missed Mikal Bridges free throw.

None of those plays are shocking for a player with OG's frame and power. What stood out was how often he went after the ball.

## WHY IT'S A PLAN, NOT AN ACCIDENT

And there are good reasons to believe this is by design. The Knicks' frontcourt looks different this season. KAT is the starting center, and he shoots from the outside. Behind him, Drummond — the 33-year-old veteran signed after Robinson left — isn't a three-point shooter, but he's comfortable spacing out to the corner, something Mitch rarely did.

That changes how the Knicks hunt second chances. When Drummond is stationed in the corner, asking him to sprint to the rim on every miss is risky — if he crashes from that far away and doesn't get the ball, the other team can run out for easy transition points. Mitch lived near the basket, so his offensive rebounding came naturally from where he stood. KAT and Drummond will often start possessions farther from the hoop.

That's where OG fits. He's strong enough to battle bigger players under the rim, but quick enough to get back on defense when a crash doesn't work. He can pick his spots, attack the glass when the angle is right, and still protect against fast breaks. Mike Brown gets a middle ground: some of the extra possessions Mitch used to create, without leaving the defense exposed.

## A GROUP EFFORT

Nobody should expect OG to become Mitchell Robinson. That kind of production isn't easy to copy, and OG isn't built to do it alone. But the Knicks don't need one player to replace Mitch. They need a group effort.

Put the pieces together: OG crashing from the wing. Drummond, who's been a rebounding machine all preseason — including five offensive boards against Washington. KAT, who sits second on the betting board — at plus-500, behind only Nikola Jokić — to lead the entire league in rebounding, and who controlled the glass in the preseason finale on his way to finishing one assist shy of a triple-double. And now Wiseman, who grabbed seven offensive rebounds across three preseason games in limited minutes. If OG adds a few extra offensive boards every week, the burden on KAT and Drummond gets lighter, and the Knicks keep doing what they do best.

And here's a fun connection. OG delivered one of the signature moments of the NBA Finals in Game 4 against San Antonio — a performance that made Victor Wembanyama look uncomfortable on the biggest stage. Whether that night was an early sign of this new aggressiveness is anyone's guess. But it showed what OG can do when he decides to impose himself physically.

## THE KAT CHECKPOINT

Now to the biggest storyline hanging over the Garden. Monday, October 19th, was the deadline for Karl-Anthony Towns to decide on his $61 million player option for 2027–28. If he declined it, he'd stay eligible to sign an extension during the season. If he picked it up, he'd lock in that salary for next year but couldn't negotiate a new deal with the Knicks until after the season. As of this writing, no decision had been made public — and we'll update you the moment it is.

The two sides are far apart. According to Shams, the Knicks offered about $200 million over four years — Ian Begley has described it as somewhere between $45 and $50 million per season. KAT is eligible for a max of four years and $276 million. He's said he's willing to take a discount, just not one this large. And then the Knicks tabled talks with all eligible players — KAT, Josh Hart, Deuce McBride, and Tyler Kolek — until later in the season. KAT said he was caught off guard and very disappointed. Technically, the deadline for those extensions is June 30th, 2027, so there's time.

## THE FREE-AGENT MATH: WHY THE KNICKS AREN'T BUDGING

So why aren't the Knicks just raising their offer? The answer is the free-agent market. Over the past five summers, big spending on players who change teams has almost disappeared — and history suggests New York doesn't need to bid against itself.

Here are the numbers. Since 2022, only seven players have signed offseason deals worth more than $25 million a year while switching teams:

**2022:** Jalen Brunson to New York — about $26 million a year over four seasons.

**2023:** Fred VanVleet to Houston — about $42.8 million a year over three seasons, with a team option in the final year.

**2024:** Paul George to Philadelphia — about $52.9 million a year over four seasons. Isaiah Hartenstein to Oklahoma City — about $29 million a year with a third-year team option. Tobias Harris to Detroit — about $26 million a year for two seasons.

**2025:** Myles Turner to Milwaukee — about $27.2 million a year over four seasons.

**2026:** Walker Kessler to the Lakers — four years, and the Lakers needed a sign-and-trade to make it happen.

Look closer and the list shrinks even more. Only Brunson, George, Turner, and Kessler cleared $25 million a year on deals with more than two guaranteed seasons. Paul George is the only player in that entire stretch to change teams and earn at least $40 million a year. Even reaching $30 million is hard now.

Now compare that to the Knicks' offer: $45 to $50 million a year. Well below the max — but more than almost anyone has received on the open market in the last half decade. The league has changed. Stars who want out usually force trades instead of walking in free agency. Teams with cap space often use it early because big names rarely reach the market. And when a star does leave, it's rarely for the money and length KAT is looking for.

## BUT KAT STILL HAS LEVERAGE

That doesn't mean KAT has no leverage. It only takes one team to drive up a price. Stefan Bondy reported that the Chicago Bulls and LA Clippers have the clearest paths to chase Towns if he becomes a free agent, with the Brooklyn Nets, Golden State Warriors, and Dallas Mavericks as more complicated options. Daily Knicks has suggested as many as three teams could be desperate enough to offer him the max next summer.

And he's coming off a championship season in which he averaged 20.1 points, 11.9 rebounds, and 3.0 assists per game. He turns 31 on November 15th.

## THE APRON TWIST

And here's an important update to something we've discussed on this channel. We'd said that if KAT picked up his option, the January trade pressure could cool, because he'd be under contract next season. But there's another side: James Dolan has called crossing the second apron "suicidal." If KAT picks up his option, his $61 million salary next season would almost certainly push the Knicks into that zone — which could force the team to consider trading him either at the deadline or next offseason anyway. So both paths come with pressure. Declining makes him an expiring contract. Picking it up creates a next-season apron problem. Either way, this storyline isn't going away.

Not every part of the Knicks' approach looks smart. The public statement about pausing talks created noise right before ring night and left a star feeling disrespected. But on the financial side, the hard line is backed by real numbers. If history repeats, the open market won't offer KAT a better deal than the one waiting in New York — and he's more likely to come back to the Knicks' number than the Knicks are to move toward his.

## THE CISSÉ SAGA: CLOSED

And one more chapter closed on Monday. Back in July, the Knicks wanted Moussa Cissé as their third-string center and sent the restricted free agent a two-year offer sheet. Dallas matched. That should have been the end. But in recent weeks, with a roster crunch before Monday's cutdown, Dallas considered keeping veteran Dwight Powell — and Marc Stein reported Cissé was the player in the greatest danger of being waived.

On Monday, the answer arrived. Shams reported that the Mavericks are waiving Powell, who had been with the franchise since 2014. Cissé stays in Dallas.

For Knicks fans, that's actually a relief. If Dallas had released him after matching New York's offer, watching him get cut and then possibly signed somewhere else would have reopened the wound. Stein also reported several teams were interested — the Bulls, 76ers, Hawks, and Lakers. In Philly's case, it would've been a two-way deal, but seeing a player the Knicks wanted land with an Eastern rival wouldn't have felt good.

Dallas made the right basketball decision, even if it was an emotional one. Powell had become one of the faces of the organization. Cissé is entering only his second NBA season, which lines up with Cooper Flagg's timeline — and the Mavericks are building around youth.

Quick note for regular viewers: in earlier videos we discussed possible paths for Cissé to reach New York. With Dallas keeping him, that storyline is officially over. And the Knicks went another direction: the 6-foot-11 Wiseman.
