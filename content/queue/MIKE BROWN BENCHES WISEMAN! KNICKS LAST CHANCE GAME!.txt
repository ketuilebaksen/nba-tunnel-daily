## WHY THE COACH IS BENCHING HIS FRONTRUNNER

First, let's understand the move. Why would Mike Brown sit the guy who's been winning the race?

Simple: you can't evaluate players who don't play. For two games, Eubanks and Bradley have barely been on the floor. In the opener, both logged around six minutes. In the second game, neither one played at all, while Brown handed the minutes to Wiseman, Bruce Brown, Tyler Nickel, and Jaden Akins. Since then, both veteran centers have been quiet in terms of camp buzz, and that's only fed the idea that Wiseman is running in front.

So tonight is about fairness — and about information. Brown wants to see what Eubanks and Bradley look like in real game minutes, against real NBA size, without Wiseman sharing the floor. That makes this a rare chance for the two veterans to make their case. And frankly, they need to treat it like a last stand.

## THE SCOREBOARD SO FAR

Let's put the numbers side by side, because the gap is real.

**James Wiseman:** In the opener against Philadelphia — a 120–97 loss — he played just over nine minutes and scored eight points on three-of-four shooting, grabbed five rebounds, three of them on the offensive glass, and added a block. He was the first big man off the bench after Towns and Drummond, which many people read as a clear sign of where he stands. Then against Washington, he was again the only third center to play, with two blocks and a couple of lobs from Jose Alvarado.

**Drew Eubanks:** About six minutes in Philadelphia. One point, one rebound, one turnover, and two fouls. Didn't play against Washington.

**Tony Bradley:** Roughly six minutes in Philadelphia. He made his only shot attempt, but didn't record much else. Didn't play against Washington.

Stefan Bondy of the New York Post reported early in camp that Wiseman had impressed in practice and should be seen as the favorite. Ian Begley of SNY called Eubanks the safer choice, pointing to his steadier production over the years. So far, the games have backed Bondy. Tonight is the veterans' chance to change that.

## THE MEASURING STICK: GOBERT AND BERINGER

And the opponent makes this test tougher — and more useful. Minnesota brings one of the biggest frontcourts in the entire league. Rudy Gobert is a proven force on defense and on the boards — a multiple-time Defensive Player of the Year who controls the paint like few players ever have. And alongside him, second-year big man Joan Beringer, who has drawn a lot of attention around the league for his length and athleticism.

For a backup center hoping to prove he can survive a few important minutes a night, there may be no better measuring stick. If you can hold your ground on the glass against Gobert, set screens that actually free shooters against Minnesota's length, and avoid getting bullied — you've shown the coaching staff something real. If you get overwhelmed, the staff has its answer too.

Think about it from Mike Brown's chair. In April, May, and June, the Knicks won't be facing G League bigs. They'll face Gobert-type centers, Anthony Davis in Washington, Jarrett Allen, Evan Mobley, and now Mitchell Robinson in Boston. A night like tonight is the closest thing to a playoff audition that the third center will ever get in October.

## THE LAST STAND TEST: WHAT EUBANKS AND BRADLEY MUST SHOW

So what does a winning night look like? Both players have built careers as role players. Both know what it means to come off the bench and do the dirty work. But the Knicks aren't looking to fill a seat. They want the best man for the job. Being a big body isn't enough. They need to show something concrete. Here's the NBA Tunnel checklist.

**Test one: rebounding in traffic.** Against Gobert, every rebound is a fight. A veteran who can box out and secure the ball against that size shows he can protect the Knicks' possessions when it matters.

**Test two: rim protection without fouling.** Eubanks picked up two fouls in six minutes in Philadelphia. That can't happen again. A third center who fouls out of short stints is useless. They need to contest, stay vertical, and stay on the floor.

**Test three: smart screening.** The Knicks' second unit lives on Alvarado, Shamet, Clarkson, and Deuce coming off screens. A big who sets hard, legal screens and creates real separation makes the whole bench better.

**Test four: decision-making on the short roll.** Neither Eubanks nor Bradley is a vertical lob threat like Wiseman. So their value on offense has to come from quick, smart decisions — a pass to the corner, a floater, a dump-off. If they hold the ball or drift into Brunson's driving lanes, that's a problem.

**Test five: no mental mistakes.** Turnovers, missed rotations, late closeouts. On a night when the coach is watching every possession, the small things become the big things.

A strong outing from either veteran would keep the door open. If neither stands out, the open competition could effectively be over tonight.

## AND THE AGBAJI QUESTION

Ochai Agbaji also gets his turn tonight, and his stakes are just as high. In the opener, he played eight minutes and went zero-of-three from deep. His entire case has always rested on his shot — a former lottery pick who's bounced around and could find another level in a winning system. Against a long, athletic Minnesota team, he'll get open looks as the defense collapses on the Knicks' creators. He needs to make them. One good shooting night tonight, and maybe another in Toronto, and the conversation changes. Another cold night, and the wing door probably closes too.

## THE ENDGAME: WHY TORONTO WILL BE ALL WISEMAN

After tonight, the Knicks have only two exhibitions left: a home-and-home with the Toronto Raptors on October 13th and October 15th. Those games are meant to be final tune-ups before the title defense starts on October 20th at the Garden against the 76ers.

And here's the logic. At that stage, it would make much more sense for the coaching staff to go full speed ahead with one third center than to keep splitting reps among three candidates. So unless Eubanks or Bradley has a huge night tonight, expect the Toronto games to be about getting Wiseman ready — not testing him.

Why do those extra minutes matter so much for Wiseman? Because he's appeared in only five NBA games over the last two seasons. Five. Every possession he gets now helps him shake off the rust and adjust to a new system. The Knicks can't control whether his body holds up over a long season. But they CAN control how prepared he is when the games start to count.

## WHY THE THIRD CENTER MATTERS MORE THIS YEAR

Some fans might ask: why are we spending this much time on the third center? He won't play every night. Here's why it matters more for this team than for most contenders.

With less than two weeks until the regular season, the Knicks have only two true centers locked onto the roster: Karl-Anthony Towns and Andre Drummond.

KAT often battles foul trouble, and after a long championship run, keeping him fresh over 82 games is a priority. Remember, he's also in the middle of a contract standoff, chasing a rebounding title at +500 odds, and turning 31 next month. The Knicks need to manage his body carefully.

Drummond brings experience, rebounding, and — so far this preseason — better defense than many expected. But there are questions about his age and how effective he can be night after night, and he's dealt with health issues in the past. If those return, the Knicks need someone ready to step in.

So the third center may not play every night. But when he does, he'll be asked to hold the line in meaningful moments — a back-to-back, foul trouble in a big game, an injury in February. That's why this decision matters.

## WISEMAN'S "SECOND DRAFT": A CAREER WITHOUT A HOME

Now let's zoom out on Wiseman, because the real debate goes far beyond one preseason game. It's about risk and reward. And there's a strong argument that the Knicks should see his uncertainty as an opportunity, not a warning sign.

Start with a simple fact: Wiseman has barely played. In five NBA seasons, he's appeared in just 152 games — less than two full regular seasons of work. And his path has been rough from the beginning.

**Stop one: Golden State.** The Warriors took him second overall in 2020, during a period when they were trying to chase championships and develop young prospects at the same time. That's one of the hardest environments in the league for a raw 19-year-old center. Wiseman struggled under that pressure, missed his entire second season with a knee injury, and was traded once it became clear he didn't fit the Warriors' read-and-react offense.

**Stop two: Detroit.** He spent two seasons with the Pistons and played 87 games — but on teams that were losing far more often than they were winning, he made little lasting impact.

**Stop three: Indiana.** He joined the Pacers in 2024, and on opening night of the 2024–25 season, he tore his Achilles tendon. Since that injury, he's played only a handful of NBA games.

Put that together, and a clear picture forms. Wiseman has never really been given a stable place to grow. A high-pressure dynasty in Golden State. A struggling team in Detroit. A major injury in Indiana. New York might be the most stable environment he's ever landed in. The Knicks are the reigning champions. They have a clear structure. And they can afford to be patient while he learns behind two experienced pros in Towns and Drummond.

## THE TOOLS AND THE JOB

The physical tools are still there. Wiseman stands 6-foot-11 and weighs about 240 pounds. He showed up to training camp in excellent shape, and in his preseason debut, he showed off his athleticism with a high-flying alley-oop finish and a strong putback after an offensive rebound.

Scoring has never been his biggest problem. During his short time with the Warriors, he averaged about 19 points per 36 minutes. And even in Detroit, he showed flashes of a more complete offensive game — including a perfect shooting night in October 2023, when he scored 20 points with 10 rebounds in 23 minutes off the bench.

There are real weaknesses too. He's never been much of a passer, and his defense hasn't matched what you'd expect from someone with his size and athleticism. But this is where the Knicks' situation helps. As a third center, he won't be asked to do everything. His job description is short: set hard screens. Run dribble handoffs. Roll hard to the rim. Finish strong. Stay active and tall on defense. Plenty of backup bigs have built useful careers by focusing on exactly those things.

## THE LONG-TERM ANGLE: WHY THE FUTURE POINTS TO WISEMAN

And then there's the long-term view — which might be the strongest part of his case.

Wiseman is only 25. Eubanks is 29. Drummond is on a one-year contract. The Knicks didn't draft a center this year, and they don't have first-round picks in two of the next three drafts. If New York keeps this core together, its flexibility in free agency will be limited for years — especially with the second apron squeezing every decision, from the KAT negotiation down to the last roster spot.

In that context, finding a young center who can grow into a reliable backup isn't a luxury. It could be a necessity. And Wiseman would come cheap this season. If he performs well, he'd likely be a player the Knicks could keep at a reasonable cost — exactly the kind of value a team in a tight financial spot needs.

Let's keep expectations in check, though. Many "second draft" players — former top picks trying to revive their careers with a new team — never reach their early promise. But some do find a home as solid rotation players. Marvin Bagley III, another former number two pick, has carved out a role as a backup center in recent seasons. Wiseman could follow a similar path if the Knicks commit to developing him and he embraces a limited role on a championship team. Depth now, value later. That's a risk worth taking.

## OUTSIDE THE GYM: THE KEL'EL WARE CONVERSATION

While the Knicks sort out their third center from inside training camp, another big man has entered the conversation from outside: Kel'el Ware. And if you've been with this channel for a while, you know we already did a full five-questions breakdown on him. But there's a new development — and it's not great news for anyone dreaming about him in a Knicks jersey.

Quick refresher on how Ware even became part of the Knicks discussion. Before Giannis Antetokounmpo was traded by the Milwaukee Bucks, there was plenty of speculation that the two-time MVP could end up in New York. ESPN's Brian Windhorst reported on that possibility, and Knicks fans talked about it for months. In the end, Giannis didn't come to the Garden. Milwaukee sent him, along with Bobby Portis, to the Miami Heat in a blockbuster — and Ware, who spent his first two NBA seasons in Miami, was part of the package going back to the Bucks.

That trade changed everything in Milwaukee. Kevin O'Connor, a senior NBA analyst at Yahoo Sports, recently wrote that the Bucks could become a central player in the trade market. He pointed to two centers who could draw strong interest around the league: Ware and veteran Myles Turner. And he named seven teams from his top 13 power rankings that should be interested in one of them: the Knicks, Celtics, Lakers, Pistons, Raptors, 76ers, and Timberwolves. That's a list full of New York's biggest rivals — which only added fuel to the idea that the Knicks could jump into the race.

## BOBBY MARKS: "HARD TO IMAGINE"

But interest and an actual trade are two very different things. And that's where Bobby Marks comes in. Marks, a former NBA front office executive who's now one of the most respected voices on contracts and the salary cap, appeared on Knicks Fan TV and was asked about the possibility. His answer was blunt: he finds it hard to imagine Milwaukee doing anything with Ware.

And when you look at who Ware is and what he costs, the reasoning is easy to follow. The Heat took him 15th overall in the 2024 draft. In the 2025–26 season, he appeared in 77 games for Miami, made 34 starts, and averaged 11.1 points, 9.0 rebounds, 0.7 assists, 0.8 steals, and 1.1 blocks per game. Those are strong numbers for a young center still early in his development. And the contract is just as appealing — he's still on his rookie-scale deal, which means near-double-double production at a cheap price.

For a Bucks team that just moved on from its franchise superstar and has to reshape its future, Ware is exactly the kind of young piece a rebuilding front office wants to KEEP, not sell. It's hard to picture Milwaukee moving him unless it receives a major return.

## WHY IT DOESN'T FIT THE KNICKS RIGHT NOW

And that's the challenge for New York. The Knicks enter their title defense with nearly every key player back from the championship roster, and they've worked hard to stay under the league's most restrictive spending limits. Building a package strong enough to pry a young, cheap, productive center away from a team with every reason to keep him would not be easy. It would likely require assets the Knicks aren't eager to move — and remember, they can't trade a first-round pick before 2033. It could also disrupt a rotation that just delivered a title.

There's also the question of need. The Knicks already have KAT starting at center and Drummond behind him, with the third spot being decided in camp. Ware would be a long-term upgrade, but the price matters for a team in championship mode. Any move has to make sense for this season AND the years ahead.

For now, there's no sign that the Knicks and Bucks are talking. No report has confirmed any discussions, and the idea remains firmly in the speculation stage. Still, the conversation says a lot about how the league sees the Knicks as champions: they're going to be linked to almost every notable player who becomes available. Some of those links will turn into real talks. Many won't. And with Ware, at least one respected voice believes this one is very unlikely to happen.

So if you're keeping score on the big-man trade board this fall: Ware looks like a long shot. And that's exactly why the cheaper, more realistic names — the ones we've covered like Ryan Kalkbrenner — and the internal option in Wiseman matter so much.
