THE STEAL: DILLON JONES TO PHILADELPHIA

Per NBA insiders Kyle Neubeck and Ian Begley, the Sixers are signing Dillon Jones to an Exhibit 10 contract. And before anyone shrugs at the name, understand what walked out the door. Jones is a TWO-TIME NBA champion — a piece of Oklahoma City's title before landing in New York and adding a second ring in orange and blue. And per the reporting, he wasn't just a body at the end of the bench: this was a player Mike Brown genuinely leaned on inside that championship locker room, one of those culture-carrier voices whose fingerprints never show up in a box score. Now picture the scene the schedule has already teed up: October 20th, ring night at the Garden, banner rising... and both Hukporti AND Jones watching from the visitors' bench in Philadelphia blue. The Sixers aren't just building a roster. They're deliberately collecting pieces of OURS.

Quick mechanics for everyone, because the contract type matters: an Exhibit 10 is a non-guaranteed training camp deal — Jones will compete for a two-way spot in Philly and could technically still be waived. But read the intent: a franchise that just went all-in on talent is now vacuuming up CHAMPIONSHIP EXPERIENCE wherever it can find it, because deep down they know exactly what their roster is missing. More on that in a minute — because it's the whole story of their season.

And let's talk about the institutional-knowledge angle one more time, because Philly is now double-dipping. We flagged it when Hukporti signed: a player who lived inside your schemes carries your playbook out the door with him. Now add Jones — a guy who sat in the film sessions, heard the coaching staff's adjustments in real time, and by all accounts had Mike Brown's EAR during the run. Two players who know how the champion thinks, both landing with the single most dangerous challenger in the conference, months before a season where these teams could meet in May with everything on the line. Is it fatal? No — playbooks evolve, and Mike Brown will have new wrinkles by October. But it's not nothing either. The Sixers are being deliberate about this, and pretending otherwise would be naive. Respect the chess move... and circle the counter.

THE EXODUS: EVERY TWO-WAY FROM THE TITLE TEAM IS GONE

Now zoom out to what this confirms on our side: every single two-way player from the championship roster is officially out the door. Trey Jemison III — gone to Toronto. Dillon Jones — gone to Philly. Kevin McCullar Jr. — as we broke down with the Edwards reporting, deemed too old with too much injury history to bring back. Clean sweep. All three two-way slots in New York sit EMPTY as of today, and not a single one has been filled.

And by now, you know better than to read that as negligence. This is the pattern of the entire offseason: hold every chip until the whole board resolves at once. The 14th standard spot is trending toward a veteran big out of the camp battle — the Eubanks-Olynyk-and-friends gauntlet we've mapped. Which, as we covered in the Nickel deep-dive, all but locks in the rookie sharpshooter's path: Tyler Nickel's route onto this team runs through a two-way contract, and I fully expect that domino to drop soon. That leaves two open two-way slots — and if you're asking me to bet, the profile is obvious: BIGS. No guards need apply with Brunson, Alvarado, Kolek, and Deuce in the building. My money says one of those slots lands on JULIAN REESE — yes, Angel Reese's younger brother, the double-double machine we profiled weeks ago. The 10.5 rebounds a game he grabbed when Washington actually played him is precisely the skill this roster bleeds without Mitchell Robinson, and on a two-way he costs nothing but a Westchester locker. A rebounding four, a developmental center, and Nickel — that's your likely two-way trio, and it fits the doctrine like a glove: young, cheap, hungry, growing.

THE PHILLY PROBLEM: YOU CANNOT SIGN CHEMISTRY

And now the main course, because the team poaching our champions deserves a full examination — and I'm going to say the thing their own fanbase is quietly whispering: this superteam is built on a fault line. Let's be fair first: on paper, Philadelphia is BREATHTAKING. LeBron James. Jaylen Brown. Joel Embiid. Tyrese Maxey. VJ Edgecombe coming off a real breakout. On raw talent, you can absolutely argue they match or exceed anybody, the champs included. Vegas prices them one notch behind us for a reason.

But championships aren't won on paper, and here comes the checklist of questions with no good answers. LeBron James is a marvel of longevity — and he is also not prime LeBron, he will not play 82, and this two-year window closes the moment he leaves or retires; miss it, and the whole project is branded a failure. Joel Embiid — do we honestly, in the year of our lord 2026, believe in a fully healthy Embiid season? History says laugh. VJ Edgecombe just had a genuine breakout... and now must voluntarily SHRINK, sliding down the pecking order of a stacked roster — ask any young player how fun that assignment is. Jaylen Brown is at his best with the ball in his hands — and with LeBron functionally playing point guard, how often does he get it? Then Tyrese Maxey, whose entire superpower is downhill lightning WITH the rock — what exactly is his role in this traffic jam? Five stars, one basketball, zero shared history.

And that's the deepest issue: you cannot trade for chemistry. You cannot sign it on an Exhibit 10 — ironically, that's exactly what the Jones signing admits; they're trying to IMPORT championship DNA one minimum contract at a time because the core has none together. Chemistry is built through shared seasons, shared playoff scars, shared sacrifices — the exact currency the Knicks have been compounding for years. Brunson took less money. Hart and Bridges and OG each absorbed role changes without a whisper of drama. Everyone on this roster knows precisely who they are and — more importantly — who they are when the game tightens in April. Philly's roster will spend all season learning that from scratch, in the loudest media market pressure cooker outside our own, with a fanbase demanding a title by June. It's EASY to assemble talent. It is brutally hard to win with it in year one — the NBA graveyard is stacked with year-one superteams. And by the time Philadelphia figures itself out? The roster churns, the window shifts, and the chemistry lesson starts over. That's the trap they've built for themselves.

History backs every word of this, by the way. Run the tape on year-one superteams: the 2011 Heat needed a full season of public failure — and an actual Finals loss — before figuring out who they were. The 2013 Nets bought a dynasty's worth of names and delivered a second-round exit. The Warriors' KD experiment worked precisely because it added ONE transcendent piece to an already-built champion — the opposite of what Philly's doing, which is five alphas and a whiteboard. Even last season proved the thesis from our side of the fence: multiple rosters were arguably more talented than the Knicks on paper. Where are their parades? Talent sets your ceiling; chemistry decides whether you ever touch it. New York spent three years paying chemistry's price in advance. Philadelphia is planning to pay it live, on national TV, with a two-year clock ticking. Good luck.

RING NIGHT: THE STATEMENT GAME

Which brings us to opening night, and the first receipts. The money line is out for October 20th: Knicks -210, Sixers +176 — New York clearly favored at home. Honestly? The gap should be WIDER. Think about what that night is: the banner going up. The rings coming out. Fifty-three years of waiting crystallized into one ceremony — and then a basketball game against the superteam constructed specifically to end the party, with two of our former champions watching from their bench. If you scripted a statement game, you couldn't do better.

And the statement writes itself: the championship didn't dull the hunger — it sharpened it. This league has crowned a different champion every year for nearly a decade; nobody has gone back-to-back since Golden State. The entire NBA assumes the trend holds. The oddsmakers, as we covered yesterday, respect the champs in the East but pick against the repeat. Brunson has already banned the trophy from the conversation. So ring night isn't a celebration with a game attached — it's the first exam of the thesis this whole season runs on: that chemistry beats talent, that continuity beats headlines, that the team that KNOWS itself beats the team still introducing itself. Beat Philly on banner night, and the message lands league-wide before Halloween.

And a quick word to the fans already stressing about these departures, because perspective matters. What did the champs actually LOSE this summer, roster-wise? A third-string center, three two-way players, and a beloved backup who priced himself to Boston. What did they KEEP? The entire starting five. The entire core rotation. The coach, the system, the culture, and every meaningful minute of last June. Meanwhile, the two-way pipeline that produced these departures is the same machine that will mint their replacements — that's what pipelines DO. Jemison, Jones, and McCullar leaving isn't erosion; it's graduation season, and the next class is already enrolling. The doctrine holds: develop, deploy, replace, repeat. Championship organizations don't mourn role players. They manufacture new ones.

THE BOTTOM LINE

So let Philadelphia keep shopping in our closet — Hukporti, Jones, whoever's next. Every poached role player is a quiet confession of what money can't buy them. The champs, meanwhile, keep doing the unglamorous thing: holding their slots, running their camp battles, developing their kids, and defending the only thing that matters. Talent gets you on the magazine covers in October. Chemistry gets you the parade in June. We already know which one this front office chose — the banner in the rafters is the receipt.
