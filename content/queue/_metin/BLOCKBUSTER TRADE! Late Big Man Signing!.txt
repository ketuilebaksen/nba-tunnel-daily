STORY ONE: N'FALY DANTE — THE MISSING INGREDIENT, STASHED

Any time Leon Rose signs a 6'11" big, this channel's ears perk up — and per Alder Almo of Heavy, that's exactly what happened: the Knicks signed center N'FALY DANTE to an Exhibit 10, while also signing-and-waiving Jaden Akins to lock his affiliate rights into Westchester — the exact pipeline mechanics we broke down when Akins first surfaced; after 60 days with the affiliate, the Exhibit 10 bonus kicks in and the development continues in-house. Quick Akins refresher, now with fuller numbers: the 6'4" Michigan State guard averaged 14.7 points and 4.8 assists on 35.8% from deep in the G League last season, carried Big Ten All-Defensive honors as a point-of-attack stopper in college, and dropped 21 on the Warriors in Vegas. The guard lane of the basement, secured.

But Dante is the headline — because look at the profile: a 6'11" interior ERASER who dominated at Oregon and then put up monster G League production with Rio Grande Valley — nearly ten boards and over two blocks a night on a scorching 74.4% from the field, the kind of efficiency you only post when everything you touch gets dunked — plus eight NBA appearances with Houston and Atlanta to prove the level isn't foreign to him. Lob-catching, glass-cleaning, shot-altering verticality. Stop me if you've heard this: it's the EXACT missing ingredient we identified in yesterday's recipe breakdown — the one Robinson trait neither KAT nor Drummond carries. Now the catch, because with us there's always a catch: Dante tore his right ACL in December and had season-ending surgery in January — he won't be ready for opening night, and nobody should fit him for a ring in October. But that's precisely what makes this smart: supervise the rehab in Westchester, on the organization's medical staff, at zero risk — and if the knee comes back, you own a bouncy 7-foot rim protector no one paid a dime for. It's the Dante stash next to the Broome watch next to the Robbins trade next to the Cissé countdown: the basement is being stocked with EXACTLY one archetype. The recipe knows what it's missing.

STORY TWO: THE "UNTRADABLE" PANIC, DEBUNKED BY THE WAR CHEST

Now to the cleanup on aisle Dadiet, because after the Bondy "no value" report sent this fanbase spiraling, Dan Favale stepped in with the rebuttal the moment needed — and it's the same distinction we drew in the autopsy video: ZERO trade value is not UNTRADABLE. The toll is real; the question is whether you can pay it. So let's finally count the wallet, because Favale laid out the full war chest and it's absurd. Draft night 2026: the front office spun picks 24, 31, and 55 into Jack Kayil at 39, Tyler Nickel at 47... and FIVE future second-rounders — Phoenix's 2029, Sacramento's 2029, Philly's 2030, Dallas's 2032, Phoenix's 2033. Stack those on the existing drawer — two 2027 seconds including Washington's, Boston's 2028 top-45 protected, an incoming 2028 from Indiana or Phoenix, plus New York's own 2032 and 2033 — and Leon Rose is sitting on up to TWELVE future second-round picks. Twelve. The man built a currency reserve specifically for cap mechanics like this.

And the precedents are right there in recent memory: it was second-rounders at last year's deadline that turned Guerschon Yabusele's player-option contract into Jose Alvarado and Jeremy Sochan — arguably the trade that won the title's margins. Around the league? Minnesota just dumped Julius Randle's $33.3 MILLION contract on Brooklyn for the price of sliding five spots in a draft. If thirty-three million moves for that, Dadiet's sub-$3 million moves for — at absolute most — ONE second out of twelve. So let's retire the panic officially: the domino was never stuck; it was waiting for the front office to decide the timing. The roster spot opens the moment they need it open. The wallet says so.

STORY THREE: THE CURRY COMPLICATION — GOLDEN STATE ENTERS THE CHAT

And now the story that genuinely raised my blood pressure, because the KAT stall we covered this week just acquired a VILLAIN — and it's the most beloved man in basketball. Steph Curry signed a two-year, $116 million extension with Golden State — roughly $20 million BELOW his full max, first-year salary at $55.7 million. Sweet loyalty story for the Bay, right? Except run the ripple: that discount leaves the Warriors with a projected $64 MILLION in cap space for the summer of 2027... and the max-slot projection that summer is $61.6 million. Golden State just built a full max chair — and now scan the calendar for which All-NBA big man might hit unrestricted free agency in 2027 if a certain player option gets declined. Yeah. THAT's why a signing in California matters at the Garden.

Do the leverage math from KAT's side of the table, because his agent certainly is: New York, per the reporting, has been seeking a discount extension starting around $45-50 million a year — well under the four-year, $276 million max he's eligible for — because Dolan's mandate keeps the payroll under the $221.7 million second-apron ceiling. That ask was always going to be delicate. Now the counterparty has a printed alternative: why absorb a $15 million annual haircut when Golden State can offer the full $61 million... to play next to Steph Curry? The stall suddenly has an explanation and an expiration risk at once. My honest verdict, updated from this week's file: the pay-cut culture is real, the hometown pull is real, the Garden legend arc is real — but you cannot ask a man to bid against NOTHING anymore. There's a number between $45 and $61 million that keeps the core intact AND respects what KAT sacrificed for this banner. Find it, Leon. Because 2027 Golden State is no longer hypothetical — it's funded.

And spare one thought for the deeper irony of the Curry mechanism, because it's almost poetic: the discount is CONTAGIOUS — just not in the direction New York wants. Brunson's pay cut became the founding myth of this championship culture; the whole apron survival plan rests on stars accepting less to keep more around them. Curry just ran the exact same play in Golden State... and his sacrifice becomes the WEAPON aimed at ours. One legend takes less so his franchise can chase another franchise's legend, who's being asked to take less. The second-apron era in one sentence, folks: every act of loyalty somewhere becomes leverage somewhere else. Welcome to the CBA's hall of mirrors.

STORY FOUR: DEUCE GOES PUBLIC — AND DROPS THE NAME

This one hurts, folks. Deuce McBride — the longest-tenured Knick, the heartbeat of the grit era — spoke publicly about his extension situation in an exclusive with Kristian Winfield of the Daily News, and the quotes deserve to be read carefully: "I love being in New York. I'm a little disappointed. Of course, you want to come to the right terms, and I want to be in New York — so yeah, it's a little hard at times. But I'm going to be a professional." Disappointed. Hard at times. From the man who played through a sports hernia, had the surgery, and still suited up for every single playoff game of the title run. Twelve points a night on 41.3% from deep at under $4 million — the best value contract in the sport — and the reward so far is silence from the table.

And then the sentence that made me sit up: Deuce specifically cited how IMMANUEL QUICKLEY went through contract negotiations before being TRADED. Read that reference for what it is, Tunnel fam — that's not a random memory. IQ was the last homegrown fan-favorite guard who couldn't get his number from this front office... and he ended up in the OG trade. Deuce name-checking that history in a public interview is a message flare: he sees the pattern, he knows the pendulum we've been tracking — the warm Begley reports, the cold Edwards math, the $12-15 million raise that flirts with the apron — and he's telling the front office, politely and professionally, that he's not naive about how this ends if the terms don't come. My position hasn't moved an inch since the plot-twist video: the compliant extension — three years, controlled raises, fully tradable — solves this TODAY. It pays the man who earned it, protects the asset, and honors the one doctrine this front office swore after Robinson: never lose a champion for nothing. And notice how this file now sits next to the KAT file on the same desk: two locker-room pillars, both publicly gracious, both privately squeezed, both watching how the other's negotiation resolves. The culture that Brunson's discount built is the most valuable asset this franchise owns — more valuable than any pick in the twelve-deep war chest — and culture is exactly the thing that erodes when champions have to ask twice. Every day of silence makes the February version of this story more likely. Pay Deuce, or the disappointment quote becomes a farewell thread.
