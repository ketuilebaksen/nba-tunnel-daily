SIGNAL ONE: THE FIRST PULL-UP

Start with the moment twenty thousand hearts will hold their breath for, even in an exhibition: Jalen Brunson's first pull-up jumper off the surgically repaired wrist. Forget the make or miss — watch the MECHANICS. Does the follow-through snap clean, or does it feather? Does he absorb contact on his first downhill drive and finish through it, or does he bail into a kick-out a half-second early? The captain told us he's fully cleared, and the medical file agrees — but clearance and comfort are different animals, and preseason is where comfort gets rebuilt one rep at a time. The second number to track: his MINUTES. Remember the collision we flagged — Brunson demanding a fast start while owning the second-heaviest workload of his career, as the only starter whose total minutes actually ROSE last season. If the staff is serious about protecting the engine, we should see short, sharp bursts — twelve to eighteen minutes, out by the fourth. If he's grinding twenty-five in a game that doesn't count, that's a data point too — and not the good kind. The wrist tells us about October. The minutes tell us about MAY.

SIGNAL TWO: OFFENSE 2.0 LEAVES THE LAB

The second thing to watch is the installation we've been promised: the redesigned attack Mike Brown sketched at camp. Three specific tells. Tell one: KAT's ELBOW VOLUME — count how many possessions begin with the big fella catching at the pinch, surveying, and running the offense from the spot that won a championship. Last year it was a playoff adaptation; this year it's supposed to be the default, and preseason is where defaults get drilled. Tell two: watch for OG and Bridges with the ball in their HANDS — actual pick-and-rolls, actions at the top of the floor, downhill reads. The coach admitted he didn't know what he had in those two as creators; now count the experiments. Every OG ball-screen rep in a meaningless October game is a weapon being loaded for a meaningful May one. And tell three, my favorite sleeper: the Drummond elbow experiment. If the second unit runs even a stripped-down version of the elbow offense through the veteran — a dribble-handoff here, a backdoor feed there — then the bench isn't resting the system this year; it's CONTINUING it. One offensive language at two volumes, exactly as we described. If instead the bench reverts to generic spread pick-and-roll, the experiment died in the lab. Watch the first unit for polish. Watch the second for AMBITION.

SIGNAL THREE: THE WISEMAN TESTS GO PUBLIC

Now the courtroom's main defendant. Everything we've built in the Wiseman file — the coach's fandom, the private sessions, the convergence of four insiders — gets its first public cross-examination, and our three tests are finally observable from your couch. Test one, HEALTH, shows up where fatigue lives: second stints. Anyone can look bouncy in his first four minutes; watch the SECOND shift, the back-half-of-the-game legs, whether the explosion that anchored a national-team defense survives when the lungs start billing. Test two, DEFENSE, is audible if the broadcast mics catch it and visible if they don't: when a guard rejects a screen and the coverage shatters, does Wiseman POINT and TALK and recover to the rim — or does he freeze, a beat late, a rotation behind? In this scheme, the mouth is as important as the feet. And test three, THE ROLE, gets graded in the moments cameras usually skip: the bench demeanor during stretches he doesn't play, the sprint back to the huddle, the towel-waving minutes. A former No. 2 pick acting like a hungry fifteenth man is the tell that the pride-swallow is real. Pass all three in games that don't count, and the fourteenth seat is his before the games that do. Fail the first one... and suddenly every other center storyline on this list gets very loud.

SIGNAL FOUR: THE COUNTER-AUDITIONS — NORMAL VS. SCAR TISSUE

Because the bracket behind him is not standing still, and preseason is where the counter-cases get made. Drew Eubanks' audition strategy writes itself, and we named it in the wire episode: LOOK NORMAL. Three straight exhibitions of correct screens, on-time rolls, clean short-roll reads, and made floaters — boring, professional, trustworthy basketball — quietly reframes a competition everyone has already called. Watch his hands specifically: the thumb surgery is the asterisk on his file, and catches in traffic are where thumbs confess. And Tony Bradley's audition is even simpler, because the man was hired by a coach's scar tissue: can he inflict, in a Knicks jersey, the quiet plus-minus damage he twice inflicted ON Knicks jerseys? Screens that free shooters, boards ripped in traffic, ten honest minutes of rim presence — the exact job description Brown read aloud at the podium. Here's the viewing trick for both: ignore their points entirely. Count possessions that END well for New York while they're on the floor. That's the stat the staff is counting, and it's the one that survived two playoff series against us in Bradley's case. The ceiling play gets the headlines, Tunnel fam. But watch whether the floor and the seatbelt refuse to go quietly — because a close battle changes the entire trade calendar.

SIGNAL FIVE: THE AGGRESSION MISSION, QUANTIFIED

Signal five belongs to Tyler Kolek, and his audition has the clearest measurable in the whole guide. The kid diagnosed himself for us: finishing at a high level early last season, then drifting into passivity — and passivity, for a small guard, is professional poison. So here's the count to keep: TOUCHES VERSUS ATTACKS. Every time Kolek catches the ball in space, track what happens — does he turn the corner with intent to score, get two feet in the paint, force a help defender to commit? Or does he swing it side-to-side, a traffic cone with a handle, letting the defense rest? The Brunson apprenticeship gives him the blueprint — the floater, the mid-range craft, the small-man finishing package — and preseason gives him the stage with none of the rotation pressure. And never forget the double audience we described on his tightrope: Mike Brown is watching to decide his minutes... and twenty-nine other front offices are watching to price the trade chip. A paint-hunting, downhill Kolek either cracks this rotation or raises his own tag in the Diabaté-framework deals that keep circulating. A passive one does neither. Count the attacks, folks. The kid's two futures both hang on that number.

SIGNAL SIX: THE CLOSEOUT HINGE

Signal six is the single most specific skill-watch on this list: Mohamed Diawara versus the CLOSEOUT. We've called him the widest range of outcomes on the roster — trade chip or rotation revelation — and we've identified the exact hinge the whole fork swings on. Right now, defenders sprint at his corner catches with zero fear, because the worst outcome is a contested three. The upgrade that changes his career is ONE HARD DRIBBLE: catch, pump, attack the sprinting defender's hip, and either rise from the elbow or dump to the dunker. So when the 7'4" wingspan checks in — and with the regulars on managed minutes, he'll get real runway — watch nothing but his catches. Shot, drive, or swing? If preseason shows even three or four possessions of decisive closeout attacks, the wing rotation deepens, the starters' May legs get fresher, and the trade-chip conversations get quieter. If the catches keep dying on the perimeter... the pressure cooker we described gets hotter, because this franchise's internal-growth thesis — no blockbuster, improvement from within — runs directly through his development. No player on the roster can change his future with fewer dribbles. Literally one.

Watch his first transition sequence like it's a playoff possession, because someday it will be.
