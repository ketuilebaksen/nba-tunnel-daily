## WHERE WE WERE: THE ROUGH START

Let's remember how ugly the start was, because it makes the turnaround so much more impressive.

**The first six games:**
- Lost the opener at home to Philadelphia on ring night
- Lost on the road in Boston
- Lost at home to Orlando — 0–3, the first defending champ to start that way since the 2011–12 Mavericks
- Beat Detroit and Chicago back-to-back
- Lost again in Philadelphia

That 2–4 start raised real questions. Did this group still have the edge that won a championship? And the man at the center of it was the same man who carried them to the trophy last June.

## COMEBACK #1: CAPTAIN CLUTCH IS BACK

**Brunson's first six games:**
- 25.5 points and 5.2 assists per game — solid on the surface
- But just 39.5% from the field and 32.5% from three
- 2.2 turnovers per game
- And — the most surprising part — he came up short in several late-game moments

For a player who usually picks defenses apart with patience and footwork, who's normally the calmest person in the building when every possession counts, that was a clear sign that something was off. Remember the context: he had surgery on his left shooting wrist this offseason and spent time in a cast. Touch is often the last thing to come back.

**Brunson's last eight games (Knicks 6–2):**
- **28.1 points** per game
- **6.4 assists** per game
- **46.7%** from the field
- **41.8%** from three

That's the Brunson the Knicks know — and the version the rest of the league fears.

## THE CLUTCH NUMBERS

And here's where the turnaround really stands out. The NBA defines clutch time as the final five minutes of a game with the score within five points. According to NBA.com stats over this eight-game stretch, Brunson:
- **Leads every player in the league in clutch points** — with 32
- **Leads the league in clutch three-pointers** — with 5
- **Leads the league in clutch assists** — with 9

Think about that last one. Brunson isn't just taking over late — he's creating for others. And a lot of those clutch assists have gone to Karl-Anthony Towns. That connection is a huge reason the Knicks have been so tough late. Defenses that load up on Brunson in the final minutes now have to deal with KAT. And when they stay home on KAT, Brunson makes them pay himself.

Remember the debate a couple of weeks ago — should KAT take over clutch possessions while Brunson's shot recovered? The answer turned out to be: both. The Brunson-KAT two-man game is the Knicks' closing weapon.

## THE TORONTO MOMENT

The best example came Sunday in Toronto. The Knicks and Raptors needed overtime. With just over 30 seconds left in the extra period, Brunson found himself in the corner with the ball. He let it fly. Bottom. A three-point lead. The Knicks held on to win 112–108.

Two nights later, he hit another big three with 1:25 left to help put Toronto away again.

It's the kind of shot Brunson has made a hundred times. And it was a clear signal: the slow start is behind him.

## WHY BRUNSON'S RESPONSE MATTERS

Here's what stands out about how he climbed out of it. He didn't change his style. He didn't force anything. He kept attacking, kept trusting his teammates, and waited for his shot to come back.

And this season adds a new layer of pressure. Every opponent circles the Knicks on the schedule. Every arena brings extra energy when the champs come to town. Brunson isn't just a star chasing a title anymore. He's the face of the defending champions, and every slump gets magnified.

A championship team can survive a few early losses, but it can't let a bad start turn into a bad season. Brunson's recovery came at exactly the right moment. Instead of sliding down the East standings, the Knicks have stabilized.

When the game is on the line, the Knicks still have the man they want with the ball in his hands. That hasn't changed, and it isn't going to.

## COMEBACK #2: LANDRY SHAMET'S UNLIKELY RISE

Of course, Brunson can't do it alone. And one of the biggest surprises of the season has come from a player few people expected to be in this conversation.

**A year ago:** Shamet barely made the Knicks roster. He landed the final spot, and there's a good chance that only happened because Malcolm Brogdon suddenly retired.

**Today:** In his third season in New York, Shamet has gone from a borderline rotation guard to an essential sixth man and one of the most trusted closers on the defending champions.

**The numbers through 14 games:**
- **10.4 points** in **25.7 minutes** per game
- **48.5%** from the field
- **43.5%** from three

For a guard whose main job is to space the floor and knock down open looks, that's excellent.

## THE ON/OFF NUMBERS

But the real impact shows up in the on-off data. According to NBA.com lineup stats:
- **With Shamet on the floor,** the Knicks outscore opponents by **16.6 points per 100 possessions** — the second-best mark on the team, behind only Jordan Clarkson.
- **With Shamet on the bench,** the Knicks are outscored by **7.5 points per 100 possessions** — the worst mark on the roster.

In simple terms, the Knicks have been a much better team with him than without him.

That's why there's a real argument that Shamet has been the Knicks' second-best player so far this season. Karl-Anthony Towns is clearly first, and by a wide margin. Saying Shamet is next isn't an insult to Brunson or OG Anunoby. It's just a reflection of how much he's meant during an uneven start.

## MIKE BROWN'S TRUST

Brown has noticed. Shamet ranks:
- **Fifth** on the team in minutes per game
- **Sixth** in first-quarter minutes
- **Fifth** in clutch minutes

The coach trusts him when it matters most. And Brown has told reporters that it's hard to find minutes for Mikal Bridges and Josh Hart while Shamet is playing at this level. That's a remarkable statement about a player who was fighting for a roster spot a year ago.

**The lineup that works:** The group of Brunson, Shamet, Bridges, OG, and Towns has posted a net rating of plus-25.6 across 52 minutes together. Small sample — but dominant. It shows how well Shamet fits next to the stars.

**The Toronto sweep:** In the two wins over the Raptors, Shamet scored a combined 35 points and went six-of-11 from three. In both games, he hit important shots down the stretch. And on Tuesday, he finished with a game-high plus-26.

Yahoo's Dan Devine had the perfect line for it: Shamet is like a $20 bill that Mike Brown keeps finding in his jacket pocket.

## SIXTH MAN OF THE YEAR?

Shamet's game doesn't look like a typical Sixth Man of the Year candidate. He's not a high-volume scorer who creates his own shot every trip. But he fits the spirit of the award: he comes off the bench, makes an immediate impact, and becomes very hard to take off the floor in close games.

The betting market hasn't caught up yet. His odds sit at plus-5,000 on DraftKings and plus-6,000 on FanDuel. Those numbers can move fast if he keeps this up. He deserves early consideration.

## THE UNDERRATED DEFENSE

And here's what often goes unnoticed. Shamet isn't a lockdown stopper, and he can struggle against bigger, stronger star scorers. But he might be the Knicks' best point-of-attack defender against quick guards. He fights through screens, slides his feet, defends with his chest, and contests well. Most importantly, his effort never drops.

Remember when Mike Brown called out Shamet and Deuce McBride for their point-of-attack defense after the Bucks blowout? This is why.

## CAN THE SHOOTING LAST?

The natural question. History says yes:
- He shot above **40%** from three over his first three NBA seasons
- He hit **39%** over the previous two
- He's healthy, comfortable in his role, and — for the first time in his career — playing a third straight season with the same team

That kind of stability makes a real difference for a shooter. Through an up-and-down start, with the starting lineup underwhelming at times, Shamet has been one of the few constants — a player Brown can count on every single night.

## COMEBACK #3: MIKAL BRIDGES BREAKS THROUGH

And every minute Shamet earns has to come from somewhere. The player most affected? Mikal Bridges.

**The early numbers were hard to look at.** Heading into Tuesday, Bridges was averaging just 6.8 points per game on 37.5% shooting and 27.5% from three. For a player who's been a reliable two-way contributor for years, that was well below expectations.

**There were flashes.** In Friday's loss in Indiana, he scored a season-high 18.

**Then came Sunday.** Two nights later, in the overtime win in Toronto, he had just five points. At one point, Jose Alvarado was seen consoling Bridges on the bench. That moment showed how much the slump was weighing on him.

## THE GAME THAT SHOWED HIS VALUE

But that same Sunday game also showed why Bridges matters, even when his shot isn't falling.

**In the fourth quarter,** with the Knicks fighting to stay alive, Bridges:
- Grabbed four rebounds
- Blocked a shot
- Came up with a steal

He scored only two points in the period, but his work on the other end helped force overtime. He spent much of the night guarding Jamal Shead and Immanuel Quickley — two quick guards who can change a game in a hurry.

**In overtime,** he didn't score, but he pulled down two more rebounds.

**At the final buzzer,** Bridges led the Knicks with a plus-15.

Five points on the stat sheet. A huge impact on the result. That's winning basketball.

## THE BREAKTHROUGH

Then came Tuesday. Back in Toronto, Bridges finally looked like himself on offense:
- **17 points** on **7-of-11** shooting
- **2-of-5** from three
- **5 rebounds** and **1 assist** in **30 minutes**

He opened his night with a smooth fadeaway jumper — and closed it with a dagger. With 36 seconds left, Bridges knocked down a three that pushed the lead to 111–102, which ended up being the final score.

That was his 13th three of the season, his fourth double-figure game this year, and his second this month. Not huge milestones — but for a player climbing out of a slump, every confident shot counts.

## THE BODY LANGUAGE

And just as important was how he looked doing it. Bridges was decisive. He caught the ball and went straight into his move instead of hesitating. He attacked closeouts, trusted his jumper, and played with the rhythm that had been missing for most of the first month.

Remember what Mike Brown asked of him: lean into his strengths — running, cutting, rebounding, driving. Stop forcing things. On Tuesday, he looked like a player who'd finally stopped thinking and started playing.

For a player whose confidence clearly took a hit, that body language might matter more than the box score.

## WHY THE KNICKS SHOULDN'T GIVE UP ON HIM

Some fans have already started wondering about Bridges' future. We've covered the trade ideas — OKC, the Post's "escape hatch" column, the Clippers proposal. But that conversation is coming far too early.

**His value isn't just scoring.** His defense, his rebounding, and his ability to guard multiple positions are a big part of what makes the Knicks so tough when everything clicks. Sunday proved it.

**But the Knicks do need more offense from him.** With Brunson and Towns drawing so much attention, Bridges will get open looks. Turning those into points makes the Knicks far more dangerous — especially in the playoffs — and takes pressure off Brunson late.

**And it's early.** Fourteen games. 8–6. Six wins in eight. Overreacting to a few weeks of basketball rarely leads to good decisions. Bridges deserves the benefit of the doubt. He's earned it with years of steady, dependable play.

## THE MINUTES PUZZLE

Now here's the challenge for Mike Brown. If Brunson's back, Shamet's playing like a top-three Knick, and Bridges is starting to find himself — where do all the minutes go?

**The guard and wing pool:** Brunson, Shamet, Bridges, Hart, OG, Deuce McBride, Jordan Clarkson, Tyler Kolek, and Jose Alvarado. That's nine players for roughly five positions' worth of guard and wing minutes.

**The pressure point:** Brown said it himself — it's hard to find minutes for Bridges and Hart while Shamet is playing at this level. That's the core tension. Two of the starters from a championship team are being squeezed by a guy who was the final roster cut a year ago.

**How I'd handle it — my analysis:**
- **Keep the starting five as is.** Brunson, Hart, Bridges, OG, and KAT. Brown has said a lineup change this early would be a panic move, and the Brunson-Shamet-Bridges-OG-KAT group has crushed teams in its minutes anyway.
- **Make Shamet the first wing off the bench, every night.** And let him close games when he's hot.
- **Let the fourth quarter be a meritocracy.** Whoever's playing best finishes. That's been Brown's approach all season — and it's working.
- **Rotate the backup point guard by matchup.** Kolek for slower, half-court games; Alvarado for faster, high-energy ones, like Tuesday in Toronto.

It's a lot to juggle. But a deep rotation is a luxury, not a problem — especially over 82 games and a long playoff run.
