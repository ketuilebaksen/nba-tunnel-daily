## THE HONEST PART FIRST

Let me be clear from the jump, because that's how this channel works. Kevin McCullar Jr. has NOT been cut. Nobody has reported that the Knicks are about to release him. He re-signed on a two-way deal in late September, and he's still in camp competing. What this video breaks down is pressure — real, visible pressure — and why, out of every young player in this building, he's the one whose future is most on the line between now and opening night on October 20th. If you've seen headlines this week saying "Knicks cut young player," this is the name those headlines are circling around. So let's look at the facts.

## CHAPTER ONE: HOW THE KNICKS FELL FOR McCULLAR

To understand why this matters, you have to understand how much the Knicks invested in this kid. McCullar came out of college with a reputation as a do-everything wing — the kind of player who fills the box score without needing plays called for him. He could score a little, pass a little, defend multiple positions, and crash the glass from the wing. The Knicks loved that profile so much that two years ago, they traded the draft rights to Oso Ighodaro to get him.

And this is where the story gets painful. Ighodaro has since built a solid role in Phoenix. Last season, he finished fourth on the Suns in minutes played — and first among their big men. Think about that for a second. This summer, the Knicks lost Mitchell Robinson to Boston. They spent the whole camp searching for a big man. They brought in Wiseman, Eubanks, and Bradley on non-guaranteed deals hoping one of them would stick. And the big man they gave away to get McCullar is now a rotation center for another team.

That doesn't mean the trade was a disaster. Nobody knew in 2024 that Mitch would leave two summers later. But it does mean that every time McCullar struggles, fans look at Ighodaro's minutes in Phoenix and ask: what if? And that "what if" is part of the pressure on him right now. He's not just fighting for a spot. He's fighting to justify a decision.

## CHAPTER TWO: THREE CAMPS, SAME QUESTION

This is McCullar's third training camp with the Knicks. And here's the thing about third camps: they're different from the first two.

In camp one, you're a project. Nobody expects much. You learn the system, you work in Westchester, and everyone says, "Give him time." In camp two, you're developing. Coaches look for flashes, and if you show a few, the organization says, "He's getting there." But in camp three, the question changes. It's not "Is he developing?" anymore. It's "Is he ready?" And if the answer isn't clearly yes, teams start looking elsewhere.

McCullar is 25 years old. In normal life, that's young. But for a fringe NBA player still trying to secure a place in the league, it's not young anymore. He's already older than six players on the current Knicks roster. And with every year that goes by, the "maybes" around his development start turning into "maybe nots." The Knicks clearly still believe in him — the fact that they brought him back for a third camp proves it. But belief has an expiration date in the NBA, and a defending champion's clock runs faster than anyone else's.

## CHAPTER THREE: THE SPEECH

Now let's go back to Wednesday. After a practice he didn't like, Mike Brown addressed the whole training camp. His message was simple: the starters are going to play. Everyone else in the gym is fighting — either to make the team or to earn minutes. And then the line that echoed around the league: none of this has anything to do with last season's championship roster. Every day is a competition.

For some players, that's just a reminder. For a veteran like Bruce Brown, who's been through camps and contracts and trades, it's motivation. But for McCullar, it sounds a lot more like a warning. Because he WAS part of last season's championship organization. And the coach just told him, publicly, that it doesn't protect him.

And here's what I think people are missing about that speech. Mike Brown didn't name anybody. He didn't have to. Everybody in that gym knows exactly who's on the bubble. When the head coach of a champion says "this has nothing to do with last season," the players who were around last season and haven't proven themselves yet are the ones who feel it most.

## CHAPTER FOUR: THE VETERAN IN THE WAY

And now meet the problem. The Knicks brought in a veteran with almost the exact same profile as McCullar. His name is Bruce Brown.

Brown has eight years in the NBA. He was a key piece on Denver's 2023 championship team, and that run earned him his first major contract with Indiana. From there, he became a valuable trade piece — Indiana sent him to Toronto in the Pascal Siakam deal, and Toronto later moved him to New Orleans in the Brandon Ingram trade. In total, he's earned more than $60 million in his career. And now he's in New York on a non-guaranteed Exhibit 9 deal, fighting for a job just like McCullar.

Here's the technical part. Brown and McCullar aren't actually competing for the same contract. Brown has too many years of service to be eligible for a two-way deal. But in practical terms, they want the exact same thing: a place in the wing rotation, and eventually a standard roster spot. And the coach has room for one of them, not both.

So let's compare them side by side, like a scout would.

**Position and role:** Both are versatile wings who can score, pass, defend, and rebound. In many ways, McCullar plays like a smaller version of Josh Hart. So does Brown. That's the problem — they fill the same box on the depth chart.

**Point-of-attack defense:** Advantage Brown. According to BBall Index, Brown rated as the second-best screen navigator last season among the players now on the Knicks roster, behind only Jose Alvarado. He makes himself skinny through screens, recovers with long strides, and stays attached to ball-handlers without fouling. That's a playoff skill.

**Ball-handling:** Advantage Brown. He's shown he can run the point in short stretches, which gives Mike Brown emergency backup point guard minutes.

**Upside:** Advantage McCullar. He's younger, he's still improving, and much of what the Knicks like about him is potential. Brown is what he is — a known quantity.

**Championship experience:** Advantage Brown. He's done it in a real rotation on a title team.

**Cost and flexibility:** Here's where it gets interesting — and it's actually a double-edged sword for McCullar. We'll get to that in a second.

Add it up, and you see the problem. A team trying to climb the standings can afford to gamble on a high ceiling. A defending champion usually prefers a known quantity. Hart — the player both of them resemble — has nothing left to prove. McCullar might still grow into that role. But can a team chasing back-to-back titles afford to give him the time and the minutes to find out?

## CHAPTER FIVE: THE PRESEASON SCORECARD

Let's look at what the two games actually showed.

**Game one in Philadelphia:** McCullar did a bit of everything — six points, four rebounds, an assist, and a block in about ten minutes. But he missed all three of his three-point attempts. Bruce Brown struggled even more that night: two points, a turnover, and four fouls in roughly ten minutes. If the battle had ended there, McCullar would've been ahead.

**Game two against Washington:** Through the first three quarters, neither McCullar nor Brown got into the game. Neither did Ochai Agbaji or Jaden Akins — two more players chasing a standard roster spot. Instead, Mike Brown leaned on established reserves. Landry Shamet hit three of his four threes and scored 13 points in 16 minutes. Jordan Clarkson added seven in nine minutes. Alvarado and Deuce McBride also got rotation time. And when Bruce Brown finally got his chance, he showed exactly the point-of-attack defense that makes coaches trust him.

That second game is the scary one for McCullar. Not because he played badly — but because the depth chart around him is so crowded that the bubble guys didn't even see the floor until the fourth quarter of a close game. When Shamet, Clarkson, Alvarado, and Deuce are all ahead of you in the wing and guard rotation, the margin for error is basically zero.

## CHAPTER SIX: THE TWO-WAY TRAP

Now the cruel part of this story — the contract math.

McCullar is on a two-way contract. Those deals are non-guaranteed, and teams are free to swap players in and out at any point during the season. That means cutting him would cause the Knicks very little financial pain. Compare that to Pacôme Dadiet, whose salary of about $2.98 million is guaranteed — releasing him wouldn't save anything, which is why the Dadiet conversation is about trades. With McCullar, there's no money stopping the decision. Only belief.

And here's the double-edged sword I mentioned. On one hand, the two-way deal is a blessing for McCullar: it doesn't count like a standard contract, so he doesn't take one of the 15 roster spots, and he doesn't add pressure to the second apron line that's shaping every decision this front office makes, including the Karl-Anthony Towns negotiation. That makes him easy to KEEP.

On the other hand, that exact same flexibility makes him easy to REPLACE. If the Knicks see a younger prospect on the waiver wire after cut day, or a G League player tearing it up in Westchester, they can swap him in with almost no cost. Right now, the Knicks' two-way spots belong to McCullar, Nickel, and Akins. If McCullar doesn't clearly outplay the competition, the Knicks have every reason to explore other options for that slot.

So the question isn't really "Will the Knicks cut McCullar to save money?" It's "Will the Knicks find someone they believe in more?" And that's a much harder question for a player to control.

## CHAPTER SEVEN: THE CASE FOR KEEPING HIM

Now, I promised you both sides. Here's the honest case FOR McCullar — and it's stronger than people think.

**One: the investment.** The Knicks traded real value to get him. They've spent three years developing him. The fact that he's entering his third training camp with this team tells you they still believe there's more to come. Front offices don't like walking away from investments right before they pay off.

**Two: the two-way flexibility works both ways.** Because he's on a two-way, keeping him costs almost nothing. He can play in Westchester, stay ready, and be called up when injuries hit. And over an 82-game season, injuries WILL hit. Every champion needs a 16th or 17th man who knows the system and won't embarrass you in a random Tuesday game in January.

**Three: the Hart insurance.** McCullar's profile — rebounding wing, multiple positions, connective passing — is the closest thing on the roster to a Josh Hart backup. If Hart misses time, the Knicks need someone who can do Hart-like things. Bruce Brown can do some of them. McCullar might do more of them, especially on the glass.

**Four: the long-term math.** Remember Josh Hart's $22 million team option, and the conversations about KAT taking a discount and Hart taking a discount to keep Deuce. This roster is going to get squeezed hard in the next couple of years. The Knicks NEED cheap young players who can contribute. If McCullar develops even into a solid backup, he becomes exactly that type of player — a minimum-salary contributor on a team pressed against the apron. Cutting him now could mean losing that option forever.

**Five: development curves aren't straight lines.** Plenty of NBA players figured it out at 25, 26, even 27. Some of the best role players in the league were "maybe nots" until suddenly they weren't. One good stretch in Westchester, one hot shooting month, and the conversation flips.

## CHAPTER EIGHT: THE CHECKLIST — HOW HE SAVES HIS SPOT

So what does McCullar need to do in the last three preseason games? Minnesota at the Garden on Monday, then Toronto twice — at home and on the road. Here's the NBA Tunnel checklist.

**Item one: make an open three.** Zero-of-three in the opener is the number everybody remembers. He doesn't need to become a sniper. He needs to make enough that defenses can't sag off him and clog Brunson's driving lanes. Two made threes in these games would change the conversation.

**Item two: win a defensive possession on camera.** Bruce Brown's whole case is point-of-attack defense. McCullar needs at least one or two moments where he stays in front of a quick guard, fights through a screen, or blows up a play. Coaches remember those clips.

**Item three: own the glass.** This is his clearest edge over Brown. If he grabs tough rebounds in traffic — especially offensive rebounds — he shows the Hart-like skill that nobody else on the bubble brings.

**Item four: zero mistakes.** In a battle this tight, careless turnovers and silly fouls are fatal. Bruce Brown's four fouls in Philadelphia hurt him. McCullar can't give the coaches the same reason to doubt him.

**Item five: be ready in garbage time.** On Thursday, the bubble guys didn't play until the fourth quarter. That means McCullar might only get six or eight minutes. He has to treat every one of those minutes like a playoff possession. That's not fair — but nothing about cut day is fair.

## CHAPTER NINE: WHAT HAPPENS IF THE KNICKS DO MOVE ON

Let's be real about the worst-case scenario too. If the Knicks decide to replace McCullar on his two-way deal, what happens to him?

He wouldn't be out of basketball. A 25-year-old wing who can defend, rebound, and pass is exactly the type of player rebuilding teams look for on the waiver wire or in the G League. A change of scenery could even help him — more minutes, lower expectations, and a coach who builds a role around him. Sometimes a player needs to leave a contender to finally get the reps he needs.

And for the Knicks, the decision would say something important about this front office's mindset. It would say: we're not developing anymore. We're defending. Once upon a time, turning a second-round pick into a rotation player was a big win for this franchise. Now, the only win that matters is another championship in June.

## MY CALL

Here's my prediction. I think McCullar keeps his two-way spot for opening night. The Knicks have invested too much to walk away before the season even starts, and the two-way deal costs them almost nothing. But I also think Bruce Brown wins the bigger battle — the rotation minutes and, if a spot opens up, the standard contract. That means McCullar's real test isn't October. It's November and December. If he's buried in Westchester and someone better becomes available, that's when the move could come.

So no — I don't think this is the end. But I do think this is the most important ten days of Kevin McCullar Jr.'s Knicks career.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

Here's where it stands. Kevin McCullar Jr. hasn't been cut, but he's the young Knick under the most pressure in camp. The Knicks traded Oso Ighodaro's draft rights to get him, and Ighodaro is now a rotation big in Phoenix while New York searches for size. He's 25, in his third camp, older than six players on the roster, and he heard Mike Brown say last season's ring means nothing. Bruce Brown — a champion, a screen-fighting specialist, and a backup point guard — is standing in his way. And the two-way contract that makes him easy to keep also makes him easy to replace. Three preseason games left. Ten days to opening night. His future is in his own hands.
