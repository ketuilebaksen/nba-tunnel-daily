## THE GOODBYE CHECK

Let's handle the headline first, the NBA Tunnel way. You might see videos titled something like "Two Knicks players saying goodbye after tough loss." Here are the facts:

**Josh Hart is not leaving.** He talked about fatigue after a loss. That's it. No trade request, no injury, no departure. He's a starter, the heart of the team, and he'll be on the floor Monday against Brooklyn.

**Pacôme Dadiet is not leaving either, at least not yet.** The Knicks declined his fourth-year option for 2027–28. That means he becomes an unrestricted free agent next summer. But he's still under contract for this season, and he's still on the roster today.

So nobody is saying goodbye. One player made an honest comment, and another got a contract decision about a season that's still a year away. Fair to discuss? Absolutely. "Big blow"? No. Let's talk about what really happened.

## WHAT JOSH HART ACTUALLY SAID

Here are Hart's words after the Philly loss, as reported:

"It's a new year. The flow's tougher. The ball gets stagnant, and I think that's a little bit from fatigue."

"We all feel the fatigue. We have to fight through it."

"It's six games in. We have to try and find a way to get our bodies ready to play every day."

He also said the team is ready mentally, and now the bodies need to catch up.

Read those quotes carefully. He isn't making excuses. He's diagnosing a problem and saying the team has to fight through it. That's very Josh Hart: honest, direct, and accountable. And he connected it to something we've all seen. When guys are tired, the offense stops moving. The ball sticks, the cuts get lazy, and possessions die.

## BRUNSON'S RESPONSE

And then there was the captain. When reporters asked Jalen Brunson about Hart's fatigue comments, he said:

"Josh said he feels the fatigue? Well, Josh is old as [bleep], so..."

That's pure Brunson-and-Hart. These two have been best friends since their Villanova days. They roast each other constantly, and this was a joke, not a public callout. Some headlines tried to frame it as Brunson "calling out" Hart. It wasn't. It was a captain lightening the mood after a tough night.

But there's something smart hidden in that joke. Brunson didn't really deny that the team is tired. He just refused to make it a storyline. That's what leaders do: deflect, laugh, and get back to work. For the record, Hart is 31 and Brunson is 30, so Brunson isn't exactly a spring chicken either.

## THE EVIDENCE: IS THIS TEAM REALLY TIRED?

Let's look at the actual evidence. What points to fatigue?

**One: the shortest offseason in the league.** The Knicks played into late June. They won the championship, celebrated a title, had a parade, and then went right back to work. Every champion deals with this. Teams that go deep in the playoffs get a shorter summer, less recovery time, and less time to rebuild their bodies.

**Two: surgery and rehab.** Brunson had surgery on his left wrist in July. He spent part of his summer rehabbing instead of training normally. That affects rhythm and conditioning.

**Three: the schedule.** Look at this week alone. Tuesday at home against Detroit, Wednesday in Chicago on the second night of a back-to-back, and then Friday in Philadelphia. That's three games in four nights. The Chicago game included a 19-point comeback, which takes a lot out of a team.

**Four: the way the league plays now.** We talked about this in the Brunson contract video. The NBA is averaging the most possessions per game and the shortest possessions of the tracking era. Defenders are picking up ball handlers farther from the basket than ever. That's more running and more physical stress, especially for primary creators.

**Five: the miles on these bodies.** Since Brunson arrived in New York, only Mikal Bridges and Anthony Edwards have logged more combined regular-season and playoff minutes than he has. Bridges played every game for years. Hart has always been a heavy-minutes guy. These are high-mileage players.

## THE EVIDENCE AGAINST: "IT'S NOT JUST FATIGUE"

Now let's be fair, because fatigue can't explain everything.

**The slow starts happen when the legs are fresh.** The Knicks trailed 29–21 after the first quarter in Philly. They scored just 21 points on 41 percent shooting, with zero free throws in the first quarter, and they were down double digits after 10 minutes. You're not tired in the first quarter. That's focus, execution, and energy.

**The turnovers.** The Knicks committed 13 turnovers, including eight in the first half. Some of that is tired legs. Some of it is sloppy decision-making.

**The stagnant offense.** The first half ended with a 22-second possession that didn't even produce a shot. The buzzer sounded with the ball in Brunson's hands. That's not just fatigue. That's a lack of movement and a lack of a plan.

**Philly isn't fresh either.** The Sixers have played six games too, and they're 5–1.

So my read is this: fatigue is a real factor, but it's not the whole story. It makes everything else worse.

## WHAT THE FATIGUE LOOKED LIKE ON THE FLOOR

Let's connect Hart's words to Friday's box score.

**Hart himself:** Seven points on two-of-nine shooting, six rebounds, three assists, and a steal in about 30 minutes. For a player who usually fills up the stat sheet with energy, that's a quiet night. When Hart says "we all feel it," he's including himself.

**Brunson late:** He scored 24 with six assists, but on eight-of-22 shooting. After hitting the go-ahead corner three with 1:32 left, he went one-for-five in the final stretch and missed a key free throw with 32 seconds to go. Two nights after 38 in Chicago, the legs weren't the same late.

**The closing minutes:** LeBron James hit a fadeaway over OG Anunoby with 1:10 left, and Maxey threw him an alley-oop down the stretch. The Knicks couldn't get stops when they needed them most. Tired legs show up on defense first.

**KAT, the exception:** Twenty-two points and 12 rebounds on eight-of-12 shooting, with a wrist brace. If anyone has a right to feel tired, it's the guy playing through a wrist injury. He's still producing.

## WHAT MIKE BROWN CAN DO

The good news is that Mike Brown already has a tool for this, and we talked about it a couple of videos ago: he spreads the minutes.

In Chicago, 10 different players logged at least 13 minutes. Bridges played a career-low 13 minutes. The starting five shared the floor for only 14 of 103 possessions. That kind of rotation is exactly how you manage fatigue across an 82-game season. Nobody has to play 40 minutes when the bench is producing.

Here's what I'd watch for, and this is my analysis, not reporting:

- **More strategic rest.** Not full games off, but shorter stints and staggered minutes for the high-mileage guys.
- **More trust in the young players.** Kolek, Diawara, and even Wiseman getting minutes in the right spots.
- **Lighter practices.** Recovery days instead of hard practices during dense stretches of the schedule.
- **Faster pace in short bursts.** Push early in possessions while the legs are fresh, then use set plays later in the shot clock.

The real question is balance. The Knicks are 2–4, and every win matters. But managing bodies now is how you have fresh legs in April and May.

## THREE KNICKS WHO NEED THE REST MOST

If I'm the Knicks' training staff, these are the three players I'm watching closest over the next month. This is my analysis, not inside information.

**Jalen Brunson.** He's the engine of the offense, a 30-year-old smaller guard who drives into contact on almost every possession, and he's coming off July wrist surgery. He also carries the heaviest creative load on the team. Friday showed what happens when his legs fade late: one-for-five in the final stretch and a missed free throw with 32 seconds left. Protecting Brunson's legs is protecting the season.

**Josh Hart.** He's 31, plays bigger than his size, crashes the glass against centers, and guards everyone from point guards to forwards. That style is why he's so valuable, and also why it wears on him. When the guy who never complains says he feels it, you listen.

**Mikal Bridges.** For years, he was the NBA's iron man, playing every single game. That's incredible durability, but it's also a lot of mileage. His slow start, including a zero-for-eight night in Boston and a career-low 13 minutes in Chicago, could be partly about rhythm and partly about legs. A lighter load early could help him find himself.

The good news is that the bench is deep enough to cover for all three. Clarkson, Kolek, Shamet, Deuce, and Drummond have all had winning nights. That's the advantage Mike Brown has built: he doesn't have to run his stars into the ground in November.

## THE DADIET CHAPTER — THE SHORT VERSION

Now to the second story. We did a full video on this decision, so here's the short version, with a few new details.

**The decision:** The Knicks officially declined Pacôme Dadiet's fourth-year team option, worth $5.37 million for 2027–28. That makes him an unrestricted free agent next summer.

**How he got here:** The Knicks took him 25th overall in 2024, and the pick raised some eyebrows. He wasn't widely projected as a first-rounder, and plenty of people wondered why New York would use the pick on a player who clearly needed time. A big part of the answer was money. He signed for 80 percent of the rookie scale for the 25th pick, a four-year deal worth a little over $13 million, with about $4.7 million guaranteed over his first two seasons. It was a first-round prospect at a discount, during a time when the aprons were making every dollar matter.

**The production:** In 48 regular-season appearances, he's averaged 1.6 points and one rebound per game, shooting 32.9 percent from the field and 25.5 percent from three. Those are hard numbers to earn minutes with on a championship team.

**The age:** He turned 21 just over three months ago. That's important. Players develop on different timelines. Plenty of wings figure it out at 22, 23, or 24.

## WHAT'S NEXT FOR DADIET

There's been speculation that the Knicks might have to attach second-round picks just to trade him. We haven't seen a national insider confirm that, so take it as speculation, not reporting. One Knicks writer has also suggested that a standard NBA contract elsewhere next summer could be hard to find, and that a training camp deal might be his realistic path.

Here's the thing to remember, which we covered in detail last time: declining the option effectively turns his contract into an expiring deal. That can make him easier to include in a trade as salary filler after December 15th. And if the Knicks ever wanted him back, the rules allow it, as long as his first-year salary doesn't exceed the declined option amount.

## THE BIGGER LESSON: WINNING NOW VS. BUILDING FOR LATER

This is where the two stories meet, and it's the most interesting conversation of the week.

On one side, the Knicks have fatigue. They have high-mileage stars, a short offseason, and the toll of a championship run. That's the cost of winning now.

On the other side, they have Dadiet, a young player who never broke through, and a front office that rarely keeps first-round picks. That's the cost of building for later, or rather, choosing not to.

When you put them together, a fair question comes up: does this team have enough young, cheap, fresh legs to survive an 82-game grind? Here's the honest answer: more than you think.

- **Tyler Kolek,** 25, a second-round pick, has 21 assists and zero turnovers over three games.
- **Mohamed Diawara,** another second-rounder on a four-year deal.
- **Dalton Knecht,** 25, getting reps in Westchester.
- **James Wiseman,** 25, waiting for his next chance.

The Knicks' version of "young talent" doesn't come from the lottery or the first round. It comes from second-round picks, two-way contracts, and buy-low veterans. Dadiet didn't work out, but the system around him has. Still, the fatigue talk is a reminder: someday this core will need a younger generation behind it. Winning a title doesn't mean you stop thinking about the future.

## THE HONESTY QUESTION: SHOULD HART HAVE SAID IT?

Some fans are frustrated. "You're the defending champs. Don't say you're tired in October." I get that. Saying "we're tired" can sound like an excuse, and opponents hear it too.

But here's my take. I'd rather have a player who tells the truth than one who pretends everything is fine. Hart didn't say "we're tired, so it's okay to lose." He said "we have to fight through it" and "we have to find a way to get our bodies ready." That's accountability. Recognizing a problem is the first step toward fixing it.

And it's very on brand. Hart has always been the most honest voice in that locker room. That honesty is part of why this team won a championship. Nobody hides. Nobody pretends. They fix it.

## MONDAY: THE NETS — A CHANCE TO RESET

Next up, the winless Brooklyn Nets come to the Garden on Monday. They're 0–5 after a 110–94 loss to Detroit on Friday. Between Friday and Monday, the Knicks get two full days with no games. That's real recovery time, maybe the most rest they've had since opening night.

So Monday is a great test of Hart's theory. If fatigue was a big part of Friday's problems, we should see a sharper team: a better start, more ball movement, and fewer turnovers. If the same problems show up after two days of rest, then it's not just the legs.

Here's what I'm watching:

- **The first quarter.** Three straight slow starts. Do the Knicks finally come out sharp?
- **Hart's energy.** He usually bounces back with a big game after a quiet one.
- **Ball movement.** Fewer stagnant possessions and more assists.
- **KAT's wrist brace.** Is it still on, and does it matter?
- **Alvarado.** A Brooklyn native returning against the Nets would be a nice storyline, but there's still no update.

## MY CALL

Here's my take. Nobody is saying goodbye. Hart is the same leader he's always been, and Brunson's joke tells you the locker room is fine. Fatigue is real, but it's not the main problem. Focus and execution are, especially early in games. After two days of rest, the Knicks beat the Nets on Monday and get to 3–4. Hart has a bounce-back night with a double-double. And by the end of November, as the schedule gets kinder and Brown keeps managing minutes, people will stop talking about fatigue. On Dadiet, my call from the last video stands: he's moved as salary filler before the deadline.
