THE $77 MILLION GAP: THE NUMBERS FINALLY SURFACE

And for the first time, we have actual numbers to put on the stall. Start with where KAT stands. He earns just over $57 million this season. He holds the $61 million player option for next season. And under his current contract, he's scheduled to hit unrestricted free agency in 2028. He's eligible for an extension worth up to four years and $277 MILLION — the full max.

Now the Knicks' side. According to the reporting, New York has put a four-year, $200 MILLION offer on the table. Do the subtraction with me: that's a gap of roughly $77 million. Break it down by year and it gets even clearer. Two hundred million over four years averages $50 million a season. Two hundred seventy-seven million averages about $69 million a season. So the Knicks are offering about $19 million a year less than the max — and that lines up exactly with the reporting we covered in the Curry video, when we learned New York wanted a discount extension starting somewhere in the $45-to-$50 million range. That wasn't a rumor, folks. That was the opening bid. And per Bondy's reporting, Towns doesn't seem interested in entertaining it.

Put a $50 million average next to the $57 million he's making right now and the $61 million option he could simply pick up, and you understand the tension instantly. From KAT's chair, the Knicks aren't just asking for a discount on the max — they're asking him to take LESS per year than he's already earning, after the best playoff run of his life. That's the emotional core of the profane quote. Imagine winning employee of the year and being offered a pay cut. That's how this feels from his side of the table.

WHY THE FRONT OFFICE IS HOLDING THE LINE

So why won't Leon Rose move? Because of the same three words that have shaped every decision this franchise has made since June: the second apron. The front office is treating that threshold almost like a death sentence — the goal isn't just to stay below it this year, it's to stay below it for SEVERAL years, because the alternative is losing trade flexibility, losing exceptions, and watching your draft future get frozen. And here's the part the casual fan misses: Towns is not the only big contract on the horizon. Josh Hart's deal is coming due. The Brunson and OG decisions come after that. Mikal Bridges' deal has its own end date down the road. Line up the calendar and you see what Leon sees: a championship core where EVERY major piece eventually needs to be paid, under an apron that won't let everyone get market value.

That's the math nobody can escape. If Leon gives Towns the full max, the money has to come from somewhere — and "somewhere" means Hart, or the bench, or a future Brunson negotiation. Remember Josh Hart's media day quote about Mitchell Robinson: the reason nobody has repeated as champion in eight years is the second apron and that CBA. The front office isn't being cheap with KAT. It's trying to keep the core that won the title together for four or five more years, and somebody has to accept less than the maximum for that to work. Brunson already did, years ago, and built the culture this team stands on. The question the front office is asking Towns, basically, is: will you be the next one?

BOTH SIDES ARE RIGHT — AND THE LANDING ZONE

Here's the honest truth, and I think it's the most important thing to understand about this negotiation: both sides have a legitimate point. Fans and analysts can easily say Towns should take less for the good of the team. It's easy to spend another person's money. But almost anybody in his position would want to be paid fairly after delivering a title. Think about what his playoff run actually did: a player questioned for years in big moments flipped his reputation completely. He combined elite shooting efficiency with real playmaking. He played the best defense of his career. As we laid out with the Wembanyama numbers, he was the one center in the entire postseason who dragged the best rim protector alive away from the rim. It's hard to find another playoff run in recent memory that changed a player's legacy so dramatically. He has every right to want that reflected on paper.

And the Knicks have every right to protect the next four or five seasons instead of just the next one. So where's the landing zone? Somewhere between $200 million and $277 million, there's a number where KAT feels valued and respected, and where the front office still feels confident it can keep Brunson, OG, Bridges, and Hart around him. If I had to guess, the deal that eventually gets signed lands somewhere in the low-to-mid $60 millions per year — above his current salary, a real raise that honors the title, but meaningfully below the max. Something in the range of $240 million over four years would split the difference almost exactly down the middle. And there's another lever both sides can pull: structure. A deal that starts higher and declines, or one with a player option at the back end, can make a number look very different to both sides without changing the total much.

Now the reality check: this is a public negotiation with a LOT of money on the line, and both sides want to protect their leverage. Things could change in days or even hours. Monday's quote might itself be leverage — a player telling the public, and the front office, that he's not going to fold. But right now neither side has shown willingness to move, and every update coming out of the building has been a little more negative than the last.

WHAT IT MEANS FOR THE SEASON

So what does this mean for the title defense? First, the immediate reassurance: losing KAT this season is NOT a real risk. He'll be on the floor on ring night against Philly, and Mike Brown and the rest of the roster will keep preparing as if nothing has changed. The player option means he's under contract, and as we covered, the trade market for him is mechanically almost impossible under the apron. Second, the real danger: the longer this drags, the more it becomes a storyline that follows the team from city to city. Every road trip, every national TV broadcast, every slump, every bad shooting night — "is it the contract?" The best teams in this league protect their locker rooms from exactly that kind of noise.

And third, the human question we raised before, which gets louder with every cold quote: how does a player approach a season when he's not sure the other side is fully committed to him? This isn't about effort or professionalism. KAT has never been accused of either. It's simply human nature to be careful when your biggest contract isn't signed. New York wants to keep Karl-Anthony Towns. Towns wants to stay in New York. The question now is how badly each side wants it, and how much each is willing to give to make it happen. My prediction hasn't changed in direction, only in timing: this gets done, but probably not before the season — and likely only after both sides feel some pressure from the calendar.

WISEMAN'S HIDDEN WEAPON: THE MARK BRYANT CONNECTION

Now to the story that made me sit up this week, because it might be the strongest single fact in the entire center battle — and it's not about Mike Brown at all. Quick backstory on where Wiseman was before New York called. Last season, he appeared in just four games for Indiana before being waived the day after Christmas. Then he waited — for almost a year. He didn't sit still: he played in the USA Basketball World Cup qualifying window, averaging 8.5 points and 4.5 rebounds in about 20 minutes; he suited up in the Miami Pro League over the summer; and there were even reports he was considering taking his career overseas to the EuroLeague. Think about that: a former number two overall pick, at 25, seriously weighing whether his NBA career was over. Then, a few weeks ago, the Knicks called.

And here's the connection everyone missed. Rewind to the 2023-24 Detroit Pistons — the team that set an NBA record with 28 straight losses in a single season. One of the worst teams in modern history. But in the middle of all that losing, Wiseman put together the best stretch of his career: over 24 games, he averaged 12.7 points and 8.1 rebounds. And the big man coach in Detroit during that stretch? MARK BRYANT. Today, Mark Bryant holds that exact same job... with the New York Knicks.

Folks, that's huge. We spent a week talking about Mike Brown's history with Wiseman from Golden State — and that's real, the coach's fandom is real. But Brown knew Wiseman as a raw rookie on a dynasty with no minutes to spare. Mark Bryant knew him as the most productive version of himself he's ever been. Bryant is the guy who actually unlocked him — the one coach in the league who has already watched James Wiseman look like a legitimate NBA center night after night. Now he's working with him again, every day, in this camp. That's not just familiarity. That's a proven blueprint. And it explains something else from this week's reporting: Ian Begley says that before camp opened, the Knicks told free agents they were looking for a big man on the YOUNGER side. Wiseman fits that description perfectly — and now we know the staff had two people in the building who knew exactly what they were getting.

Let me do the honest reconciliation with my own coverage. Yesterday's Eubanks video made the case for the forgotten veteran, and I stand by it — Eubanks is the only one of the three who doesn't need anything to go right. But in fairness, the Bryant detail is the strongest pro-Wiseman fact we've seen. The full career arc makes the case on both sides: a terrific rookie year — 11.5 points and 5.8 rebounds in 39 games — before a torn meniscus cost him Golden State's entire championship run the next season; traded to Detroit; the best stretch of his career under Bryant; then a contract with Indiana that ended on opening night with a torn Achilles, while the Pacers went all the way to Game 7 of the Finals without him. 152 career games, 9.0 points and 5.5 rebounds, 56% shooting. Brutal luck, real talent. From New York's side, this is the classic low-risk, high-reward bet: a camp deal costs almost nothing, and if it works, a championship team lands a young, athletic big man at a bargain price — exactly what a team under tight financial limits needs. He still has to earn it: the body has to hold up, he has to defend within the system, and he has to be reliable in short minutes. But now he has a head coach who believes in him, a big-man coach who has already gotten the best out of him, and a team with a clear need at his position. For once in his career, the timing might be on his side.

WHY THE KNICKS SHOULD TRADE EARLY

And even if Wiseman wins the job, the conversation about the frontcourt isn't over — because there's a strong argument that New York should be shopping for another big RIGHT NOW. Here's the calendar logic. In the NBA, trade season unofficially opens on December 15th, the date when most players who signed new contracts in the summer become eligible to be traded. Before that date, the market is quiet: unless a star demands out, most teams wait. They want to see what they have, they want more players available to choose from, and they need a few weeks of games to figure out what they actually need.

The Knicks are in a completely different position. They don't need to wait to figure out what they need. They're bringing back more than 80% of the rotation from a championship roster — and the only notable players who left were Mitchell Robinson and Ariel Hukporti. Both centers. The math couldn't be simpler: two big men walked out the door, and the hole is obvious. Even with Wiseman, Eubanks, and Bradley in camp, New York doesn't have a clear permanent answer at third center. And honestly, there's an uncomfortable question underneath that: do the Knicks even have KAT's main PLAYOFF backup locked in? Andre Drummond was a smart signing at the veteran minimum, and his rebounding and physicality will help during the regular season. But think about what happened last spring: when New York swept Philadelphia in the second round, the Sixers barely played Drummond. His defensive limitations made him hard to use in that matchup. Playoff series expose slower bigs, and the Knicks have seen that up close.

THE ASSETS, THE TARGETS, AND THE EARLY-MOVER EDGE

So what can New York actually offer? The same apron that shapes every decision limits this one too. The Knicks can't chase a big name without sending out a core player, so the main salary pieces are Pacôme Dadiet's $2.9 million and Deuce McBride's expiring $4 million deal. Add the war chest of second-round picks we've covered, and that's the toolbox. It's limited but workable. The real problem is competition. Backup centers shouldn't cost a fortune, but the names that would interest the Knicks won't be given away either. The list being discussed includes Moussa Diabaté, who this channel has followed since July; Dylan Cardwell, the young rim protector; Zach Edey, the massive 7'4" Memphis big we broke down in an earlier video; Kel'el Ware, Miami's young shot-blocker; and Yves Missi, the Pelicans prospect New York already tried to get this summer.

THE 7'4" MONSTER: WHY ZACH EDEY IS THE MOST FASCINATING NAME ON THE LIST

Now let's spend real time on the name that makes everyone stop scrolling, because he's unlike anyone else on that list. Zach Edey is SEVEN-FOOT-FOUR — the tallest player on any of New York's target boards by a mile, a two-time National Player of the Year at Purdue, and a man who walks into every NBA game as the biggest human being on the floor. We broke down his file earlier this summer, so here's the short version with a Knicks lens on it: Edey is a rebounding and finishing MACHINE near the basket. When he catches the ball within five feet, it's basically a dunk or a foul. He swallows offensive rebounds because nobody can box out a man that size. And defensively, simply standing in the paint changes what opponents try — shots that would be layups against a normal center become floaters and kick-outs against him.

Now think about the exact problem we just described with Drummond. When the Knicks swept Philadelphia, the Sixers barely played Drummond because playoff series punish slower bigs. Edey isn't fast either — let's be honest, that's his biggest weakness, and quick guards will try to drag him into space. But here's the difference: Edey's size is so extreme that in a drop coverage, sitting near the rim, he can protect the basket in a way Drummond simply can't. And there's a matchup angle that makes this fascinating: think about who New York has to beat. Embiid in Philly. Duren in Detroit if that standoff ever resolves. And Wembanyama, the alien who just warned the Knicks he's coming for the mountain. A 7'4" body you can throw at giants for twelve physical minutes in a playoff series is a weapon almost no other contender has.

And the fit next to KAT might be the best part. Imagine the double-big look we discussed after media day — Towns sliding to the four, stretching the floor from 25 feet, while Edey anchors the paint. KAT's shooting pulls the opposing center out; Edey punishes the smaller defender left behind on the glass. It's the old Towns-Robinson pairing on steroids: one big who spaces, one who dominates the paint. That's exactly the combination the Knicks lost when Mitch left.

Now the honest problems, because there are real ones. First, availability: is Memphis even willing to move him? A young, cheap, productive center is the kind of player rebuilding and playoff teams alike want to KEEP. Second, his contract: Edey is still on his rookie-scale deal, which makes him cheap for whoever has him — but also makes his team reluctant to sell, because cheap and productive is the most valuable combination in the second-apron era. Third, the price: if Memphis listens at all, they're going to want more than a bag of second-round picks. They'd likely ask about Deuce, or Diawara, or real draft capital New York simply doesn't have. And fourth, the fit questions: Edey's lack of foot speed is a real issue in switching defenses, and Mike Brown's scheme asks bigs to move. Put all of that together and here's my honest read: Edey is the HIGHEST-impact name on the list, but probably the HARDEST to get. If there's any path to him, it's exactly the one we just described — move early, before December, when Memphis isn't hearing ten offers at once. If New York waits until the trade season opens, the competition for a young 7'4" center will be fierce. So no, the Knicks haven't signed a monster yet. But if Leon Rose has the courage to call early, this is the one name on the board that could change the entire frontcourt — and the entire title defense — in a single phone call.

Once more teams get involved in December, New York will be at a disadvantage. They have no blue-chip prospects to offer. McBride's upcoming free agency limits what other teams will pay for him. They can't trade a first-round pick before 2033 — and that's a pick they should never move just for a bench center anyway. And there's something deeply uncomfortable, as I've said many times on this channel, about trading a player who might be the sixth-best guy on your roster just to get a second- or third-string center. Deuce is worth more than that.

Which is exactly why acting EARLY becomes New York's biggest advantage. While other teams sit and wait for clarity, the Knicks can offer second-round picks and bench salary to get a deal done in October or November, before the bidding wars start. Think of it like buying a house before it hits the open market: you don't need to outbid anyone if you're the only buyer at the door. That's the luxury of knowing exactly who you are and exactly what you need. Now, this doesn't mean every target is realistic, or that every team will sell early. But it means Leon Rose shouldn't wait for December 15th just because everyone else does. The champions already know their hole. They should fill it before the market wakes up.

THE BOTTOM LINE

So here's where we stand, nine days before ring night. The KAT negotiation has a number now — $200 million offered, $277 million possible, $77 million apart — and a quote, "it doesn't look good," that moved the timeline even if it didn't change the destination. Both sides are right, the landing zone is somewhere around $60-plus million a year, and the calendar will eventually push them together. The center battle got its most important new fact: Mark Bryant, the coach who unlocked Wiseman in Detroit, is unlocking him again in New York. And the front office has a smart path available on the trade market — move early, while everyone else waits, using picks and bench salary instead of the players who won the title. A lot of uncertainty, Tunnel fam. But uncertainty with a plan is just strategy.
