## THE GAME: A LEAD AFTER THREE, A LOSS AT THE BUZZER

Quickly, the result. The Knicks are now 0–2 in the preseason. They led Washington 84–81 after three quarters, but the Wizards won the fourth quarter 30–25 and stole it late, 111–109. Is anybody losing sleep over a preseason record? No. The starters played around 20 minutes each, and nobody hangs a banner for October exhibitions. Speaking of banners — the real one goes up on October 20th against Philadelphia, opening night.

But the scoreboard isn't the story. The story is the rotation. Because when you watch which bench players Mike Brown trusted, and which ones sat through three full quarters, the picture of this roster gets a lot clearer.

## THE LADDER: WHO'S CLIMBING

Let's build the ladder, starting with the players whose stock went UP over these two games.

**James Wiseman — climbing.** For the second straight game, Wiseman was the backup center who got the call. Against Washington: nine minutes, four points on two-of-three shooting, and two blocked shots. In the opener in Philadelphia: eight points, five rebounds, and a block in nine minutes, on three-of-four from the field. The only blemish was the free-throw line, where he went two-of-six. The New York Post had already called him the favorite to become the Knicks' 14th man before these games, and nothing that happened Thursday changed that.

And here's the context that makes it even more impressive. Wiseman tore his Achilles last December, right before Christmas. The preseason opener in Philadelphia was his first game action since that injury. Coming back from an Achilles and immediately producing efficient, active minutes is not a small thing. Add the connection we keep coming back to — Wiseman and Mike Brown were together in Golden State from 2020 to 2022, when Brown was an assistant on a championship staff — and you understand why the coach keeps calling his number. Brown knows what Wiseman can do, and what he still needs to fix.

## WHY THE NEW SIGNING IS "EXPLODING" — AND WHAT THAT REALLY MEANS

Let's put Wiseman's two games together, because the combined line is what makes this story pop. Eighteen minutes total. Twelve points. Seven-of-nine from the field — that's 78 percent. Three blocked shots. And five rebounds in the opener alone. Per minute, that's one of the most productive bench bigs in the entire preseason so far.

Now, to be honest with you — because that's how we do it here — "exploding" in October means something different than it does in May. He's playing nine minutes a night, mostly against second units. He's not dropping 30. And the free throws, two-of-six, are a real issue for a big man who's going to draw contact around the rim. But here's what matters: in a battle for one spot, he doesn't need to dominate. He needs to be the guy the coach trusts when the starters sit. Two games, two nights where he got the call first. That's the explosion that counts.

And think about what that means for the roster. The Knicks don't have Mitchell Robinson anymore. They need a third center who can protect the rim, finish lobs, and play 10 to 15 meaningful minutes when injuries or foul trouble hit. If Wiseman can be that guy at a minimum salary, it's one of the best low-cost moves any contender made this offseason — and a big reason the Knicks can live under the second apron without losing their size. That's the surprise. Not a blockbuster trade. A cheap bet that suddenly looks like a smart one.

**Andre Drummond — locked in.** Drummond isn't on the bubble; he's the clear number two center, and Thursday showed why. He nearly put up a preseason triple-double: eight points, nine rebounds, four assists, and two steals in 20 minutes. His job was to battle a Washington frontcourt that now features Anthony Davis, and on the glass, he held his ground. With Mitch gone, Drummond's role this season is massive. He's the bridge between KAT's minutes and whoever wins that third-center job.

**Landry Shamet — shooting his way into the conversation.** Three-of-four from deep, 13 points in 16 minutes. In a camp where Brown is giving bench minutes to proven reserves, Shamet looked like exactly that: a veteran who knows his role and hits open shots.

**Jordan Clarkson, Jose Alvarado, and Deuce McBride — trusted.** Clarkson added seven points in nine minutes, and Alvarado and McBride both got time in the rotation. These are established players, and Brown leaned on them. That matters for the bubble guys, because every minute these veterans take is a minute the young players don't get.

## THE LADDER: WHO'S SLIDING

Now the other side of the ladder — the players who need the last three preseason games badly.

**Drew Eubanks — sliding.** Ian Begley recently made the case for Eubanks as the safest play in this battle: eight years in the NBA, a good rebounder, willing to play physical on both ends. For a defending champion, a reliable veteran who knows his role can look like the smart, low-risk choice. And I've made versions of that case on this channel myself. But the minutes are telling a different story. In the opener, Eubanks played about six minutes, all in the fourth quarter, with one point and one rebound. Against Washington, he didn't see the floor through the first three quarters. Neither did Tony Bradley. And there's another factor: Eubanks is coming off surgery to repair a torn ligament in his left thumb, the injury that ended his 2025–26 season with Sacramento back in March. He's healthy enough to compete — but he hasn't been given the stage to prove it.

**Kevin McCullar Jr. — under pressure.** This is the young player whose situation got the most serious this week, and we'll spend a full section on him in a second.

**Bruce Brown — struggling.** Two points, a turnover, and four fouls in about ten minutes in Philadelphia. Then, against Washington, no minutes through three quarters.

**Ochai Agbaji and Jaden Akins — waiting.** Both chasing a standard roster spot, and both sat through the first three quarters on Thursday. After Agbaji's scoreless opener, these last games are now must-impress for him.

## THE SPEECH THAT CHANGED THE TEMPERATURE

Now here's the moment that turned up the heat on all of those players. After a practice he didn't like on Wednesday, Mike Brown addressed the entire training camp. His message was simple: the starters are going to play. Everyone else in the gym is fighting — either to make the team or to earn minutes. And then he added the part that really landed: none of this has anything to do with last season's championship roster. Every day is a competition.

Think about how unusual that is. Most coaches of a defending champion would protect the guys who were part of the title run. Brown did the opposite. He told the room that a ring doesn't buy you anything in October. For some players, that's motivation. For a young player on a two-way contract, it sounds a lot more like a warning.

## THE McCULLAR FILE: WHY THE PRESSURE IS REAL

Let's talk about Kevin McCullar Jr., because he's the young player whose future in New York is suddenly the biggest question at the bottom of the roster.

The Knicks clearly like McCullar. Two years ago, they traded the draft rights to Oso Ighodaro to get him. And here's the painful part of that trade: Ighodaro has built a solid role in Phoenix. Last season he finished fourth on the Suns in minutes played, and first among their big men. Given how badly the Knicks need size right now — especially with Mitch gone — Ighodaro would have made a lot of sense on this roster. Instead, the Knicks bet on McCullar, and they've kept that bet alive. He's in his third season with the team, and he re-signed on a two-way contract in late September.

On paper, McCullar is exactly the kind of player the Knicks love. He can score, pass, defend, and rebound, and he gives you flexibility across the wing positions. In a lot of ways, he plays like a smaller version of Josh Hart. The problem? The Knicks brought in a veteran with almost the same profile.

Bruce Brown has eight years in the NBA. He was a key piece on Denver's 2023 championship team, and that run earned him a big contract with Indiana. From there, he became a trade chip — Indiana sent him to Toronto in the Pascal Siakam deal, and Toronto later moved him to New Orleans in the Brandon Ingram trade. He's made more than $60 million in his career. And now he's in New York on a non-guaranteed Exhibit 9 deal, fighting for a job.

Technically, Brown and McCullar aren't competing for the same contract. Brown has too many years of service to be eligible for a two-way deal. But in practical terms, they want the same thing: a place in the wing rotation, and eventually a standard roster spot.

And then there's age. McCullar is 25. That's young in normal life — but for a fringe NBA player still trying to secure a place in the league, it's not young anymore. He's already older than six players on the current Knicks roster. With every year, the "maybes" around his development start turning into "maybe nots."

The preseason hasn't given him much room either. In Philadelphia, he did a little bit of everything: six points, four rebounds, an assist, and a block in about ten minutes, but he missed all three of his three-point attempts. Against Washington, he didn't play through three quarters.

And here's the most uncomfortable detail of all: because McCullar is on a two-way contract, cutting him would cost the Knicks almost nothing financially. Two-way deals are non-guaranteed, and teams can swap players in and out at any time. If McCullar doesn't clearly outplay someone like Bruce Brown, the Knicks have every reason to explore other two-way options. To be clear, nothing has happened yet. But if you're looking for the young Knick most at risk of being cut this fall, this is the name. And that's the honest answer to every "Knicks cut young player" headline you'll see this month.

## THE PHILOSOPHY QUESTION: SAFE OR BOLD?

So why do I think Wiseman wins his battle, and why does that matter for McCullar? Because of who's making the decision.

Safe has never been the Knicks' style under Leon Rose. Look at the track record. In 2022, New York outbid the competition for Jalen Brunson when plenty of people around the league doubted he could be a franchise point guard. Later, the Knicks sent a huge package of first-round picks to Brooklyn for Mikal Bridges, a player who had never been an All-Star. And after reaching the Eastern Conference Finals for the first time in a quarter century, the front office fired Tom Thibodeau and handed the job to Mike Brown. Every one of those decisions was questioned. Together, they ended with the Knicks winning the 2026 NBA championship — their first since 1973 — by beating the San Antonio Spurs in five games.

With that history, choosing the safe veteran would almost feel out of character. Betting on a 25-year-old former number two pick who already knows the head coach, costs only a minimum salary, and keeps earning the minutes? That looks a lot more like the Knicks way.

But here's the twist for McCullar: the same philosophy works against him. The upside bet on the wing is McCullar. The proven bet is Bruce Brown. And a team chasing back-to-back titles can afford one upside gamble at the end of the bench — not necessarily two. A rebuilding team can wait for a young player to grow. A defending champion usually wants the known quantity. Once upon a time, turning a second-round pick into a rotation player was a big win for this franchise. Now, the only win that matters is another parade in June.

## KAT UNDER THE MICROSCOPE

Now let's move to the biggest contract question in New York. Karl-Anthony Towns and the Knicks still don't have an extension, and the longer the standoff lasts, the more trade speculation follows him. And Thursday night did not help his case on the court.

Against Washington, KAT finished with eight points, seven rebounds, and three assists — but he made just two of his seven shots and committed three turnovers. And he lost his matchup with Anthony Davis, who scored 16 points with seven rebounds in under 17 minutes in his first game as a Wizard. Davis and Trae Young combined for 31 points in about 17 minutes. And the number one overall pick, AJ Dybantsa, added 11 points and four steals.

Is a preseason box score going to decide a $200 million negotiation? No. Starters played about 20 minutes. KAT wasn't trying to win the October title. But here's why it still matters: every time KAT has a quiet night, the "is he worth it" crowd gets louder. And every time he dominates, his side of the table gets stronger. That's the reality of playing in a contract year in the world's biggest media market. Even exhibitions become evidence.

## THE CLEVELAND IDEA: WHY IT'S THE MOST UNCOMFORTABLE ONE

And this is where the story gets spicy. In a piece for Bleacher Report, Andrew Peters listed three teams that could make a move for Towns if he and the Knicks can't reach an agreement. One of them is the Cleveland Cavaliers.

Let me be clear about what this is: a columnist's idea, not a report. No insider has said Cleveland has called, and no insider has said the Knicks are shopping KAT. But it's worth breaking down, because of who Cleveland is.

The Cavaliers are the team the Knicks dominated in last spring's Eastern Conference Finals. They're entering their first full season with the trio of Donovan Mitchell, James Harden, and Evan Mobley, and they added Peyton Watson this offseason. Even so, Peters argues that Cleveland is starting this season with essentially the same core that fell short last year. Adding Harden got them to the conference finals, but not over the top. With a star big man potentially available, Peters thinks Cleveland should at least reconsider its plan.

What would it cost? According to Peters, Cleveland would have to give up either Jarrett Allen or Evan Mobley. Mitchell is the centerpiece, so he's not part of the conversation. Mobley is a former Defensive Player of the Year and an All-NBA selection in 2024–25, so Peters sees a package built around Allen, plus role players or draft picks, as the more realistic version.

Now here's why this idea makes me uncomfortable as a Knicks fan. Trading your All-Star big to the team you just beat in the conference finals is the kind of move that can come back to haunt you for years. You'd be handing your biggest rival the exact piece they were missing. Imagine KAT in a Cavs jersey in a playoff series at the Garden. That's a nightmare scenario.

But I'll be fair: the reason the idea exists is that Cleveland has two quality bigs the Knicks could actually target if they ever felt they had no choice. Allen is a proven rebounder and rim protector. Mobley is just 25 and entering his prime — which is exactly why Cleveland wouldn't want to give him up. So in theory, the pieces fit. In reality, I don't see either side jumping into this anytime soon. And for now, there's no sign that a trade is coming at all.

## THE CONTRACT, ONE MORE TIME

For anybody just joining the story, here's the contract picture in one place. Towns is eligible for a four-year extension worth about $276 million. According to ESPN's Shams Charania, the Knicks' latest offer was four years and roughly $200 million. KAT is due to make about $57 million this season, and he holds a player option worth $61.1 million for 2027–28. Owner James Dolan has said the team won't go into the second apron, and that line is shaping every decision the front office makes.

KAT has said he's willing to take a discount. But Stefan Bondy of the Post reported that Towns expected talks to go smoothly after winning a title — and instead, he feels disrespected. Before the preseason opener, KAT laid out exactly what he wants: a deal that keeps the team together, works for the Knicks, and values him. Then he added that none of those things are close to happening, and admitted an extension before the regular season doesn't look likely. The good news? Talks can continue into the season. There's no hard deadline forcing a decision right now.

KAT is a six-time All-Star who turns 31 on November 15th. He averaged nearly 16 points and more than 10 rebounds during the title run. His argument: reward that. The Knicks' argument: protect the flexibility around the championship core. Both arguments make sense. That's exactly why this is so hard.

## THE EAST ARMS RACE CUTS BOTH WAYS

And finally, the bigger picture. The Washington game was a reminder of how fast the competition around the Knicks is improving. Look at this East. Philadelphia added LeBron James, Jaylen Brown, and Anfernee Simons. Miami landed Giannis Antetokounmpo. Boston brought in Paul George — and Mitchell Robinson. Toronto brought Kawhi Leonard home. Washington now has Davis, Young, and Dybantsa. Indiana expects Tyrese Haliburton back from his Achilles injury. Detroit is coming off a 60-win season. And Cleveland could decide KAT is its missing piece.

Here's why that cuts both ways in the KAT standoff. On one side, it's the strongest argument for keeping him: with every rival loading up, breaking up the core a few months after a title would be a massive gamble. You don't get weaker in an arms race. On the other side, it's the reason other teams might pay real value for him in January if things fall apart. A stronger East means more desperate contenders. And desperate contenders make offers.

My take hasn't changed: keep him, find the middle number, run it back. But the longer this drags, the more columns like Peters' will pile up — and the more pressure lands on both sides to end it.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

So here's what two preseason games told us. The new signing is exploding: Wiseman, ten months removed from an Achilles tear, is seven-of-nine with three blocks and is winning the third-center battle in the only way that counts — by getting the minutes — while Eubanks and Bradley are waiting for a chance. Drummond is the clear number two behind KAT now that Mitch is in Boston. Mike Brown told the whole camp that last year's ring buys nobody anything, and Kevin McCullar Jr. is the young player feeling that message the most. And KAT, after a rough night against Anthony Davis, is now being floated by Bleacher Report as a possible target for Cleveland — the one destination that would hurt Knicks fans more than any other. Three preseason games left: Minnesota at the Garden on Monday, then Toronto twice. Then the banner.
