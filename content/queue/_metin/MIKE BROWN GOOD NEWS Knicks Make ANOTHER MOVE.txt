## THE HONEST FRAMING

Quick clarity, the NBA Tunnel way. This isn't a move for the opening-night roster. Dink Pate isn't taking a spot on the 15-man team, and he's not getting a two-way contract right now. It's a development move: the Knicks are bringing him back into the organization through their G League affiliate, the Westchester Knicks. And "good news for Mike Brown" means good news for the pipeline that feeds his bench — not a quote from the coach. With that clear, here's why it matters.

## THE MOVE: EXHIBIT 10, THEN WESTCHESTER

According to NBA insider Michael Scott, the Knicks signed Dink Pate to an Exhibit 10 deal after he withdrew from Providence. Multiple Knicks beat writers — Stefan Bondy, Ian Begley, and Steve Popper — confirmed it.

Then, almost immediately, the Knicks waived him. And if that sounds strange, it's actually the whole point. Here's how Exhibit 10 deals work: a team signs a young player to a training-camp contract, then waives him before the season. If he signs with that team's G League affiliate, he receives a bonus, and the team keeps his G League rights. That's the path that sends Pate back to Westchester — familiar coaches, familiar system, and a direct line to the Knicks.

So don't read "waived" as bad news. In this case, waiving him was the plan.

## WHY HE LEFT PROVIDENCE

So why would a 20-year-old leave college basketball to come back to the G League? According to Heavy's Kevin McNamara, Pate had been fighting with the NCAA for months over his collegiate eligibility. He'd already played professionally — with the Ignite, in Mexico, and in Westchester — which made his case complicated. And he didn't want to risk being denied eligibility with no professional situation to fall back on.

Remember the backstory: after his strong season in Westchester, Pate reportedly received two-way offers from multiple teams, including the Knicks. He declined them to pursue college basketball, where the money in today's NIL era can be significant. That was a reasonable bet. But when the eligibility fight dragged on, the risk of losing an entire season became too big. So he chose certainty. And with that uncertainty behind him, he's back in familiar surroundings, ready to keep developing.

And honestly, it's hard to blame him for either decision. Turning down two-way offers to chase a college opportunity made sense when the money and the exposure looked real. Walking away when the eligibility fight dragged on made sense too. At 20 years old, the worst thing that can happen to a developing player is a lost year without games. Pate chose to keep playing. For the Knicks, that's a gift: a player they already invested in, already know, and already liked enough to offer a two-way deal — back in their system at almost no cost.

## WHO IS DINK PATE?

If you haven't followed him, here's the story — and it's a unique one.

**The Ignite (2023):** Pate signed with the G League Ignite at just 17 years old, making him the youngest American professional basketball player ever at the time. That tells you how highly he was regarded as a teenager.

**Mexico City (2024–25):** He spent the next season with the Mexico City Capitanes in the G League, gaining more pro experience against grown men.

**Westchester (2025–26):** After going undrafted in 2025, he joined the Knicks organization. And his first season in Westchester was a big step forward: about 16.3 points, 6.6 rebounds, and 3.9 assists across 50 appearances, while shooting 34.6 percent from three.

So by age 20, he's already played three professional seasons. Most players his age are sophomores in college.

## THE TOOLS: 6'8" GUARD, NEAR 7-FOOT WINGSPAN

What makes Pate so intriguing is the combination. He's 6-foot-8 with a near seven-foot wingspan, and he handles the ball like a guard. He can rebound. He can play guard or wing. He runs the floor in transition better than most players in Westchester and scores on the break. And his playmaking and court vision are seriously underrated — at his size, he can drive, collapse the defense, and kick it out to shooters.

Think about the kind of player the Knicks love. Big, versatile, connective, able to defend multiple positions. That's the profile Pate is growing into.

## THE SHOOTING STORY: WHY THE NUMBERS MATTER

Now, the biggest knock on Pate coming into the pros was his shooting. Early in his professional career, he was around 27 percent from three and about 39 percent from the field. Those were real problems.

So when you see 34.6 percent from three in Westchester, you might shrug — that's not elite. But look at the trajectory. From the high 20s to the mid-30s. That's real, steady improvement. The same goes for his turnovers and his defensive awareness — two other early weaknesses that have gotten better every year.

That's the key with Pate. It's not just what he can do now. It's the direction he's moving. At 20 years old, with those physical tools, a player who keeps improving his weak spots at this rate can become something real.

## WHAT HIS GAME LOOKS LIKE ON THE FLOOR

Let's paint the picture for anyone who hasn't watched him. Pate grabs a defensive rebound and doesn't look for a guard — he pushes it himself. At 6-foot-8, he sees over smaller defenders in transition, and he's comfortable finishing through contact or dropping it off to a trailing big. In the half court, he's at his best attacking downhill: he gets into the paint, draws two defenders, and makes the pass. He's not hesitating anymore on open threes, and he's developed a little mid-range game to punish defenders who go under screens.

Defensively, the tools are obvious. With that length, he can switch onto guards and bother wings, and his wingspan makes him dangerous in passing lanes. The next step is consistency — staying locked in off the ball and making the right rotations. That's exactly the kind of thing a G League coaching staff can drill all season. And it's the kind of thing Mike Brown demands from anyone who wants minutes.

## THE TWO-WAY PRESSURE

And here's where it gets interesting for the players already in the building. The Knicks' three two-way contracts right now belong to Tyler Nickel, Kevin McCullar Jr., and Jaden Akins. With Pate back in Westchester, every one of them should take notice.

Two-way deals are non-guaranteed, and teams can swap players in and out at any point. If Pate tears up the G League again — and if one of the current two-way players struggles — Pate could be the guy who takes that spot. Maybe not this month. Maybe not even this season. But if he keeps developing at this rate, a two-way contract with the Knicks could come sooner rather than later, possibly as early as next season. And if he plays above that level? Who's to say he couldn't earn a standard contract and a spot off the bench down the road?

There's one more layer, though: the Knicks' most urgent two-way need right now is a center. They have zero bigs on two-way deals, and former Knick Trey Jemison was just waived by Toronto. So if a two-way spot opens soon, it might go to a big first. Pate is more of a long-term piece.

## WHY THIS IS GOOD NEWS FOR MIKE BROWN

So why call this good news for Mike Brown? Because of how he's running this team.

In camp, Brown told everyone outside the starting five that they're fighting — to make the team or to earn minutes — and that last season's ring doesn't protect anyone. And then he backed it up. He started Mohamed Diawara, a second-round pick, in the preseason dress rehearsal. He gave Tyler Kolek late-game minutes, and Kolek hit the winning shot. He trusted James Wiseman, a reclamation project, as his third center.

That's a coach who rewards development. And for a coach like that, the more talented young players in the pipeline, the better. Westchester is where those players are built. Adding a 6-foot-8 playmaker who already knows the system gives Mike Brown another potential option down the line — especially during an 82-game grind when injuries pile up.

## THE PIPELINE PHILOSOPHY

And step back for a second, because this move fits a bigger pattern. The Knicks have built a champion partly by developing and trusting players other teams overlooked.

Deuce McBride was a second-round pick who became a sixth man and one of the best defensive backup guards in the league — so good that his value has skyrocketed and keeping him under the apron is now a real challenge. Landry Shamet came in on an Exhibit 9 deal and became a $24 million champion. Diawara, a second-rounder, earned a contract and a start in the dress rehearsal. Kolek is a cheap, productive backup point guard.

Why does this matter so much now? Because the Knicks have to stay below the second apron. That means they need productive players on small contracts. They don't have first-round picks to trade before 2033. Sometimes they trade back in the draft or skip picks entirely. So their other path to cheap talent is exactly this: undrafted players, overlooked players, guys like Dink Pate — sign them, develop them in Westchester, and turn them into contributors.

In a few years, maybe even next season, this Knicks roster won't look the same. Contracts will force changes. The teams that stay competitive in the second-apron era are the ones with a pipeline. Signing Pate back is a small move that supports a big strategy.

## THE DEUCE LESSON: WHY YOU ALWAYS NEED THE NEXT ONE

There's a bittersweet lesson hidden in all of this. Deuce McBride is the perfect example of the pipeline working. A second-round pick, developed patiently, given a chance, and now one of the most valuable bench players on a championship team. That's a win for the organization.

But look at what happens next. Because Deuce got so good, he's now in line for a raise the Knicks may not be able to afford under the apron. His agent says he'd love an extension and has earned one. And with Brunson, KAT, and Hart ahead of him in the payday line, he might end up getting paid somewhere else — either next summer or in a trade.

That's the cycle in the second-apron era. You develop a player, he becomes valuable, and then he becomes expensive. The only way to keep winning is to always have the next one coming. Shamet stepped up. Diawara is rising. Kolek is ready. And now Dink Pate is back in the system. When the bill comes due for one player, the next one needs to be ready to take his minutes. That's why a G League signing in October matters more than it looks.

## HOW WESTCHESTER FITS THE MACHINE

And Westchester isn't just a place to stash players — it's part of the Knicks' system. Players there learn the same terminology and concepts the big club uses, so when someone gets called up, the adjustment is small. We saw it last season with Trey Jemison, who moved back and forth between Westchester and New York and was ready when the Knicks needed emergency minutes. We've seen it with Kevin McCullar Jr., who's been developed in the organization for three seasons.

For Pate, that familiarity is a big advantage. He already knows the coaches, the schemes, and the expectations. He doesn't need a learning period — he can pick up right where he left off last season, when he was one of the most productive players in the building.

## WHAT TO WATCH FROM PATE THIS SEASON

If you want to track whether Pate is on his way to a two-way deal, here are four benchmarks I'll be watching.

**One: the three-point shot.** Can he push from 34.6 percent toward 36 or 37 percent on solid volume? That would make him a real NBA-level threat.

**Two: turnovers.** A 6-foot-8 playmaker who takes care of the ball is rare. If his assist-to-turnover ratio keeps improving, that's a huge sign.

**Three: defense.** With his length, he should be a plus defender. Watch his steals, his deflections, and whether he can guard multiple positions consistently.

**Four: winning.** Production on a winning Westchester team carries more weight than empty numbers on a losing one. If Pate is a leader on a good G League team, the Knicks will notice.

But in the East, the market still sees the champions as the team to beat. The rivals got scarier — Kawhi in Toronto, LeBron and Jaylen Brown in Philly, Tatum back and Mitch added in Boston — but the market still trusts the champs. Now they have to prove it.
