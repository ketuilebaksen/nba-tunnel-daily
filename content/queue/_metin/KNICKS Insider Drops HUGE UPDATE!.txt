## THE DEADLINE THAT CAME AND WENT

According to Daily Knicks, Karl-Anthony Towns can no longer sign an extension with the Knicks that builds on top of picking up his $61 million player option for next season. That window closed at 11:59 p.m. on October 19th. By letting that cutoff come and go, New York made something clear: it wants KAT's salary to come down starting in 2027–28. The scenario where he keeps the full $61 million next year and then tacks on a long extension is now off the table.

KAT can still reach a new agreement with the Knicks during the season — but to make that happen, he first has to decline the option. And frankly, the fact that the deadline didn't faze the front office says a lot about how much leverage New York believes it holds.

Daily Knicks also laid out two ways the Knicks could still get his number down. One: sign him to an in-season extension that starts at his max of about $61.6 million and then declines by 8 percent each year. Two: wait until after the season, have him opt into the $61 million, and then extend him.

## A QUICK CLARIFICATION FOR REGULAR VIEWERS

Honest moment, because the reporting on this deadline has been described in slightly different ways by different outlets. In our earlier video, we framed October 19th as the date KAT had to decline his option in order to negotiate during the season. The cleanest read now is this: the October 19th window for one specific type of extension has closed, KAT hasn't publicly announced his option decision, and — according to Jake Fischer — he's likely to decline it so talks can continue during the season. The big picture we described hasn't changed: in-season talks are still possible, but the path is narrower and the pressure is real.

## FISCHER: DECLINE, THEN WAIT FOR FEBRUARY

So what happens next? According to Jake Fischer, Towns will likely decline the option, allowing him and his agents to keep negotiating with the Knicks once New York's front office decides — quietly — to reopen talks. The risk for the Knicks is obvious: without an extension, KAT can walk out the door for nothing as a free agent next summer.

And when will those talks restart? Fischer believes the next time we hear about the Knicks and KAT — and potentially Josh Hart — having extension conversations, it'll be after the trade deadline. He called that pretty typical of how this front office operates.

Think about what that means. The trade deadline comes in February. If extension talks don't restart until after it, then the Knicks will have passed the last moment when they could trade KAT this season. In other words, if Fischer is right, the front office is signaling that the January pivot — trading him rather than risking free agency — isn't the plan. The plan is to play the season and negotiate after.

## BONDY: "STUCK BETWEEN $200 MILLION AND A HARD PLACE"

Stefan Bondy summed it up perfectly: Karl-Anthony Towns is stuck between $200 million and a hard place — and so far, both KAT and the Knicks have chosen the hard place. Negotiations are suspended, and Towns could hit free agency in about eight months. But Bondy's conclusion is the one that matters: logic says the Knicks and Towns will agree to an extension. They make the most sense for each other.

## THE PRICE-TAG PANEL

And this is the most important new information. Bondy asked people around the league what KAT would actually get on the open market. Here's the panel.

**A Western Conference executive:** "On the open market, I don't know that KAT gets paid a ton."

**An Eastern Conference executive:** "I would feel better in the 45 to 50 million range for next season."

**An agent:** "I think a team would offer a 40 to 50 million range annually on a short-term deal."

Read those three together. Forty to fifty million. That's almost exactly what the Knicks reportedly offered — about $200 million over four years, which Ian Begley described as somewhere between $45 and $50 million per season. In other words, the league's view of KAT's market value lines up with New York's offer, not with the max.

And remember the free-agent history we broke down on ring night: since 2022, only seven players have signed deals over $25 million a year while switching teams, and Paul George is the only one who got $40 million-plus. The open market has dried up for stars. Stars who want out force trades. So the panel isn't just three opinions — it matches the data.

## SO WHAT'S THE MIDDLE GROUND?

Now, let's be fair to KAT. He's eligible for four years and $276 million. The Knicks are offering about $200 million. That's asking him to leave roughly $76 million on the table — after a title season in which he averaged 20.1 points, 11.9 rebounds, and 3.0 assists. He's said he's willing to take a discount. Just not that big.

So where's the deal? Here's my NBA Tunnel projection, clearly labeled as opinion: somewhere around four years and $220 million — about $55 million a year. Above the Knicks' current offer, above what the executives think he'd get on the open market, but well below the max. That gives KAT a win, gives the Knicks a contract that declines in value relative to the cap, and gives both sides what they said they want: KAT on a team that can win, and a front office that keeps some flexibility.

And here's a detail that makes a middle ground more realistic: according to Windhorst, KAT has already shown a willingness to reduce that 2027–28 number to give the Knicks flexibility in exchange for a large long-term guarantee. The gap is about total dollars and years — not about whether he wants to stay.

## THE WINDHORST WARNING: TWO TEAMMATES COULD PAY

But here's the cost — and this is the part that hurts. According to ESPN's Brian Windhorst, because of the apron rules, the Knicks are headed toward losing at least one player from their title-winning core. Windhorst noted that Josh Hart and Deuce McBride would likely be moved if the Knicks prioritize retaining Towns and he signs a deal close to what he prefers.

Let that sink in. Pay KAT near his number, and Hart — the heart of this team, with a $22 million team option for 2027–28 — and Deuce — the sixth man, already fourth in the payday line — could both be gone.

Even at my middle-ground number, the math is brutal. If the Knicks give KAT around $55 million a year and extend Hart on a team-friendly deal, Deuce almost certainly isn't staying at the contract he'll want. And looming over everything: future extensions for Jalen Brunson — projected around $290 million — and eventually OG Anunoby. Every dollar to KAT is a dollar that can't go somewhere else.

Windhorst also outlined the other pathway: the Knicks could simply let KAT pick up his option and not radically alter the roster this year. That avoids the immediate squeeze — but as we've covered, his $61 million next season could push the Knicks toward the apron anyway.

## THE HART DILEMMA

Let's pause on Josh Hart, because his name in Windhorst's report should get every Knicks fan's attention. Hart is making about $21 million this season with a team option around $22 million for 2027–28. He's been a full-time starter, one of the best rebounding guards in the league, and the guy who does the dirty work that never shows up on highlight reels. He's also the one who keeps telling everyone the truth about this era — that nobody has repeated in years because of the second apron and this CBA.

Publicly, Hart has stayed calm about his own contract. He's said he believes everything will work out. But if Fischer is right that his extension talks — like KAT's — won't resume until after the trade deadline, then Hart is playing this season under the same cloud. And if the Knicks commit big money to KAT, the front office will have to decide whether there's room for Hart at all.

Here's my honest view: a Knicks team without Josh Hart is a different team. Not just statistically — emotionally. If I'm forced to choose between Hart and Deuce under the apron, I keep Hart. But the fact that we even have to ask that question tells you how brutal this system is.

## THE SUITOR MAP: WHO COULD CHASE KAT?

So if KAT declines his option and no extension gets done, who's out there? Let's combine the reporting.

**From Bondy and the executives:** Chicago and the Clippers have the cleanest paths in terms of cap space and fit. The Nets can get there with the money, but it's not a good fit. Golden State and Dallas could get to around $50 million in space with more tinkering.

**From Windhorst:** The Warriors, Hawks, and Clippers would likely line up to make him big offers, along with others.

So the names that keep coming up: Bulls, Clippers, Warriors, Hawks, Nets, Mavericks.

## THE CONTENDER CHECK

Now look at that list and ask one question: which of those teams is a real contender right now? Maybe Golden State, if you squint. After that? Not one of those teams offers what New York offers: a defending champion, a Finals MVP point guard who plays perfectly with KAT, and a shot at multiple titles.

And then there's everything else that comes with being the second star in New York. The biggest market in the world. Endorsement deals that can make up a lot of the difference between $55 million and $69 million a year. A fan base that just chanted his name on ring night. If KAT goes to Chicago or Atlanta for slightly more money, he gives up most of that.

And don't forget the basketball fit. The Knicks' title run turned on making KAT the offensive hub — high-post passes, dribble handoffs, Brunson attacking off his screens. That system was built around his unique skill set as a shooting, passing center. Very few teams in the league could use him that way. On a rebuilding team, he'd be asked to carry everything. In New York, he's the perfect second star.

That's why I still believe the most likely outcome is that KAT and the Knicks figure it out. The teams interested in him are real. But the best basketball situation, the best market, and the best chance to win are all in New York.

## WHY THE KNICKS SHOULD STILL MOVE A LITTLE

And let me say this to the front office, too. The data supports New York's number. But the "walk for nothing" risk is real. If Fischer is right and talks don't resume until after the trade deadline, the Knicks will have given up the chance to trade him this season. At that point, all the leverage that comes from "he has no market" disappears if even one team — the Clippers, the Bulls, the Warriors — decides he's worth $55 million or more. It only takes one.

So the smart move is to find the middle before July. Not the max. Not $200 million. Something both sides can live with — even if it means painful decisions on Hart or Deuce.

## THE CALENDAR FROM HERE

If Fischer's timeline is right, here's how the KAT saga likely unfolds.

**Now through mid-December:** KAT plays, the Knicks play, and the contract stays quiet. Most of the offseason signings can't be traded until December 15th, so the trade market is sleepy.

**December 15th to the February trade deadline:** The market opens wider. This is when Begley's January pivot would have come into play — but if the Knicks aren't planning to reopen talks until after the deadline, it signals they're keeping KAT for the title run.

**After the trade deadline:** Quiet extension talks resume — for KAT, and potentially for Hart.

**June 30th:** The deadline for extensions with this year's eligible players.

**July:** If no deal and KAT declines his option, free agency opens — and the Bulls, Clippers, Warriors, Hawks, and others can make their pitches.

So KAT is essentially playing a full contract year in front of the most demanding fan base in the league. That's pressure — and opportunity.

## WHAT KAT CONTROLS

And here's the part KAT controls completely: how he plays. He's second on the betting board to lead the league in rebounding at plus-500, behind only Nikola Jokić. He turns 31 on November 15th. If he plays like an All-NBA center this season — dominating the glass, spacing the floor, running the offense as the hub the way he did in the playoffs — then every executive who said "$40 to $50 million" will have to rethink it. A dominant contract year is the best negotiating tool in basketball.

And if he stays healthy and leads the Knicks deep into another playoff run? The Knicks' "he has no market" leverage gets a lot weaker, fast.

## THE TWO-SIDED RISK

Let's lay out the risks on both sides, because that's why a deal makes sense.

**The Knicks' risk:** Losing an All-Star for nothing in free agency. Watching him sign with a rival or a West contender. Having to rebuild the frontcourt — again — after already losing Mitchell Robinson this summer.

**KAT's risk:** The market data says $40 to $50 million. He's 31. Injuries happen. And no other suitor offers a championship-level roster, a Finals MVP partner, and New York's market.

When both sides have real risk, that's usually when deals get done. Neither side wants to find out what happens if they don't.
