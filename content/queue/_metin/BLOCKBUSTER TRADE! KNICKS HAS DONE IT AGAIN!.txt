THE SIGNING: LIAM ROBBINS, EXHIBIT 10

The news via Steve Popper and Ian Begley: the Knicks have officially signed Liam Robbins to an Exhibit 10 contract. He was with New York in Summer League, the organization now holds his G League rights, and he'll compete in camp for one of the open two-way spots. The production line from last season with the Rip City Remix: 8.5 points, 5.1 rebounds, and 1.1 blocks in just 18 minutes a night across 32 games — solid, efficient big-man work at the developmental level.

But here's my favorite part of this story: the COURTSHIP. Robbins didn't stumble into this deal — he chased it. All through Vegas, the 7-footer made his intentions loud, telling reporters exactly what he could bring: "I'm someone who can come in, give good energy, play D, and play the Knick way — championship effort." Read that phrasing again. "The Knick way." The man studied the culture, named it, and pitched himself INTO it. Two years ago, fringe big men weren't campaigning to join this franchise — now they're auditioning for it in press conferences. One more small receipt of how completely the gravity has flipped. He wanted New York. New York wanted him back. That mutual hunger is exactly how camp-deal success stories start.

THE SCOUTING FILE: WHAT THE KID CAN DO

I watched this man in Summer League, and the toolkit is genuinely more interesting than the average camp big. Start with the obvious: he blocks shots — the skill this roster has been starving for since Mitch left. He rebounds. He plays comfortably in TRAFFIC — no panic when bodies collide, and he's got an actual hook shot, a lost art that lets him finish over contests down low even with multiple defenders draped on him. And here's the separator from the usual Exhibit 10 fare: the man can step out and shoot the three. A 7-footer with rim protection AND range is the exact hybrid profile this five-out system dreams about. Add multi-position defensive flashes and you understand my honest take: I'd rather have Liam Robbins than the Kelly Olynyks and Tony Bradleys of the remaining market. At this stage of the calendar, on this contract structure, he's the best big-man value the free market had left. That's not hype — that's just what the shelf looks like in mid-September.

And appreciate the Summer League audition for what it revealed beyond the quotes. Vegas is where fringe players either shrink or seize — the rosters are chaos, the minutes are political, and nobody runs plays for the camp big. Robbins seized: he protected the rim in scramble situations, ran the floor without cheating on effort, and — the detail the staff surely noticed — kept competing on possessions that didn't involve him. The organization then did something teams only do when genuinely intrigued: they secured his G LEAGUE RIGHTS along with the Exhibit 10. That's the tell. You don't lock up the developmental rights of a body; you lock up the rights of a project you intend to keep cooking, whichever side of the two-way line he lands on. Westchester's frontcourt — with Robbins potentially joining the Mascari-and-friends pipeline — is quietly becoming a real asset factory.

THE HONEST FLAWS — AND THEY'RE REAL

Now the other side, because a fair file needs both pages. Flaw number one, and it's a big one: the man is FOUL-PRONE — almost comically so. Watch his tape and you'll see the same disease KAT battled for three straight years: jumping into pump fakes, reaching from bad positions, fouling his way out of stretches he should own. And think about the nightmare stacking: KAT picks up his careless early whistles, Drummond collects a couple of his own... and your emergency big is ANOTHER foul magnet? That's a January night that ends with Diawara playing emergency five. The discipline has to come — and ironically, he's joining the one building where the head coach just spent a full year re-engineering exactly this problem in his star center. The cure lives here.

Flaw number two: the clock. Robbins is 27 — old in NBA-development terms — and he has never played anything close to a full NBA season; his longest look was a brief 13-game stint in Milwaukee. The G League production is real, but we've said it before in the Mascari breakdown: the G League is one animal, the NBA is a different beast entirely, and a 27-year-old must know his strengths and weaknesses COLD, because NBA opponents will identify them for him within two possessions. This is not a prospect with runway; this is a now-or-never professional. Which, for a camp battle, is honestly the right energy.

And for the fans asking "why does a 27-year-old career G Leaguer deserve this much analysis" — because this is EXACTLY the profile that championship depth miracles are made from. Every June, some anonymous camp signing ends up playing real playoff minutes for somebody — the injuries pile up, the rotations shrink, and suddenly the guy from the Rip City Remix is boxing out in a conference semifinal. The Knicks lived it: ask Jemison, ask the two-way carousel of last year's title run. You analyze the Liam Robbinses of the world in September precisely so you're not googling them in May.

THE LEON ROSE REPORT CARD: FAIR AND BALANCED

Now the segment this fanbase needs, because after months of center chaos, it's time for an honest grade — both columns. THE CRITICISM, stated plainly: the center situation was mishandled. There's no way around it. The front office clearly believed it would retain Hukporti even if Robinson walked — and when Philly's offer blew that assumption apart, there was NO plan B on the shelf. No draft pick spent on a big, no early free-agency pivot, just a scramble: the Cissé offer sheet Dallas matched, the rejected Missi calls, the Richards saga ending in Miami. Drummond is a fine soldier, but he doesn't replace what Mitch gave this defense, and pretending otherwise insults everyone's eyes. That's a real planning failure, and champions deserve honest coverage.

One more word on that criticism, because accountability cuts both ways — ours included. This channel trusted the process all summer, and we still do. But honest coverage means admitting when the plan had a hole: betting a championship center room on Hukporti's loyalty without a fallback was a genuine miscalculation, and the two months of scrambling that followed — the matched offer sheets, the rejected calls, the Nick Richards watch that ended in Miami — were the price of that single assumption. Great front offices aren't the ones that never err; they're the ones that fix errors before they cost banners. February is the repair window. That's where this grade gets finalized.

THE CREDIT, equally plainly: everything ELSE about this offseason was masterful. Leon Rose brought back essentially the entire championship core — the most expensive, most poachable roster in basketball — while staying UNDER the second apron the whole way. He lost only the center room (Robinson, Hukporti, the departed depth) and replaced the margins with smart, cheap bets: Konchar winning a four-team free-agent battle, the Miličić and Robbins camp fliers, the Mascari pipeline trade, Alvarado and Diawara locked up below market. The Brunson-discount culture held. The trade chips stayed polished. And the one hole that remains is the one hole he's been openly working to fill via trade since June. So the final grade: an A-minus offseason with one glaring F on a single line item — and a front office that KNOWS it, which is why the phones haven't stopped. Perspective matters: check the title odds — OKC at +240, the Spurs at +280, and the champs tied with Philly at +1000, ahead of Boston, Toronto, Detroit, Cleveland, Denver. Thirteen rostered players, one unfinished position... and still every rival's measuring stick. If this is what a "flawed" offseason looks like, most franchises would kill for it.
