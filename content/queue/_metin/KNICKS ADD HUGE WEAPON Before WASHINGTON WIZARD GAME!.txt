## WHAT THE WIZARDS GAME EXPOSED

Let's start with the problem, because a weapon only matters if it solves something. This summer, Mitchell Robinson left New York and signed with the Boston Celtics. That changed the whole structure of the frontcourt. The Knicks' center depth right now is Karl-Anthony Towns, then Andre Drummond, and then an open battle for the third spot between James Wiseman, Drew Eubanks, and Tony Bradley.

Against Washington, the cracks showed. KAT had a rough night: eight points, seven rebounds, three assists, two-of-seven shooting, and three turnovers, and he lost his matchup with Anthony Davis. Drummond was excellent on the glass — eight points, nine rebounds, four assists, two steals in 20 minutes — but Drummond is a rebounder and a battler, not a vertical shot-blocker who scares people away from the rim. Wiseman gave good minutes again, with two blocks in nine minutes. But that's a lot of hope resting on a player who's just ten months removed from an Achilles tear.

And Washington isn't the only problem. Look at the East. Philadelphia added LeBron James and Jaylen Brown. Miami has Giannis Antetokounmpo. Boston has Paul George and now Mitch. Cleveland still has Jarrett Allen and Evan Mobley. Detroit is coming off a 60-win season with size everywhere. Every road to another banner runs through big, physical frontcourts. And what's the one thing the Knicks lost this summer? Their best rim protector. That's the hole. Now let's look at the weapon.

## THE WEAPON IN FIVE NUMBERS

Instead of throwing adjectives at you, let's do this with five numbers that tell you everything about Ryan Kalkbrenner.

**Number one: 7'1".** That's his height, and he plays every inch of it. He's a true center — not a stretch four, not a tweener. He lives in the paint, he walls off the rim, and he gives you the size that the Knicks lost when Mitch walked out the door.

**Number two: 4.** That's how many Big East Defensive Player of the Year awards he won at Creighton. Four. That's a record. And here's why it matters for the NBA: you don't win that award four times by being athletic for one season. You win it with timing, positioning, and discipline — skills that travel to the next level. Kalkbrenner's defense isn't built on wild jumping. It's built on being in the right spot every single time.

**Number three: 34.** That's where he was drafted in 2025 — the 34th pick, second round. Which means teams passed on him 33 times. And he still made them regret it. He played his way out of a two-way contract and into a real role in Charlotte. That's the kind of story this Knicks front office loves. Remember, this is the organization that built a champion with players other teams undervalued.

**Number four: 75.3.** That's his field-goal percentage as a rookie last season. Seventy-five point three percent. Over 69 games, he averaged 7.6 points, 5.5 rebounds, and 1.5 blocks in about 21 minutes per game. He doesn't take bad shots. He catches lobs, finishes dump-offs, and puts back misses. On a team with Jalen Brunson collapsing defenses every night, a center who converts three out of every four shots is a dream.

**Number five: 2nd.** That's where he finished among all rookies in win shares last season — second, behind only Kon Knueppel. Think about that. A 34th pick on a rebuilding team finished second in his entire rookie class in one of the most important all-in-one impact numbers. He also helped Charlotte win the 2025 Las Vegas Summer League title. This isn't a project. This is a productive young big who's already proven he belongs.

Put those five numbers together, and you get a player who checks every box the Knicks have been hunting for since June: young, cheap, a true rim protector, efficient, and already experienced in a real NBA rotation.

## WHY CHARLOTTE MIGHT FINALLY LISTEN

Now the big question: why would the Hornets ever trade a player like that? Because Charlotte's whole situation changed this summer.

First, the Hornets traded LaMelo Ball to the Minnesota Timberwolves. In return, they got Naz Reid, an unprotected 2033 first-round pick, three first-round pick swaps, and three future second-round picks. They also moved Miles Bridges this offseason. That's not a team chasing wins this year. That's a team resetting its timeline and collecting assets.

Second, they drafted Hannes Steinbach in the first round this year — a big man who played his college ball at the University of Washington. And here's a fun twist for our title: the Washington connection runs both ways in this story. Steinbach, the Washington product, could be exactly the player who makes Kalkbrenner expendable. Charlotte's own beat coverage has said Steinbach could become a starting-caliber player with more offensive versatility than Kalkbrenner.

Third, Charlotte still has Moussa Diabaté, the big man the Knicks tried to get earlier — remember, the Hornets rejected the Dadiet-for-Diabaté type of framework. So the Hornets have Kalkbrenner, Steinbach, Diabaté, and Naz Reid all fighting for frontcourt minutes. That's a crowded room.

And here's the most important piece. In August, Charlotte Hornets On SI published a column titled, word for word, "The Hornets Should Consider Trading Ryan Kalkbrenner... Just Not Right Now." The argument: seven-foot shot-blockers are rare, so somebody will want him. But the writer advised against trading him in the summer and suggested Charlotte revisit the idea near the trade deadline — especially if Steinbach plays well and Diabaté stays healthy.

Near the trade deadline. Where have we heard that timeline before? That's the exact window Ian Begley described for the Knicks' January pivot. Two franchises, two different problems, and the calendar pointing both of them toward the same weeks in the middle of the season.

## THE COUNTERARGUMENT: CHARLOTTE MAY LEAN ON HIM

Now, fairness. Because there's another side to this, and you deserve both.

After the LaMelo trade, national coverage said the Hornets appeared "poised to lean more heavily on Kalkbrenner during his second season," and Charlotte didn't make any major center addition this summer. Most of their other bigs are really power forwards. So in the short term, Kalkbrenner may be MORE important to Charlotte this season, not less.

And the honest scouting report has weaknesses too. Charlotte's own coverage noted that Kalkbrenner struggled against physicality and offers limited offensive production. He's not a shooter. He's not a post scorer. He's a lob catcher, a rebounder, and a rim protector. If you're expecting him to stop Anthony Davis one-on-one, that's not who he is yet. His value is in rotations, help defense, and making drivers think twice. Against bruising centers, he still has growing to do.

So what does that mean? It means the trade window — if there is one — is probably not right now. It's mid-season, after Charlotte sees what Steinbach can do. And by then, the Knicks will know a lot more about whether Wiseman is the real deal.

## HOW A DEAL COULD WORK UNDER THE APRON

Now the money, because nothing happens in New York this year without the second apron. And here's the beautiful part: Kalkbrenner is one of the few useful big men the Knicks could actually afford.

He's a second-round pick who played his way off a two-way deal, which means he's on a cheap contract. That's exactly what makes him a fit. The Knicks can't add big salaries without breaking Dolan's apron rule. But a low-cost center doesn't blow up the books.

What would New York offer? Here's the analysis — not a report. The Knicks can't trade a first-round pick before 2033, so a first is basically off the table. But they have something many teams don't: a huge stack of second-round picks — up to twelve, as we've covered. For a rebuilding team like Charlotte that just loaded up on future picks in the LaMelo deal, more draft capital is the language they speak. And Charlotte On SI said it themselves: on his own, Kalkbrenner would probably bring back a back-end rotation player or future second-round picks. That's the Knicks' sweet spot.

For salary matching, a small contract like Pacôme Dadiet's — around $2.9 million — could be part of the conversation, even though Charlotte has already turned down one Dadiet-based idea for Diabaté. Different player, different season, different situation. A package of Dadiet plus multiple seconds for a cheap backup center is the kind of deal that could make sense for both sides near the deadline.

And remember the roster spot. In our Cut Day video, we talked about the Knicks possibly leaving the 15th spot open. An open spot means the Knicks could even take back Kalkbrenner without sending a player out — though salary rules still apply. Every small detail of how this roster is built right now keeps the door open for exactly this type of move.

## WHAT IT MEANS FOR WISEMAN, DRUMMOND, AND KAT

So if Kalkbrenner ever arrived, where does he fit? Let's go player by player.

**James Wiseman.** This isn't either-or. Wiseman is earning his spot right now — seven-of-nine from the field and three blocks across two preseason games, and he keeps getting the backup minutes. But he's coming back from an Achilles tear, and the Knicks can't bet a title defense on one player's health. Kalkbrenner would be insurance and competition at the same time. If anything, the two of them would push each other.

**Andre Drummond.** Drummond is the clear number two center, and he's a monster on the glass. But Drummond and Kalkbrenner bring different things. Drummond is about rebounding and physicality. Kalkbrenner is about rim protection and efficiency. Against teams with mobile bigs, Kalkbrenner fits. Against bruisers, Drummond fits. That's a real playoff toolbox.

**Karl-Anthony Towns.** And here's the KAT angle. A young, cheap, defensive center would let KAT spend more minutes at the four, where he can stretch the floor without having to guard the biggest body on every possession. That could keep him fresher for the playoffs. And if the January pivot ever happens and KAT gets moved — which, again, I hope it doesn't — a young rim protector on a cheap deal would be an important piece of the next frontcourt. Either way, Kalkbrenner makes sense. He fits the version of this team with KAT, and the version without him.

## THE NEXT WASHINGTON GAME: HOW THE WEAPON CHANGES IT

Let's bring it back to the Wizards, because the next time these two teams meet, Washington won't be the pushover it used to be. This is a team with Anthony Davis, Trae Young, and the number one pick in AJ Dybantsa. On Thursday, Davis and Young combined for 31 points in about 17 minutes. That's a pick-and-roll machine — Trae probing, AD rolling to the rim or popping to the elbow, Dybantsa attacking closeouts.

So imagine the next Wizards game with a rim protector like Kalkbrenner on the floor in the second unit. When Trae turns the corner, there's a 7-foot-1 shot-blocker waiting at the rim instead of a smaller lineup scrambling to rotate. When Dybantsa drives, he sees length, not an open lane. Kalkbrenner's whole game is built on drop coverage — staying back, protecting the paint, and forcing guards into tough floaters. That's the exact coverage you want against a team that lives on lobs and drives.

Would he stop Anthony Davis by himself? No — and we already said physicality is his biggest weakness. That's still KAT's and Drummond's job. But he'd make everybody else on Washington's roster work harder for every point near the basket. And that's what a weapon does. It doesn't win the game alone. It changes how the other team has to play. On Thursday, the Knicks lost by two points. Two points is a blocked shot. Two points is one contested lob. Two points is exactly the margin a rim protector can swing.

## MY CALL

So what's my honest prediction? I don't think anything happens before the season starts. Charlotte wants to see Kalkbrenner and Steinbach together, and the Knicks want to see if Wiseman's comeback is real. But if Wiseman's health becomes a question, if the Knicks get pushed around by big frontcourts in November and December, and if Steinbach shows Charlotte he can handle the job — then by January or February, this could become one of the smartest deals on the board. I'd put the chances at real but small right now — and growing every time a big man bullies the Knicks the way Anthony Davis did on Thursday.

And here's the bottom line on "weapon": the most dangerous weapons in the NBA aren't always the biggest names. Sometimes they're a 7-foot-1 second-round pick who makes 75 percent of his shots and blocks everything near the rim. That's exactly the kind of weapon a champion needs.

Here's where it stands. The Wizards loss exposed the Knicks' biggest need after Mitchell Robinson left: size and rim protection. Ryan Kalkbrenner — 7'1", four-time Big East Defensive Player of the Year, 75.3 percent shooting and 1.5 blocks as a rookie, second in rookie win shares — fits that need perfectly.
