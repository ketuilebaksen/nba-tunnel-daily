THE McCULLAR VERDICT

Let's set the scene. All summer long, a chunk of this fanbase has been asking the same question: why not bring back Kevin McCullar Jr.? The affectionate nickname floating around was "Josh Hart 2.0" — a hustle guy, a glue guy, an energy piece who could spell Hart and preserve him for the long season. He's still unsigned, the roster still has room, and on the surface it seemed like the easiest reunion in the world.

Well, James L. Edwards of The Athletic just answered that question in his mailbag — and he answered it with a scalpel. A reader asked whether McCullar was the perfect guy to back up Hart, and Edwards' response deserves to be read in full, because every sentence carries weight: "Kevin McCullar Jr. has only been in the NBA for two seasons, but he's 25. He'll turn 26 in March. He's already had knee issues that forced him to drop in the draft. At this point, he is what he is as a player. Sure, maybe with more opportunity there's a way for him to garner more consistency. However, if I'm the Knicks, I'd invest minutes into younger players — 19, 20, 21, or 22-year-olds with upside. Those are the players that will help keep this train on track three years from now."

There it is. Cold, clinical, and honestly... correct. And Edwards went further, performing a full autopsy on the "Hart 2.0" comparison: "I could see why someone believes McCullar can replicate some of what Josh Hart does, but I don't see him ever being a big part of New York's plans. Yes, McCullar plays hard and has some of the hustle qualities. To me, though, that is where the comparisons stop. McCullar is nowhere near the shooter that Josh Hart is — and Hart is a league-average three-point shooter for his career. We have college, G League, and a very small NBA sample size to reach that conclusion from. McCullar isn't the facilitator that Hart is either."

Translation: the hustle is real, but hustle alone doesn't survive on a championship roster. The shooting isn't there, the playmaking isn't there, the body has already betrayed him once — those knee issues are literally why he slid in the draft — and at nearly 26, the "wait for the leap" window has closed. This isn't cruelty; it's mathematics. Every roster spot and every developmental minute on the defending champs is a scarce resource, and the front office refuses to spend either on a finished product with a limited ceiling. Kevin, we genuinely wish you the best wherever you land — the effort was never the question. But the insider has spoken, and it's exactly what we expected: McCullar is not part of New York's future.

THE DOCTRINE BEHIND THE DECISION

Now zoom out, because the McCullar verdict isn't really about McCullar. It's about a RULE. Look at that age range Edwards named: nineteen to twenty-two. That's not a throwaway line — that's the organizational filter. The Knicks aren't just collecting "young players"; they're collecting young players with genuine remaining UPSIDE, players whose best basketball is mathematically still in front of them. A 25-year-old two-year veteran is, in this framework, functionally the same as a 30-year-old: what you see is what you get. And what a champion needs from its margins isn't more known quantities — it's lottery tickets that might still hit.

Why does this matter so much for THIS team specifically? Because of the second apron, the invisible wall around everything New York does. Hear this clearly: the Knicks cannot shop in free agency anymore. Not really. Staying under that apron means the mid-level signings and veteran splashes other contenders make are simply off the menu. So where does tomorrow's rotation come from? Three places only: creative trades, bargain contracts... and the draft-and-develop machine. When your stars age — and they will — you won't replace their supporting cast with money. You'll replace it with players you grew yourself. That's why these small decisions about the 14th and 15th spots on the roster are actually enormous. They're the seed corn of the next half-decade.

And appreciate how far this organization has traveled to even HAVE a doctrine like this. Remember the dark ages we covered in our documentary — two decades of torching draft picks on busts, handing bloated contracts to finished players, mortgaging tomorrow for mediocre today. The old Knicks would have re-signed McCullar in July out of pure sentimentality, picked up every team option reflexively, and wondered why the roster calcified. The new Knicks treat every marginal decision like it matters — because they learned the hard way that the margins are where franchises quietly die. Ruthlessness about the 14th man is a championship habit, not a character flaw.

DADIET: THE CLOCK IS OFFICIALLY TICKING

Which brings us to the name that should feel this report the most: Pacôme Dadiet. Edwards didn't spare him either, and this part reads like a formal notice: "The Knicks are at a crossroads even with someone like Dadiet. He's only 21 and still has room to grow, but he hasn't shown much in his limited game minutes. Now, development happens behind the scenes, not just in games — and maybe the Knicks are seeing more to make them believe he's worth a continued investment. But Dadiet has a $5-million-plus team option for next season. The Knicks need to be diligent about their finances and find ways to have roster flexibility. Dadiet has to show something this year — in game action."

Read between those lines, folks. Dadiet fits the age doctrine perfectly — he's 21, exactly the profile they want to invest in. But the doctrine has a second clause everyone forgets: young players must CONSTANTLY EVOLVE. Youth buys you patience; it doesn't buy you immunity. And that $5 million team option turns this from a philosophical question into a financial one with a literal deadline. If Dadiet shows real growth in actual NBA minutes this season, the option gets picked up and the investment continues. If he doesn't? On a team squeezing every dollar under the apron, a $5 million bet on unrealized potential becomes an obvious place to reclaim flexibility. This is his last audition, and everyone in the building knows it. You're on notice, Pacôme. The talent was never doubted — now show it where it counts.

And let's be concrete about what "showing something" actually means, because it's not about box scores. Nobody expects Dadiet to drop 15 a night on this roster — the minutes won't exist for that. What the staff needs to see is simpler and harder: knock down the open corner threes when the stars create them. Stay attached defensively so Mike Brown can trust him for six-minute stretches without holding his breath. Make the right swing pass, crash the glass, run the floor with purpose. Rotational competence — that's the entire exam. Preseason and garbage time will be his proving grounds early, and if he strings together enough trustworthy stretches, real minutes will follow. The bar isn't stardom. The bar is: don't make us think about you for the wrong reasons. Plenty of 21-year-olds have cleared it. Now it's his turn.

THE CHOSEN ONES: DIAWARA, KOLEK & NICKEL

So who DOES the organization believe in? The picture is sharpening. Mohamed Diawara — extended cheap, already earning real trust, the 3-and-D forward project that's actually progressing on schedule. Tyler Kolek — the body transformation we covered, the summer grind, the guy positioning himself for whatever opportunity the Deuce situation eventually opens. And keep an eye on Tyler Nickel, because the quiet confidence around him is real. He hasn't shown it on an NBA floor yet, but that three-point stroke is legitimate — the kind of shooting that plays on any roster in this league. Give him development time and don't be surprised if he becomes a fixture of this bench in a couple of years. And this trio isn't an accident — it's a supply chain. Kolek arrives via a shrewd draft-night maneuver, Diawara via a late-second-round flier nobody else wanted, Nickel via one of those trade-back moves fans love to complain about. Different acquisition routes, same profile coming out the other end. Notice what all three have in common: the right age, a clearly identifiable NBA skill, and a visible development curve. That's the checklist. McCullar failed it on age and ceiling. Dadiet is passing on age and failing — so far — on visible growth. The machine is consistent even when it's cold.

WHAT THE LEAGUE'S YOUNG STARS TELL US

And if you want proof this youth obsession is the right bet, just look at who's projected to dominate the hustle categories around the league this season. The names at the top of the steals-leader conversation — Cason Wallace, Ausar Thompson, Dyson Daniels — are all YOUNG, hungry, ascending players, exactly the archetype New York is hunting for in its own pipeline. (And a fun footnote for us: OG Anunoby sits at the long end of those odds at 25-to-1 — disrespectful for the most switchable defender alive, and knowing OG's healthy seasons, a sneaky-live number.) The league's margins belong to the young and ravenous. The Knicks know it, and it's exactly why the McCullar and Dadiet verdicts look the way they do.

One fair pushback, though, and I'll say it myself before you do: if the doctrine is draft-and-develop, then New York can't keep PUNTING on drafts — trading down, trading out, treating picks like loose change. I hear you, and you're partly right. But look at the actual results: even when this front office trades back, they keep finding real talent in the shadows — Nickel being the freshest example, Diawara before him, McBride and Mitchell Robinson in the previous era. The scouting engine works. The ask going forward is simply to FEED it, because the aging curve of this championship core makes the math unavoidable: in three years, the bench you'll need won't be signable. It'll have to already be here, grown in-house, on rookie money.

And don't miss how this connects to the roster puzzle we've been tracking all month. The final standard spot and all three two-way slots are still open — and now we know the selection criteria they'll be judged against. Whoever wins those spots in camp won't win them on name recognition or past résumés; they'll win them by fitting the doctrine: young enough to grow, cheap enough to keep, hungry enough to evolve weekly. It also reframes the Dadiet situation one more way — if he's part of the package that eventually lands the young center this front office has hunted all summer, the doctrine is satisfied on BOTH ends of that trade: out goes stalled potential, in comes ascending potential at the position of greatest need. Every road keeps leading back to the same philosophy.

THE BOTTOM LINE

So here's the doctrine in one breath: be young, be cheap, and NEVER stop improving — or be gone. McCullar's time expired before it started. Dadiet has one season to justify the faith. Diawara, Kolek, and Nickel carry the torch. And behind all of it stands a front office that just proved — with a parade — that ruthless clarity about the margins of a roster is exactly how you build a champion... and exactly how you keep one.
