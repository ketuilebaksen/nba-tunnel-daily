## STOCK ONE: ANDRE DRUMMOND — RATING: BUY

Let's start with the biggest question this team brought into training camp: who replaces Mitchell Robinson? Mitch was the longest-tenured Knick, a fan favorite, and he left in free agency for the Boston Celtics. That hurt twice. You lose a beloved player, and you lose him to a direct rival in the East.

A few weeks later, Leon Rose answered by signing Andre Drummond — a New York native and a veteran who has built his entire career on owning the glass. And the reaction was split right down the middle. One group of fans believed Drummond could copy the best part of Robinson's game: relentless work on the offensive boards. The other group worried about two things — his ability to finish near the rim, and his defense, especially in the pick-and-roll.

Two preseason games in, the early evidence is leaning toward the optimists. Let's look at the chart.

**Game one, Monday, October 5th, in Philadelphia** — against the 76ers, one of his former teams. The Knicks lost, but Drummond gave Mike Brown exactly what a backup center should. In 11 minutes: six points, five rebounds, one assist, and THREE blocks. And one of those blocks came on a jumper in the corner — a play big men almost never make. He was active guarding the pick-and-roll, which was the biggest question mark about his game. He contested shots all night, his rebounding was elite as usual, and he kept setting hard, solid screens.

**Game two, Thursday, against Washington at the Garden.** Another loss, the Knicks now 0–2, but another step forward for the big man. With Brown giving his main rotation players more time, Drummond played 20 minutes. He finished with eight points on two-of-six shooting, nine rebounds — FIVE of them offensive — four assists, and two steals. The four assists stood out, because Drummond isn't known as a passer, and they gave the second unit another way to keep the ball moving.

And on defense, he again looked comfortable against the pick-and-roll. On one possession, he stepped up as the lone help defender, got level with the screen, and put himself in position to bother the roll man. That's awareness. That's the exact thing the doubters said he couldn't do.

## THE DRUMMOND-ALVARADO PAIRING

Here's a detail I love from Thursday. That was Jose Alvarado's first preseason game. And watching Alvarado's constant pressure on the ball, with Drummond's size sitting behind him, you could see the outline of one of the most fun defensive pairings on any bench in the league. Alvarado picks up full court, harasses the ball-handler, forces him into a bad decision — and if the guard escapes, there's a massive rebounding machine waiting behind the play. Pressure in front, size in back. Opposing second units are going to hate that combination.

## THE RISKS IN THE DRUMMOND CHART

Now, every good analyst shows you the risks too. And there are real ones.

First, the free throws. In Philadelphia, Drummond went two-of-five at the line. That matters, because better foul shooting was one of the reasons some people believed he could actually be an upgrade over Robinson in certain areas. It's not a high bar — but he has to clear it.

Second, the finishing. Through two games, Drummond is shooting four-of-11 from the field, including zero-of-two from three. And that's where the biggest gap with Robinson shows up. Mitch was explosive around the rim. Drummond, at this stage of his career, isn't. Several times already, he's caught a pass or grabbed a rebound right under the basket and lacked the quickness to go straight back up. Those plays turn into extra dribbles or pump fakes, and that gives the defense time to recover.

Third, the three-point temptation. Drummond had a career year from deep last season — but he still shot only 35.6 percent from beyond the arc. The Knicks would be smart to keep him from drifting out to the perimeter and falling in love with that shot. His value lives near the basket. Every three he takes is a rebound he's not in position to grab.

And fourth, the minutes. Twenty minutes on Thursday is probably more than he'll see on a normal regular-season night. The exceptions will be games when Karl-Anthony Towns gets into foul trouble — and over an 82-game season, that will happen.

## WHY IT'S STILL A BUY

So why "buy" with all those risks? Because the Knicks don't need Drummond to BE Mitchell Robinson. That would be ideal, but it's not required. This roster won the 2026 NBA championship, and it's deep enough to absorb the loss of one player, even one as popular as Mitch. What New York needs from Drummond is simple: control the boards, protect the paint for stretches, set good screens, and don't become a liability in coverage.

So far, he's done all four. His rebounding is exactly as advertised, and his defense has held up better than many expected. If he keeps adding a little playmaking like he showed Thursday, and keeps out-shooting Robinson at the line, the Knicks should feel comfortable with him as the backup center.

Will the front office keep an eye on the market for a possible upgrade before the playoffs? Probably — and if you watched our Kalkbrenner video, you know exactly which kind of player I'd be watching for. But right now, the stock that fans were scared to buy is trending up. Buy rating confirmed.

## STOCK TWO: PACÔME DADIET — RATING: SELL

Now the uncomfortable chart. After Thursday's loss, the Knicks may no longer be able to avoid a hard truth: Pacôme Dadiet's time with the franchise looks like it's coming to an end.

The French wing was selected 25th overall in the first round of the 2024 draft. Two years later, he's become a classic case of a prospect who never took off. Now in his third season, he's taking up a roster spot and a slice of the payroll that the reigning champions could clearly use on someone else.

Here are the numbers. Dadiet is earning about $2.98 million this season, after the Knicks picked up his team option last October. There's another team option for the 2027–28 season, worth about $5.37 million — and the deadline to decide on it is October 31st. Given how little he's shown, it's very hard to see New York picking that one up.

And here's an important connection to something we said before on this channel. In our Cut Day video, we explained that cutting Dadiet wouldn't save the Knicks money, because his salary is guaranteed. That's still true. Waiving him doesn't make the money disappear. Which is exactly why the smart move isn't a cut. It's a trade.

## HOW YOU SELL A STOCK NOBODY WANTS

The real question is whether the Knicks can move him before the regular season starts. And the biggest obstacle is obvious: to trade a player whose value has fallen this far, you need a partner willing to take him. No front office becomes a landing spot for free.

But two factors are working in Leon Rose's favor.

**Factor one: the war chest.** Rose has quietly built a healthy collection of second-round picks — and those are exactly the sweetener that makes a deal like this possible. Remember the draft in late June? A lot of fans criticized the Knicks for passing on intriguing prospects and trading back instead. That decision kept rookie salaries down, but it also stocked the cupboard. Through those moves, Rose picked up five future second-round picks the team didn't own before: 2029 seconds from both Phoenix and Sacramento, a 2032 second from Philadelphia, a 2032 second from Dallas, and a 2033 second, again from Phoenix. That brings the Knicks' total to at least 11 second-round picks between 2027 and 2033.

And here's the precedent. Earlier this offseason, the 76ers needed to move big man Johni Broome — still on his rookie contract, just like Dadiet. Philadelphia had to attach a future second-round pick to get the Los Angeles Clippers to take him. Then the Clippers waived Broome, which made it crystal clear: they wanted the pick, not the player. That's the market for a young player who hasn't developed. Dadiet's value may be low enough that a deal could require more than one second-rounder. For New York, with picks to spare, that's a small cost.

**Factor two: the buyers.** In a pure salary dump, the Knicks would want to send Dadiet out without taking any player back. That means the other team needs an open roster spot and either cap space or a trade exception big enough to fit his contract. According to Spotrac, the Memphis Grizzlies are the only team in the league with cap space right now — about $3.85 million. That makes Memphis an obvious candidate, especially if they want extra picks for their rebuild.

Several other teams hold trade exceptions that could work. The Charlotte Hornets have exceptions worth $4.5 million, $8.2 million, and more than $40 million. The New Orleans Pelicans, who could be heading toward a major reset and might be shopping for picks, own exceptions worth $4.5 million and $7 million. The Clippers, short on draft capital after the league penalties tied to the Kawhi Leonard case, have an exception of just over $3 million — enough to fit Dadiet's salary by the slimmest margin. And the Sacramento Kings, who could be headed for the lottery, hold two exceptions between $4.9 million and $5.4 million.

## WHY SELLING DADIET HELPS EVERYBODY

For the Knicks, the logic is simple. Defending a title requires every roster spot to have a purpose. Clearing Dadiet would open a roster spot for a more useful player. It would also save money, because his salary is higher than a minimum contract would cost. And for a team already above the luxury-tax line, pressed right up against Dolan's second-apron rule, every dollar at the end of the bench matters.

And for the other team? It's not a bad deal at all. They take on a player who won't change their playoff odds, but they get draft assets for their future. And there's some real upside too. A change of scenery, more playing time, and lower expectations could finally help Dadiet show something at the NBA level. Don't forget — he's still only 21 years old. Lots of players have figured it out on their second team. I'd honestly be rooting for him.

So: sell. Not because he's a bad kid, not because the pick was a disaster forever, but because a champion can't afford to wait — and somewhere else, he can.

## STOCK THREE: BRUCE BROWN — RATING: BUY THE DIP

And if Dadiet's spot opens up, there may already be a candidate waiting for it. Bruce Brown arrived at Knicks camp as one of several veterans on a tryout, all chasing what's likely a single open roster spot.

His first preseason appearance, Monday in Philadelphia, didn't leave much of a mark — two points, a turnover, and four fouls in about ten minutes. That was the dip. But when he got his chance against Washington on Thursday, it was a different story. Brown showed exactly the kind of skills that could convince the defending champions to keep him.

The clearest strength is his defense at the point of attack. Among all the fringe players in camp, no single trait has stood out more than Brown's ability to fight through screens and stay in front of the ball. And the numbers back it up. According to BBall Index, Brown rated as the second-best screen navigator last season among the players now on the Knicks roster — behind only Jose Alvarado.

## THE ART OF FIGHTING THROUGH SCREENS

Let's break down WHY he's so good at it, because this is the stuff that wins playoff series.

First, he makes himself skinny. When a screen comes, Brown turns his body and slides through tight spaces, staying attached to the ball-handler without fouling. Second, his lateral quickness lets him keep up with guards — and his long arms give him the reach to recover when he's a step behind. Even when a ball-handler gets an edge, Brown can still bother the shot or the pass. That combination of speed and length is hard to teach.

Third, his hands. They're always active. Sometimes he gets close to the line between smart pressure and reaching in, but he usually stays on the right side of it. He uses the arm away from the ball to guide himself through the screen and make contact with the screener's body — without drawing a whistle. It's almost like he carves out his own lane through the action.

Fourth, the recovery. Once he's through the screen, he doesn't turn his hips the wrong way, he doesn't foul, and he doesn't fall behind the play. He takes a long first step and meets the driver at the exact spot where the ball-handler wants to go.

And it's not just on the ball. Brown has been impressive chasing shooters off the ball too. He turns, gets a hand on the offensive player, and works over the top of the screen. That's attention to detail. Effort is his calling card — and he's leaning on it to earn this spot.

## WHY BROWN FITS THIS TEAM

Think about what Mike Brown wants in the playoffs. When the Knicks face Philadelphia with LeBron, Jaylen Brown, and Anfernee Simons, Boston with its guards, Cleveland with Donovan Mitchell and James Harden — screen navigation is everything. The team that fights through screens forces the other team into tough shots. The team that dies on screens gives up open threes.

Right now, Alvarado is the best screen fighter on the roster. Bruce Brown is second. Imagine those two together on the bench — an energy unit that can pick up full court and stay attached through every screen. Add Drummond behind them, and you've got a second unit built to make opposing guards miserable.

And there's the championship experience. Brown was a key piece on Denver's 2023 title team. He knows what playoff basketball requires. For a defending champion, that matters more than potential.

Now, the defense is only half of his case. The other half is on offense — whether he can handle the ball in short stretches, make the right read, and knock down open shots so teams can't ignore him. That's what the final three preseason games need to show. If he can be even a solid secondary ball-handler, his defense makes him a real rotation option.
