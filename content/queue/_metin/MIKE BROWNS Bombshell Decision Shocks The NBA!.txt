## THE HONEST FRAMING

Quick note, the NBA Tunnel way. Waiving Bruce Brown was a front office transaction — Leon Rose's office makes the roster moves, and the second apron drove the math. But coaches shape those decisions through who they play, who they trust, and who they test. And Mike Brown's choices in that final game were the clearest signal anyone got. That's what we're calling the bombshell. Did it "shock the NBA"? It shocked a lot of people who expected Bruce Brown to make this team. Now let's look at the decisions.

## DECISION ONE: DIAWARA STARTS, NOT BRUCE BROWN

When Hart was scratched, Mike Brown had options. Bruce Brown — a champion with Denver in 2023, a defender Mike Brown himself called "textbook" on the ball — was the obvious veteran choice. Instead, Brown started Mohamed Diawara, the young forward the Knicks signed as a second-round pick.

**What it tells us:** Mike Brown wanted to see the young player with the first unit in a regular-season-like setting. Diawara has earned trust all preseason — remember his chase-down block on Kentavious Caldwell-Pope in the opener. Starting him in the dress rehearsal says the coaching staff sees him as part of this team's present, not just its future. And on a roster pressed against the second apron, a young, cheap, athletic forward who can hold his own with the starters is gold.

## DECISION TWO: ZERO MINUTES FOR BRUCE BROWN

This is the one that told the story. With Hart out, a regular-season-like game, and a final chance to evaluate him, Bruce Brown didn't play at all.

At the time, there were two ways to read it. One: the Knicks already knew exactly what Bruce Brown was and didn't need to see more. Two: the decision was already leaning toward letting him go. Now we know. On Saturday, Shams reported that the Knicks waived him.

And the reason was always the math. Under James Dolan's second-apron mandate, the Knicks had room for exactly one more veteran minimum. To keep both Wiseman and Bruce Brown, they'd need to send salary out in a trade. Ian Begley had said that if the Knicks wanted to keep Brown through a trade, a deal would have to happen by Monday. There was also a theoretical path to carry both on non-guaranteed deals temporarily and decide later — as long as one was released by late November. The Knicks chose clarity instead.

## DECISION THREE: THE DADIET SHOWCASE

While Bruce Brown sat, Pacôme Dadiet got extended run alongside the core. That's not random.

Reports said the Knicks explored moving Dadiet before opening night. He and Tyler Kolek were named as the most logical trade pieces if the Knicks wanted to open room for Bruce Brown. Kolek, by the way, carries a $2.4 million team option for the 2027–28 season — which makes him a cheap, flexible chip too.

But here's what happened: the Knicks kept both. And as we broke down in our last video, there's a smart reason. Free agents who signed this offseason can't be traded until December 15th or three months after signing. That leaves Dadiet, Kolek, and Deuce McBride as the only non-starters the Knicks can move early in the season. Dadiet's roughly $3 million salary is the most useful of those contracts — enough, with draft compensation, to make a deal work for a player like Moussa Diabaté, and enough combined with Deuce's to bring back a player making up to about $6.9 million.

So Mike Brown giving Dadiet real minutes with the starters served two purposes. It gave the coaching staff a look at him in a real setting — and it gave the rest of the league a look at him too. If a trade comes later, that tape helps.

## DECISION FOUR: WISEMAN IS HIS GUY

Every step of camp, Mike Brown made one thing clear with his rotation choices: James Wiseman was the camp big he trusted most. Wiseman was the first camp center off the bench in Philadelphia. The only third center to play against Washington. The starter in Toronto on Tuesday, where he put up 18 points and 10 rebounds. And now he's set to open the season as the third center behind KAT and Andre Drummond.

The connection is real: Mike Brown worked with Wiseman as the top assistant in Golden State. He knows what Wiseman can do. And with Eubanks lost to a torn pectoral muscle and Bradley waived with a hamstring injury, the coach's preferred big also became the last one standing.

## DECISION FIVE: THE ALVARADO EXPERIMENT

One more coaching decision worth noting — and this one might change in the regular season. All preseason, Mike Brown barely played Jose Alvarado alongside Karl-Anthony Towns. Seventy-three seconds together against Washington. Two possessions against Minnesota. Alvarado spent most of his minutes with Drummond and camp bigs — and he struggled, shooting nine-of-25 with seven turnovers across four games.

But last season, Alvarado was at his best with KAT: plus 8.8 per 100 possessions together in the regular season, plus 32.5 in the playoffs. So that preseason split looks like an experiment — testing new combinations in October. When the games count, expect Mike Brown to go back to what works. And the opener is against Philadelphia — the same team Alvarado torched with eight threes last season.

## THE FINALE: WHAT THE STARTERS SHOWED

And let's not forget the actual game, because the main group looked like champions. After Toronto ran a reserve-heavy Knicks lineup out of the building on Tuesday, the full frontcourt was back Thursday — and the difference was obvious.

Karl-Anthony Towns looked like the do-everything center who carried the title run. He took the shots he wanted, controlled the glass, and finished one assist short of a triple-double — days after the Knicks shut down extension talks and with his player-option decision due Monday.

Andre Drummond was just as effective in a smaller window: 14 points, eight rebounds, and four assists in only 15 minutes. And both bigs looked comfortable attacking Toronto center Jakob Poeltl — putting the ball on the floor, facing up, and going right at him in space. That's a mismatch the Knicks will look to exploit all season. The search for a third center has gotten a lot of attention, but Thursday was a reminder: two reliable bigs like KAT and Drummond already give New York more at the position than most teams in the league.

And then the ending: Tyler Kolek's late three bounced on the rim, sat there for a second, and dropped. The Garden erupted. Some of that championship magic is still in the building.

## OUTSIDE THE BUILDING: FOUR TEAMS STILL OVER THE LIMIT

Now let's look beyond the Knicks. Before the season starts on October 20th, every team must trim down to 15 standard contracts or fewer. And four teams are still above that number: the Milwaukee Bucks, the Charlotte Hornets, the Memphis Grizzlies, and the Los Angeles Lakers.

That means useful players could become available at any moment. Here are the names worth watching — and an honest look at whether the Knicks could actually get any of them.

## NAME ONE: MOUSSA CISSÉ

The Knicks tried to pry him away from Dallas this summer with an offer sheet, but the offer was cheap enough that the Mavericks matched it. His salary became fully guaranteed on October 1st, and Dallas already has 15 guaranteed contracts — while longtime Maverick Dwight Powell is pushing for a spot on a non-guaranteed deal. If Dallas needs room, Cissé is at risk.

**The catch:** Because Dallas matched the offer sheet, Cissé can't be traded to New York for a year. Some coverage says the Knicks' interest might not be completely dead if he's released; other reporting says the rules block the Knicks from acquiring him either way. As we said in our last video, I'd treat the Cissé-to-New-York path as very unlikely this season.

## NAME TWO: JERICHO SIMS — A FAMILIAR FACE

This one will make Knicks fans smile. Jericho Sims, the former Knick, opted into his $2.8 million player option with Milwaukee. But the Bucks have 17 players on standard deals, too many big men, and they're heading into a rebuild after trading Giannis Antetokounmpo. Sims could be the odd man out. Last season, he averaged a career-high 19.9 minutes, with 5.0 points and 5.5 rebounds. There have also been rumors about the Lakers showing interest in a deal involving Dalton Knecht, which could reshuffle things further.

**The catch for New York:** Sims is a veteran with too many years of service for a two-way contract, and with Wiseman taking the 14th spot, the Knicks have no room under the apron for another minimum — unless a trade sends salary out. So a Sims reunion would likely require a Dadiet move first.

## NAME THREE: MICAH PEAVY

The 25-year-old was the 40th pick in the 2025 draft and played 61 games for New Orleans as a rookie, including four starts, before being traded to Memphis — one of the teams still over the limit. He's known as a defense-first wing. Because he's early in his career, he could be the kind of player who's still eligible for a two-way contract — though the Knicks' bigger need on a two-way slot is a center, not another wing.

## THE CHARLOTTE ANGLE

And notice who else is on that list of teams over the limit: Charlotte. The Hornets have been at the center of Knicks big-man talk all year — Moussa Diabaté, the name connected to a Dadiet-plus-picks framework, and Ryan Kalkbrenner, whose own local coverage said the Hornets should consider trading him near the deadline. When a team has to cut down, its decision-makers start listening a little more closely. Nothing is reported here, but if you're watching for a Knicks big-man move before the season starts, Charlotte's cutdown is worth tracking.

## HOW THE KNICKS COULD ADD ANYONE

Let's be clear about the mechanics, because this is where fan dreams meet the apron.

**Path one: a two-way contract.** Doesn't touch the apron. But the player has to be two-way eligible, and the Knicks would have to release one of their current two-ways — Tyler Nickel, Kevin McCullar Jr., or Jaden Akins. This is why a player like former Knick Trey Jemison, waived by Toronto this week and still two-way eligible, makes so much sense as a fourth center.

**Path two: the 15th roster spot.** Only possible if the Knicks send out salary first — which is exactly why keeping Dadiet's contract mattered.

**Path three: wait for December 15th.** When the trade market opens wider, the Knicks will have more options — and they kept the contracts they need to use them.

## WHAT THESE CHOICES SAY ABOUT MIKE BROWN

Step back and look at all five decisions together, and a clear coaching philosophy comes through.

First, he meant what he said. Back in camp, after a practice he didn't like, Mike Brown named five players — Brunson, Bridges, OG, KAT, and Hart — and told everyone else they were fighting, either to make the team or to earn minutes. He said it had nothing to do with last season. Every choice in the final week matched that message. No one outside the starting five got a free pass, not even a champion like Bruce Brown.

Second, he trusts youth more than people expected. Diawara started the dress rehearsal. Kolek got crunch-time minutes and hit the winning shot. Dadiet played with the core. For a coach defending a title, that's a willingness to invest in young players that a lot of contenders don't show.

Third, he values relationships and fit. Wiseman wasn't just the most productive camp big — he was the one Mike Brown knew. The coach saw him in Golden State, knows his strengths and weaknesses, and believes the right situation can unlock him. That trust gave Wiseman the minutes to prove it.

And fourth, he's willing to experiment in October to be ready in April. The Alvarado lineups, the different combinations around Drummond — that's a coach stress-testing his roster before the games count.

## A FAREWELL TO BRUCE BROWN

Before we move on, a proper send-off for Bruce Brown. He came into camp on a non-guaranteed deal after a summer where, in his own words, the second apron kept coming up as the reason teams wouldn't commit. He said he'd been on eight teams in nine years and had to prove himself every season. He told the Post he was "fighting for a job" and would happily accept any role. He played harder than almost anyone in camp, earned praise from the head coach and the captain, and still lost out — not because of his play, but because of a financial line drawn by ownership. That's the second-apron era in one story. He'll land somewhere fast, and whichever team gets him is getting a champion. Knicks fans should remember him well.

## THE OPENING-NIGHT QUESTIONS FOR MIKE BROWN

Now the decisions get real. Here's what I'll be watching from the coach on Tuesday night against Philadelphia.

**Is Hart back?** The ankle didn't look serious, and he had nearly five full days of rest. If he's out, does Diawara start again?

**Who's the first big off the bench?** Drummond is the clear backup to KAT, but how quickly does Wiseman get his first regular-season minutes?

**How much do Alvarado and KAT share the floor?** If the coach goes back to last season's formula, Alvarado's preseason struggles could disappear fast.

**Is Kolek in the rotation?** With Brunson, Deuce, Shamet, Clarkson, and Alvarado ahead of him, Kolek might be on the outside — but his finale shot earned him a longer look.

**Who closes?** In a tight game, does Mike Brown trust Deuce, Shamet, or Hart down the stretch alongside the core four?

Opening night won't answer everything. But it'll show us which of Mike Brown's preseason ideas survive into the regular season.

## MY CALL

Here's my take on Mike Brown's final-week decisions: every one of them made sense. Diawara earned his look. Wiseman earned his spot. Bruce Brown was a victim of the apron, not of his play. Dadiet got a showcase that could help a future trade. And the Alvarado-KAT pairing will come back when it matters. As for the waiver wire, I don't expect the Knicks to add a standard-contract player before Tuesday — but I wouldn't be shocked by a two-way move for a big man, and I'll be watching Charlotte's and Milwaukee's cuts closely.
