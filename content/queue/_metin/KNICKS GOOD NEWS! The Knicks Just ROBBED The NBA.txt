## ACCOUNTABILITY FIRST

In our last video, I said I thought Knecht would probably sign somewhere with a clearer path to minutes — and that if the Knicks opened a two-way spot, they'd likely go for a center first. I was wrong on both. Knecht picked New York, and the front office chose talent over position. What I did get right: if it happened, Kevin McCullar Jr. was the most likely player to be waived. That's exactly how it played out. Now let's get into why Knecht chose the Knicks — and why the Knicks chose him.

## THE MOVE

Here's what happened. The Lakers waived Knecht on Monday to get down to 15 players before opening night. They had tried to trade him and couldn't find a deal, so they cut him and ate his salary instead. He cleared waivers — meaning no team wanted to take on his contract. But once he became a free agent, a lot of teams wanted him. According to Hoops Rumors, the Kings, Timberwolves, and Raptors were all interested. Jake Fischer had said a handful of teams saw him as a two-way target.

And Knecht picked New York. Shams reports he's agreed to a two-way deal with the Knicks. To open the spot, the Knicks are waiving Kevin McCullar Jr.

## THE ROBBERY MATH

Now here's why people are saying the Knicks robbed the NBA. Look at the money.

**The Lakers' side:** Last fall, the Lakers picked up Knecht's third-year option, which made his $4.2 million salary for this season fully guaranteed. By waiving him, they're still on the hook for that money. And they also lose the $6.45 million team option they held on him for next year. So the Lakers are paying a former first-round pick not to play for them — and giving up control of him for the future.

**The Knicks' side:** A two-way contract doesn't count against the salary cap at all. No cap hit. And remember, the Knicks are only about $3.2 million below the second apron — the line James Dolan refuses to cross. That's why they couldn't keep Bruce Brown. That's why they couldn't add a standard contract. But with a two-way deal, they just added a former first-round pick without adding a single dollar to their payroll math.

The Lakers used the 17th overall pick on him two years ago. The Knicks got him for a two-way slot. That's the robbery.

## HOW A TWO-WAY CONTRACT WORKS

Quick rules, so everyone's on the same page. On a two-way deal, Knecht can be active for up to 50 NBA games this season, and he'll split time between the Knicks and the Westchester Knicks in the G League. He can't play in the playoffs unless the Knicks convert him to a standard contract. So this is a development-and-depth move — with a real chance to become more if he plays his way into it.

## WHO IS DALTON KNECHT? A 2K MY CAREER STORYLINE

Knecht's road to the NBA was anything but normal. Honestly, it reads like an NBA 2K MyCareer storyline. He started at a junior college. Then he transferred to Northern Colorado. Then, for his final college season, he transferred to Tennessee — and exploded. He averaged 21.7 points per game, was named SEC Player of the Year, and earned consensus first-team All-American honors. That got him drafted 17th overall by the Lakers in 2024.

From junior college to a first-round pick. That's a player who's used to proving people wrong.

## THE RECORD NIGHT AND THE ROOKIE YEAR

And as a rookie, he hit the ground running. In November 2024, he scored 27 points against the Pelicans. Three nights later, he dropped 37 on the Utah Jazz — with nine threes on 12 attempts, tying the NBA record for most threes in a game by a rookie.

Over the full rookie season, he played 78 games with 16 starts, averaging 9.1 points in about 19 minutes a night. He shot 37.6 percent from three on more than four attempts per game, with a true shooting percentage of 59.4 — really efficient for a rookie. And he had a 32-point game against the Denver Nuggets in March, which shows you the ceiling is real.

## THE SHOT PROFILE: BUILT FOR THE KNICKS

Here's a stat that tells you exactly what kind of player he is: as a rookie, 61 percent of his shots were threes, and 92 percent of the threes he made were assisted. He's a catch-and-shoot guy. You find him, and he lets it fly.

But he's not just a spot-up shooter. As a rookie, he finished well at the rim and threw down 45 dunks. When he gets out in transition, he can finish. That's a real athlete with a real jumper — and that's hard to find on a two-way contract.

Even better for New York: he hit 40.7 percent of his corner threes as a rookie, and he rarely turns it over — his turnover rate was just 5.9 percent.

Now think about Mike Brown's offense. Brown loves what he calls the "spray three" — someone drives into the paint, the defense collapses, and you kick it out to an open shooter. Jalen Brunson draws two defenders on almost every drive. KAT pulls bigs away from the rim and runs the offense from the top of the key. Both are excellent passers. That's exactly where Knecht makes his money. There might not be a better team in the league for his skill set — and that might be one reason he chose the Knicks over the other teams in the mix.

## WHAT WENT WRONG IN LOS ANGELES

So how does a player like that get cut two years later? It started at the 2025 trade deadline. The Lakers traded Knecht to the Charlotte Hornets in a deal for center Mark Williams. Two days later, the trade was rescinded because Williams failed his physical, and Knecht awkwardly came right back to Los Angeles.

And look at what that trade said about how the Lakers saw him. They put him in a package with Cam Reddish, a future first-round pick, and a pick swap. Knecht wasn't the prize anymore. He was the salary and the young player that made the trade work — after the Lakers had moved Anthony Davis for Luka Dončić and needed a big man.

Imagine being a rookie. Your first year in the league. You get traded, you start packing your bags, and then you're told: never mind, go back and keep playing for the team that just tried to get rid of you. That's an incredibly awkward situation. And his role started shrinking from there.

## THE DECLINE — AND THE DEFENSE

The numbers tell the story. His minutes were nearly cut in half, from 19.2 a game to 10.2. Last season, he played 54 games with one start and averaged 4.2 points. His three-point percentage dropped to 34.2.

And defense has been the knock on him the whole way. His defensive box plus-minus was negative both years: minus 1.7 as a rookie, minus 1.6 last season — no real improvement yet. This preseason, the same problems showed up: according to Sports Illustrated, he shot just 25 percent from the field in four preseason games. The Lakers ultimately chose to keep players like Bronny James and Jaden Hardy over him.

So let's be honest: he's not a finished product, and he's not guaranteed to bounce back.

## THE STAT THAT SAYS HE CAN STILL SCORE

But here's the stat I keep coming back to. Even in that bad second year, when he did shoot, he was still efficient: a 56.8 true shooting percentage and 61 percent on his two-pointers. He didn't forget how to score. He just stopped getting the ball, and stopped being a real part of the offense.

That's the difference between a player who lost his skill and a player who lost his role. Knecht looks like the second one. Shooters live on rhythm and confidence. When your minutes drop from 19 to 10 a night and you're never sure if you'll play, it's almost impossible to find either. And the Knicks are betting that the right role brings the shooter back.

## WHY THIS IS A SMART MOVE

Let's lay it out.

**One: zero risk.** Sports Illustrated called it a low-cost lottery ticket with a big potential payoff. If it doesn't work, it costs the Knicks nothing against the cap.

**Two: the Knicks need young talent, especially on the wing.** Outside of Mohamed Diawara and Tyler Kolek — neither of whom is firmly in the rotation — there isn't much young talent on this roster. Add Knecht to a pipeline that already includes Jaden Akins, Tyler Nickel, and Dink Pate in Westchester.

**Three: the apron future.** If Knecht shoots well, the Knicks could eventually give him a longer-term contract that's extremely cheap relative to his value — exactly what a team trying to stay under the second apron needs.

**Four: the track record.** The Knicks have done this before. Isaiah Hartenstein wasn't finding his role with the Clippers. Landry Shamet nearly fell out of the league. Donte DiVincenzo was solid but unspectacular before he broke out in New York. All of them found their best basketball at the Garden.

**Five: the immediate need.** In the opener, the Knicks shot 33 percent from three. OG went one-of-nine from deep. Mikal Bridges barely shot. There was a lot of criticism about the lack of three-point production. Knecht solves that kind of problem instantly, because he's never afraid to let it fly.

And he's 25 years old with 132 NBA games of experience. Not a raw rookie. A player who already knows the league.

## WHY HE CHOSE NEW YORK

Think about this from Knecht's side for a second. The Kings, Timberwolves, and Raptors were interested too. Some of those teams could probably promise more immediate minutes than a defending champion with a deep wing rotation. So why the Knicks?

First, the fit. As we just covered, Mike Brown's spray-three offense, built around Brunson's drives and KAT's passing, is a perfect environment for a catch-and-shoot wing. Second, the development track record. Shamet, Hartenstein, DiVincenzo, Drummond — the Knicks have a reputation for reviving careers. Third, the winning culture. After an awkward exit from Los Angeles, landing with the champions is a fresh start with a real purpose. And fourth, the stage. If Knecht gets hot in Madison Square Garden, everyone will notice.

For a player trying to prove that his rookie year wasn't a fluke, New York might be the best place in the league to do it.

## HOW MIKE BROWN COULD USE HIM

So what does Knecht's role look like in practice? Here's my projection — clearly analysis, not a report.

**The emergency shooter.** On nights when the wings go cold — like opening night — Knecht is a guy Brown can bring in for a few minutes just to change the spacing. Even two threes in a short stint can swing momentum.

**The corner specialist.** With Brunson and KAT drawing so much attention, the corners are where open shots live. Knecht's 40.7 percent on rookie corner threes is exactly what the Knicks want there.

**The transition weapon.** Put him with Jose Alvarado, who pressures the ball and creates turnovers. Alvarado steals it, pushes, and Knecht runs the wing for a catch-and-shoot three or a dunk.

**The Westchester go-to.** In the G League, he'll likely be the focal point — lots of shots, lots of reps, lots of confidence-building. That's where the rhythm comes back.

## WHAT KNECHT HAS TO FIX

Of course, there's a price of admission for minutes on a Mike Brown team: defense. Brown has made that clear to everyone outside the starting five. Knecht's defensive numbers have been negative both seasons, and that's the first thing he has to address.

The good news is that a lot of defensive improvement comes from effort and positioning, not just athleticism — and Knecht is athletic enough to hold his own if he's locked in. Westchester will give him a chance to work on rotations, closeouts, and staying in front of wings. If he can become even a neutral defender, his shooting makes him playable. If he stays a liability, his minutes will stay limited. That's the deal.

## FAREWELL, KEVIN McCULLAR JR.

Now, let's give McCullar a proper send-off, because he deserves it. He's 25, and this was his third straight season on a two-way deal with the Knicks. He just got a championship ring. Last season, he played 21 NBA games at about seven minutes a night, averaging 2.4 points, and he showed real defensive ability. In Westchester, he was excellent — in 10 G League games, he put up 17 points, five rebounds, and 6.7 assists per game.

But the jumper never came. This preseason, he went zero-for-six from three across three games. And for a wing on this roster, the shooting has to be there. When the Knicks had the chance to swap him for a former SEC Player of the Year with a rookie record on his résumé, they took it.

If you watched our "McCullar Crossroads" video, you know we saw this risk coming: the two-way deal that made him easy to keep also made him easy to replace. That doesn't make it any less tough. McCullar did everything right in Westchester, earned a ring, and represented this organization well. He'll get another shot somewhere. And we're rooting for him.

## THE QUESTION THAT REMAINS: A TWO-WAY CENTER

One thing this move doesn't solve: the Knicks still have no center on a two-way contract. Their two-ways are now Knecht, Nickel, and Akins — all perimeter players. Center depth behind KAT and Drummond is still just James Wiseman. And after KAT's foul trouble on opening night forced Drummond into heavy minutes, that's still something to watch. The front office clearly decided that Knecht's talent was too good to pass up. If injuries hit the frontcourt, they'll have to revisit the center question.

## WHO GETS CONVERTED FIRST?

And here's a fun question for the season: which two-way player earns a standard contract first? Knecht immediately becomes a favorite because of his pedigree and his shooting. But don't sleep on Tyler Nickel — the Knicks invested in him for a reason, and he's a shooter too. And Jaden Akins has the front office's attention with his defense and approach. That's a healthy competition — and exactly what Mike Brown wants when he tells everyone outside the starting five that they're fighting for minutes.

## MY CALL

Here's my take. This is one of the smartest low-cost moves the Knicks have made all year. Knecht spends time in Westchester rebuilding his confidence, gets called up for spot minutes when the Knicks need shooting, and by the second half of the season he's pushing for a bigger role. If the corner three comes back, he's the kind of player who could earn a cheap, team-friendly standard contract — the exact type of deal this apron-squeezed roster needs. The Lakers paid for him. The Knicks get to keep the upside. And if it doesn't work out? They lost nothing but a two-way slot. That's what a smart front office looks like in the second-apron era.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

Here's where it stands. Shams reports Dalton Knecht has agreed to a two-way deal with the Knicks, who are waiving Kevin McCullar Jr. to make room. The Lakers waived Knecht on Monday and are still paying his guaranteed $4.2 million while losing a $6.45 million option, and his two-way deal carries no cap hit for a Knicks team about $3.2 million below the second apron. The Kings, Timberwolves, and Raptors were also interested, but Knecht chose New York. He's a former SEC Player of the Year and the 17th pick in 2024, who tied the rookie record with nine threes in a game and shot 37.6 percent from deep as a rookie — before a rescinded trade for Mark Williams sent his role into decline. Even in his down year, he posted a 56.8 true shooting percentage. Defense is the question. Shooting is the answer. And for the Knicks, it's free.
