## LENS ONE: THE WRIST

Let's start with the question every Knicks fan had in the back of their mind this summer. Brunson had wrist surgery this offseason — on his SHOOTING hand. He's earned a reputation as one of the most dependable players in the league, so not many people panicked when the procedure was announced. But surgery on the shooting hand of a 30-year-old guard is not something you just ignore.

So far, there's very little reason for concern. Through two preseason games, Brunson is averaging 12.5 points while shooting 50 percent from the field. He had a rough night from deep against Washington, going just one-of-six from three. But the overall picture looks like a player already close to midseason form.

And here's the part that matters even more than the numbers: he's not playing with any hesitation. He's attacked the basket. He's hit his floaters. He's pulled up from midrange. And he's kept firing from beyond the arc even after the misses. A player worried about his wrist doesn't keep shooting through a cold night. On defense, he's competed hard and already drawn a few of those offensive fouls that have become one of his trademarks. Once again, Brunson looks like the least of New York's worries. And that's the foundation everything else in this video sits on.

## LENS TWO: THE SHAMET SURGE

Now let's look at the guy making Brunson's job easier off the bench. Landry Shamet is turning heads, and his hot start could matter a lot more than a typical October storyline.

Rewind the tape. On September 14th, 2024 — just over two years ago — Shamet joined the Knicks on an Exhibit 9 contract. A simple training camp deal that offered him nothing more than a chance to compete for one of the last roster spots. Nobody inside the organization could have predicted what came next. Twenty-five months later, Shamet isn't just a solid NBA player. He's a reigning champion and a trusted part of the rotation that brought New York its first title since 1973. And that journey earned him a new four-year, $24 million contract this summer.

But a new deal brought new questions. Could he stay on the court after dealing with injuries in both of his seasons in New York? Were the defensive gains he showed last year real, or just a hot stretch? And what about the shooting? Over his career, Shamet has finished a season above 38 percent from three only four times.

So far, he's answered every single one of those questions. Through two preseason games: 23 points in just 27 combined minutes. Eight-of-15 from the field. Five-of-nine from three. The Knicks' official account even celebrated his back-to-back threes in Philadelphia on October 5th. And Ian Begley pointed out how remarkable it is that a player who spent the last two preseasons fighting just to make the team now stands out as one of New York's most impressive performers this fall.

Think about that journey from Brunson's perspective. When you're the guy who draws two defenders on every drive, the most valuable thing in the world is a teammate who punishes the help. Shamet is that guy right now. Every kick-out becomes a dagger.

## SHOULD SHAMET START? THE NUMBER THAT SAYS NO

Naturally, some fans have started asking: what would the starting lineup look like with Shamet in place of Josh Hart?

That change is very unlikely, and there's a number that explains why. Last season, a lineup with Shamet alongside Brunson, Mikal Bridges, OG Anunoby, and Karl-Anthony Towns posted a negative 3.7 net rating across 114 minutes. That's not a tiny sample. More shooting on paper didn't translate into winning basketball. Hart's rebounding, toughness, and connective passing do things for that unit that don't show up as clearly in the box score. And Mike Brown, who's willing to sit Hart in certain moments, values him highly and has kept him in the starting group. So no — the surge doesn't change the starting five. Where it could change things is the sixth-man job.

## LENS THREE: THE SIXTH-MAN QUESTION AND DEUCE

No team is required to name an official sixth man. But most contenders have a reserve who's starter quality, and who's often trusted to close games. Recently, the Knicks have split that role between Miles "Deuce" McBride and Shamet.

And here's where it gets interesting. McBride has struggled with his shot in the first two exhibition games. Now, there's little reason to panic. Deuce has been a reliable outside shooter for a couple of years. But he's also coming off an NBA Finals series against the San Antonio Spurs where he looked outmatched on offense. Part of that can be explained by a sports hernia that McBride later revealed was still bothering him during that series. So there's context. But context doesn't stop a coach from riding the hot hand.

If McBride's struggles continue, Shamet could start separating himself as the clear sixth man. That wouldn't push Deuce out of the rotation — at most, he might lose a few minutes a night. But if both players keep trending in their current directions, there's a real case for Shamet playing more than the 23.0 minutes per game he averaged last season. Deuce remains the better defender. Shamet competes hard on that end, and has a slight size advantage.

## THE DEUCE TRADE ANGLE — HANDLE WITH CARE

And now the angle that makes this more than a rotation story. Many observers believe McBride is simply too valuable to move. He's an elite defensive backup who's shot the ball very well from three over the last couple of seasons. Trading him would be a risky choice for a team whose only goal is another championship.

But if the front office ever decided it needed to get value for Deuce before he could leave in free agency — and you know this channel has covered that possibility, with his contract running out and the apron squeezing everything — a stronger, more consistent Shamet would make that decision much easier to accept. That's the hidden value of this surge. Shamet's $24 million deal isn't just a reward for the past. It might be insurance for the future.

To be clear: nobody has reported that a Deuce trade is coming. And honestly, two preseason games are a tiny sample. Deuce's shooting will very likely bounce back, and Shamet's red-hot touch will almost certainly cool down. Nobody should make major decisions based on a few October nights. But context matters. Shamet has faced uncertainty most of his career — short contracts, injuries, roster battles. Now he's producing on a team trying to become the first back-to-back champion since 2018. Finding out your depth is even stronger than expected is a big deal.

## LENS FOUR: THE FOUR SIGNALS FROM THE STARTING FIVE

The Knicks are 0–2 in the preseason after losses to Philadelphia on October 5th and Washington on October 8th. For the defending champions, those results mean almost nothing. What matters in October is how the core looks, even in limited minutes. Beyond Brunson's wrist, three more signals stand out — and every one of them affects Brunson's life on the court.

**Signal one: OG lives at the line.** It feels like OG Anunoby has been in the league forever, but he's still only 29. Since arriving in New York in December 2023, he's steadily grown his game — a better midrange shooter, a stronger driver, a more confident three-point shooter above the break. And this preseason, one new trend jumps out: OG is getting to the free-throw line. He leads the Knicks with 5.5 free-throw attempts per game, and he's doing it in just 16.6 minutes a night. Drawing fouls is a real skill that many players never develop. It's a small sample. But if it lasts, OG — a strong free-throw shooter — becomes more efficient, the whole team does too, and it takes scoring pressure off Brunson.

**Signal two: KAT is still the hub.** During last season's title run, the Knicks started running more of the offense through Karl-Anthony Towns, and that change became one of the turning points of the playoffs. The question this fall was whether that approach would stick, or whether it was a postseason fix born out of necessity — and whether the team would drift back to a Brunson-isolation offense. The early answer is encouraging. Especially in the opener in Philadelphia, KAT spent a lot of time in the high post, starting possessions with passes and running dribble handoffs. And this is happening while Towns is openly frustrated about not getting his extension. On the court, the contract talk hasn't touched his role as a playmaker. What made the Knicks so dangerous last spring was that they executed this style at a high level without even leaning on it in the regular season. Now, with a full camp and a full schedule, they have time to sharpen it. For Brunson, that means fewer possessions where he has to create everything alone.

**Signal three: Bridges is attacking.** After a strong postseason, Mikal Bridges is on great terms with Knicks fans. But one complaint has followed him: he tends to avoid contact on drives. In the playoffs, he was noticeably more physical on both ends — attacking the rim and absorbing hits. Early this preseason, there are positive signs that version is carrying over. Several times, Bridges has driven straight to the basket instead of settling, and he's said publicly that attacking more is something he wants to do. He's not going to suddenly draw fouls like Brunson or Towns, and he's still fallen back on fadeaways at times. But with his height and wingspan, there's no reason to shy away from contact.

**Signal four: Hart stays.** Josh Hart rounds out the group. Mike Brown has kept the starting five that delivered the title together, and the early returns give him no reason to change.

## LENS FIVE: THE PARTNER BRUNSON LOST

Now the heart of this video. When Mitchell Robinson left this summer, most of the conversation was about money and rebounding. On July 1st, Mitch agreed to a three-year, $47.4 million deal with the Boston Celtics, with a player option in the third season. Working under James Dolan's mandate to stay below the second apron, the Knicks couldn't match that offer while keeping the rest of the championship roster intact.

Losing Mitch's offensive rebounding was the obvious blow. But there's another loss that's gotten far less attention — and it hits Brunson directly.

Brunson and Robinson built one of the most effective pick-and-roll partnerships in the NBA. Sportradar analyst Todd Whitehead recently shared a chart showing every team's most productive screen-and-roll pairing over the past five seasons, measured by points generated above expectation. Brunson and Robinson weren't just New York's top duo. They ranked EIGHTH in the entire league. And that's even more impressive considering the sample: over four seasons together, Brunson and Mitch shared the floor for more than 3,600 minutes across the regular season and playoffs. Mitch was never a high-volume scorer. But that two-man game was a real weapon. And the Knicks don't have it anymore.

Brunson made it clear how much Robinson meant to him. Speaking to the New York Post, he said he was happy for his close friend — while joking that Mitch won't be his friend on the four nights a year these teams play each other. And Robinson told OG on social media that he had tried to stay. Fans won't wait long for the reunion, either: Boston faces New York in the Celtics' second game of the season. Brunson running pick-and-roll against the big man who used to set his screens — circle that one.

## WHY DRUMMOND ISN'T THE ANSWER TO THIS PARTICULAR PROBLEM

So who fills that void? Andre Drummond was signed to back up Karl-Anthony Towns, and he's been good in the preseason — rebounding, defending the pick-and-roll better than expected, even passing. But he doesn't bring the same style as Mitch in this specific area. These days, most of Drummond's interior chances come from cutting in from the corner, putbacks, and post-ups. He's no longer an above-the-rim force who sets a screen and dives hard to the basket, and he hasn't been a positive roll-man threat in some time.

And KAT? Towns is far too skilled to be boxed into a pure rim-runner role. He's the hub, the shooter, the high-post passer. You don't waste that by asking him to just dive to the rim. So the rim-running job — the job Mitch did for Brunson — is open. And that leads straight to the camp battle.

## THE ROSTER MATH: WHY THERE'S ONLY ONE SPOT

Quick clarification on the roster math, because it's important. The Knicks have 13 players on guaranteed deals, and because of the second apron, they can realistically add only one more. That's the 14th spot — and the 15th is likely to stay open, which lines up with what we talked about in our Cut Day video. Competing for that one spot: Drew Eubanks, Bruce Brown, Ochai Agbaji, Tony Bradley, and James Wiseman.

And the insiders are split. Ian Begley of SNY has reported the Knicks could hand the spot to Eubanks because he's the safest option. Stefan Bondy of the New York Post has reported that Wiseman has impressed in camp and should be considered the favorite.

## LENS SIX: THE RIM-RUNNER AUDITION — EUBANKS VS. WISEMAN

So let's judge the two bigs the way Brunson would: who makes the pick-and-roll better?

**Drew Eubanks.** He's a reasonable third big. And here's something interesting: he actually screens more like Robinson than Wiseman does. But he lacks the vertical threat that pulls defenses toward the rim. After setting a screen, he's more likely to trail the play or drift into the middle of the floor. That can work if a team wants a big who makes decisions on the short roll or tosses up a quick floater. But it also means he could end up standing in the same space Brunson likes to attack. And the numbers back that up: over the past six seasons, Eubanks has ranked better than 125th in roll-man points over expectation per 100 possessions only ONCE.

**James Wiseman.** His track record isn't much stronger on paper. He's logged enough possessions to qualify for those rankings only three times, and finished inside the top 125 just once. But in that one season, he ranked 46th. His ceiling is clearly higher — which is what you'd expect from the younger player in the competition. And even coming back from an Achilles injury, his style looks much closer to what Robinson provided. He may slip screens more often than Eubanks, but his rolls and cuts tend to carry him all the way to the rim, and he's a far more natural lob target. In 2022–23, the last season he was healthy and in a regular role, more than 46 percent of his made baskets came on alley-oops or dunks. That's slightly higher than the 43 percent Eubanks posted the last time he played at least 1,000 minutes in a season.

Wiseman is still only 25. He was the number two overall pick in the 2020 draft and has played for Golden State, Detroit, and Indiana. In 152 career games, including 56 starts, he's averaged 9.2 points and 5.5 rebounds — numbers that explain why many people still see him as a draft disappointment. But there's a familiar face in his corner: Mike Brown was an assistant in Golden State during Wiseman's time there, and reports say Brown is a fan. And in this preseason, he's been the backup big getting the call — efficient, active, and blocking shots.

## THE FIT ARGUMENT

Here's the strongest argument in this whole battle, and it's about fit, not résumé. Brunson's downhill handle and footwork deserve at least one pure rim-runner next to him. Towns is too skilled for that role. Drummond doesn't fit it anymore. Eubanks doesn't really fit it either. Robinson did. And Wiseman — hardly the safe choice — is the only candidate whose best version fits that description too.

Think about what that means in a playoff game. Brunson comes off a high screen. If the big rolls hard and catches lobs, the defense has to drop back — and Brunson gets his midrange pull-up. If the defense commits to Brunson, the lob is open. That's the two-way pressure Mitch created, and it's the reason that duo ranked eighth in the league. Without a rim-runner, defenses can load up on Brunson and live with whatever the big does in the middle of the floor.

Is Wiseman a guaranteed solution? Absolutely not. His consistency and health have been questions his whole career. But "safe" in this case might also mean "limited." And as we've said before on this channel, safe has never really been Leon Rose's style.

## MY CALL

Here's my prediction. Brunson's wrist is a non-issue, and he'll be his normal self on opening night. Shamet will cool off a little, but he'll end up playing more than last season, and he and Deuce will share the sixth-man job — with Deuce's defense still too valuable to give up. KAT stays the hub, OG keeps living at the line, and Bridges keeps attacking. And in the big-man battle, I'm going with Wiseman. Not because he's the safest choice, but because he's the only one who can give Brunson back the weapon he lost in July. If he wins the spot, keep an eye on him in that early-season game against Boston. Brunson against Mitch, with a new rim-runner trying to prove he can fill those shoes.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

So here's the preseason through Jalen Brunson's eyes. His surgically repaired wrist looks fine — 12.5 points on 50 percent shooting and no hesitation. Landry Shamet, an Exhibit 9 guy two years ago and now a $24 million champion, has 23 points in 27 minutes and is pushing Deuce for sixth-man minutes. OG is living at the line, KAT is still the hub, Bridges is attacking, and Hart keeps his starting spot. But Brunson lost one of the league's top pick-and-roll partners when Mitch went to Boston for three years and $47.4 million — a duo that ranked eighth in the NBA over five seasons. With only one roster spot left, Begley says Eubanks is the safe choice, Bondy says Wiseman is the favorite, and the fit argument points to the rim-runner.
