## THE INTERVIEW, LINE BY LINE

Malika Andrews set it up: when contract discussions eventually pick back up, there have been reports that KAT would be willing, to some degree, to take less than the max money in front of him to keep this group together.

KAT: "For sure."

Andrews: "You're willing to take less?"

KAT: "Oh, yeah."

Then she asked why that matters to him. And here's the answer that should calm a lot of nerves: "Because I want this team to stay together. That's always been my main priority — to keep everyone here, to keep this core, this special team that we have. So it was the first thing on my mind as soon as it was... and there was nowhere in my mind a max was even thought of."

Read that last part again. "Nowhere in my mind a max was even thought of." That's not a negotiating tactic. That's a player telling you the starting point of his thinking was keeping the team together, not maximizing his own number.

And then the moment that went viral. Andrews asked Jalen Brunson what he hopes to see happen with KAT and their partnership. Brunson didn't give a speech. He said: "Sign a contract." Simple. Straightforward. The captain and Finals MVP, publicly voting for KAT to stay.

## WHAT WE ALREADY KNEW — AND WHAT'S NEW

Let's be honest: KAT has said for a while that he's willing to take a discount. What's different now is the tone and the timing. This comes after the Knicks publicly tabled extension talks, after KAT said that news "caught him by surprise" and left him "very disappointed," and after the October 19th window passed. A lot of fans worried those moments had damaged the relationship. This interview says the opposite: the desire to stay is still there, and the willingness to give something up is still there.

What we still don't know is the most important number: how much less. "Less than the max" could mean $270 million. It could mean $220 million. The Knicks' offer, around $200 million, is also "less than the max." So the gap is still real. But the will to close it is clearly there.

## WHAT THE INTERVIEW SAYS ABOUT THE RELATIONSHIP

There's another layer here that matters as much as the money. Two weeks ago, the relationship between KAT and the front office looked shaky. He said the paused talks were "news to me." Carmelo Anthony said the Knicks dropped the ball on the communication. Anonymous executives started naming the Bulls and Clippers as possible suitors.

Now look at the tone. KAT isn't talking about disrespect. He isn't hinting at free agency. He's talking about "this special team" and keeping "everyone here." And Brunson — the most respected voice in that locker room — is publicly in his corner. That's how relationships get repaired in the NBA: not with press releases, but with the stars on the same page.

And don't underestimate Brunson's role here. He took less money to help build this team. When the captain who sacrificed says "sign a contract," it puts gentle pressure on both sides — on KAT to meet the Knicks somewhere reasonable, and on the front office to not let the gap become a wall.

## THE MATH OF "LESS"

So let's run the numbers, because this is where it gets interesting. There are three realistic reference points.

**The max:** Four years, about $276 million. KAT has now said, on camera, that the max was never in his mind.

**The Knicks' offer:** About four years and $200 million — roughly $45 to $50 million per season, as Ian Begley described it. And remember the executives Stefan Bondy talked to: they put KAT's open-market value right in that $40 to $50 million range.

**The declining structure:** This is the one that might bridge the gap. Daily Knicks pointed out that the Knicks could sign KAT to an extension that starts at his max of about $61.6 million and then declines by 8 percent each year. Let's do that math together:

Year one: about $61.6 million.
Year two: about $56.7 million.
Year three: about $52.2 million.
Year four: about $48.0 million.

Total: roughly $218 to $219 million.

Tunnel fam — that's almost exactly the four-year, $220 million number this channel projected as the realistic middle ground. KAT gets a huge first-year salary and a total well above the Knicks' current offer. The Knicks get a contract that shrinks every season — which matters a lot as Jalen Brunson's projected massive extension kicks in around 2028–29 and the salary cap keeps rising. Both sides get to say they won.

To be clear, that's NBA Tunnel analysis, not a reported offer. But when you see KAT say he never thought about the max, and you see how neatly a declining deal lands in the middle, it's not hard to picture how this ends.

## WHY THE DECLINING DEAL HELPS EVERYONE

Let's spell out why that structure is so attractive.

**For KAT:** Security. Four guaranteed years, more total money than the current offer, and his biggest paychecks up front. At 31, front-loaded money is valuable.

**For the Knicks:** Flexibility later. As KAT's salary drops each year, the team gets more room under the apron right when it needs it most — when Brunson's next deal arrives and when OG Anunoby's future comes into focus.

**For the team:** Continuity. KAT stays as the hub of the offense that won a title. Brunson keeps his co-star. And the locker room gets the message that the front office and its stars can find common ground.

## THE OTHER OPTIONS ON THE TABLE

The declining deal isn't the only path. Let's quickly lay out the alternatives.

**Option A: The Knicks' current offer.** About $200 million over four years. It's in line with what executives think KAT would get elsewhere. But KAT has already said it's too big a discount, so it probably needs to move at least a little.

**Option B: A flat four years around $220 million.** Same total as the declining version, but about $55 million every year. Simpler, but less helpful to the Knicks in the later years when Brunson's next deal arrives.

**Option C: A shorter deal.** One agent told Bondy a team might offer $40 to $50 million a year on a short-term deal. A three-year contract at a higher annual number could appeal to KAT — more per year, and another shot at free agency later. But KAT has been clear he wants long-term security, so this seems less likely.

**Option D: Pick up the option and wait.** KAT plays next season at $61 million and extends after. Simple, but it likely pushes the Knicks toward the apron next season — the exact problem Dolan wants to avoid.

Put them side by side, and the declining four-year deal looks like the cleanest compromise.

## THE TIMELINE HASN'T CHANGED

Now, a reality check on timing. The interview doesn't mean a deal is imminent. The Knicks have paused talks until later in the season. Jake Fischer has reported that KAT is likely to decline his player option so negotiations can continue in season — and that the next real extension conversations, possibly including Josh Hart, probably won't happen until after the trade deadline.

So think of this interview as a temperature check, not a signing. The temperature is warm. The paperwork is still months away.

## THE COST: WHO PAYS FOR "LESS"?

Even with KAT taking less, the apron math doesn't disappear. Brian Windhorst has warned that if the Knicks pay KAT near what he prefers, Josh Hart and Deuce McBride would likely be moved. A $218 million deal is less than the max — but it's still more than the Knicks' current offer, and every dollar has to come from somewhere.

And the opener didn't help Deuce's case. He went one-of-five in 14 minutes against Philadelphia, continuing a cold stretch that goes back to last season's playoffs and the preseason. If the front office has to choose, a struggling Deuce on an expiring deal is the most vulnerable piece. That's not what Knicks fans want to hear. But KAT's "keep everyone here" goal runs straight into a system designed to make that almost impossible.

And if the choice ever comes down to Hart or Deuce, I'd keep Hart. He's a full-time starter, one of the league's best rebounding guards, and the emotional engine of this team — with a team option around $22 million for 2027–28 that gives the Knicks some control. Deuce is a terrific player, but a shrinking role, a cold stretch, and an expiring deal make him the more likely casualty.

## THE YOUNG GUARD IMPRESSING THE FRONT OFFICE: JADEN AKINS

And that's exactly why the second story in this video matters. If the Knicks are going to keep their stars under the apron, they need cheap young players who can contribute. And according to Ian Begley, they think they might have one.

Here's Begley: "Something worth noting from a preseason and training camp perspective: the Knicks have been impressed by two-way guard Jaden Akins. His play and approach to his craft have not gone unnoticed by the organization. The Knicks are optimistic about his future. Something to keep an eye on as the team navigates the second apron. New York will not spend above the second apron threshold, per owner James Dolan. Unless that stance changes, the club will need to find younger players on team-friendly deals who can impact winning. Based on early feedback, it sounds like they think Akins has a chance to be one of those guys."

That's a big endorsement for a two-way player.

## WHO IS JADEN AKINS?

Quick profile. At Michigan State in 2024–25, Akins averaged nearly 13 points, 3.5 rebounds, and 1.5 assists, and he made the All-Big Ten defensive team. His calling card is defense and motor — he's relentless on the ball, attacks the basket, and isn't afraid of contact. The weakness is shooting: about 40 percent from the field and 29 percent from three in college. But shooting is the most improvable skill in the sport, and the Knicks' development staff — the same group that helped Dink Pate climb from the high-20s to the mid-30s from three — clearly believes he can get there.

## THE AKINS DEVELOPMENT PATH

So what does the road look like for him? Two-way players split time between the NBA team and the G League, with limits on how many NBA games they can be active for. That means Akins will likely spend a lot of this season in Westchester, playing big minutes, working on his shot, and showing the coaching staff he can handle NBA defensive assignments.

The goal is to follow the path other Knicks have walked: develop in the system, earn trust, and eventually turn a small contract into a real role. Deuce McBride did it as a second-round pick. Landry Shamet did it on an Exhibit 9 deal. If Akins can become even a reliable defensive guard who hits open threes, he's exactly the kind of player the Knicks can afford to keep when the apron forces tough choices on veterans.

What to watch from him this season: his three-point percentage on real volume, his on-ball defense against quick guards, and whether Mike Brown trusts him enough to use him in NBA minutes when injuries hit.

## A CORRECTION ON OUR TWO-WAY TAKE

And accountability time. In our Trey Jemison and Dink Pate videos, I said that if the Knicks needed to open a two-way spot for a center, Jaden Akins looked like the most logical player to swap out. Begley's reporting changes that. The organization is impressed with Akins and optimistic about his future. ## THE YOUTH PIPELINE TAKES SHAPE

Step back and you can see what the front office is building around its stars. Mohamed Diawara, the second-rounder Mike Brown started in the preseason finale. Tyler Kolek, the backup point guard who hit the finale's winning shot. James Wiseman, the 25-year-old former number two pick on a minimum. Dink Pate, the 6-foot-8 playmaker back in Westchester. And now Jaden Akins, the two-way guard the organization is high on.

None of them are stars. But in the second-apron era, the champion that wins long-term isn't the one with the most stars — it's the one with the best cheap depth behind its stars. If KAT takes less and the young guys develop, the Knicks can keep a contender together longer than the CBA wants them to.

## FRIDAY: BOSTON, MITCH, AND THE UNDERDOG LINE

Now to Friday night. The Knicks visit Boston, and the betting market has the Celtics as favorites — about minus-146, with the Knicks at plus-124.

Boston lost its own opener, blowing a big lead against the Detroit Pistons. But there were plenty of good signs for the Celtics: strong defense, Jayson Tatum and Paul George playing well off each other, smooth offense, good shooting, and depth. And they have a stronger frontcourt now — Mitchell Robinson is starting at center in his first games in green. That's what the Knicks have to deal with, and it's the first time Mitch faces his old team.

## THE KEYS FOR FRIDAY

Here's what the Knicks need to do to bounce back.

**The starters have to show up.** The opening-night performance from the first unit can't repeat. Brunson's six-of-19 night was the outlier, not the rule.

**KAT has to stay out of foul trouble.** Operate from the top of the key. Make Robinson and Queta come out to him. The Knicks can't afford another 24-minute night from their second-best player.

**Don't overload Drummond.** His first half against Philly was phenomenal — 10 points, 12 rebounds, three assists, and two blocks. When he's used the right way, he's a weapon. When he's overused, he gets gassed. Manage those minutes.

**Brunson has to move the ball.** Trust the system. Let KAT, OG, and Bridges create. The open looks will come back to him.

**OG takes the right shots. Bridges makes his presence felt.** Whether he's the third option or the fifth, Mikal Bridges needs to impact the game on both ends.

**Win the glass against Mitch.** The ultimate statement would be outrebounding the man the Knicks couldn't afford to keep.
