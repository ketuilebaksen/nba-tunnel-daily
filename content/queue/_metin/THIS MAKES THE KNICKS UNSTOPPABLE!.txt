## EXHIBIT A: THE ARITHMETIC

Before any witness speaks, the jury needs to understand the facts of the case. Because in this trial, the math is everything.

The Knicks have 13 players on guaranteed contracts. On paper, that leaves two open roster spots. But here's the catch: New York is about $3.2 million below the second apron — and ownership has made it clear that line will not be crossed. And here's the rule a lot of fans miss: a veteran minimum contract counts against the apron at its full value, based on the player's years of service. So one veteran minimum fits under that $3.2 million cushion. Two don't. Which means one camp player makes the roster. That's the whole competition.

Now, why can't the Knicks just stay at 13 and save even more? Because during the season, NBA teams are generally required to carry at least 14 players on standard contracts, with only short exceptions. So the 14th spot isn't optional. The Knicks have to fill it. The 15th spot IS optional — and that's the one the apron takes away, unless the Knicks move salary somewhere else.

A quick note for long-time viewers: in our Cut Day video, we talked about the possibility of the Knicks leaving a roster spot open. This is exactly why. The open spot is the 15th, and the apron math is what's keeping it closed.

So who's competing for spot 14? Three centers: Tony Bradley, Drew Eubanks, and James Wiseman. Two wings: Bruce Brown and Ochai Agbaji. John Konchar was part of it too, before he and the team agreed to part ways for personal reasons. Five candidates. One chair. Let's hear the witnesses.

## WITNESS ONE: HEAD COACH MIKE BROWN

The first witness frames everything else. After a practice he didn't like, Mike Brown told reporters that he'd gone down the line in the gym and named five players: Jalen Brunson, Mikal Bridges, OG Anunoby, Karl-Anthony Towns, and Josh Hart. Those five, he said, are going to play. If they do something wrong, he'll get on them and they'll figure it out together.

But everybody else in that gym? He told them: you're fighting. Fighting either to make the team, or fighting for minutes. And he said this has nothing to do with last year. The five starters are the same. Everyone else — it's a new year and a new team. Every day is a competition, whether there's a game that night or not. And then the warning: they weren't practicing well, and if they kept practicing like that, at the end of the day it wasn't going to go in their favor.

**Cross-examination.** Two things stand out in this testimony. First, it matches exactly what Brown said after the first preseason game. Back then, the message was about physicality. Now, it's about competition in practice. Same message, different setting. The bench players are being evaluated every single day — not just in games.

Second, look at who's NOT on that list of five. Deuce McBride. Jose Alvarado. Landry Shamet. Andre Drummond, Mitchell Robinson's replacement. Jordan Clarkson. These are important players, and several of them were key pieces in the title run. Brown is telling them, publicly, that they don't have guaranteed minutes either. That isn't a coach being harsh for the sake of it. It's a coach using the one tool he has against the most dangerous thing a champion faces: complacency.

And this testimony cuts both ways for the young players already on guaranteed deals. Take Tyler Kolek. In the opener in Philadelphia, he had five points and five assists without a single turnover — one of the best nights of any reserve. Under a coach who says every day is a competition, that kind of game matters. It doesn't guarantee him minutes behind Brunson, Alvarado, McBride, and Shamet. But it puts his name in the conversation every time Brown decides who's earned the next opportunity.

## WITNESS TWO: IAN BEGLEY, FOR THE SAFE CHOICE

The second witness is the most respected Knicks reporter in the business, and his argument deserves to be taken seriously, not dismissed. Ian Begley's case is for Drew Eubanks.

Here's his testimony. Before Konchar left, the Knicks were looking at adding both a wing and a big. If they still want both, they'd have to make a trade, because you can't get to 15 players without moving someone. So assuming no trade happens, you have to go with a big — and Eubanks would be the safer play.

Then he explained his hesitation about Wiseman. There's obviously upside, he said — Wiseman didn't go that high in the draft for no reason. There's a lot of talent there. But health has always been the question. If Wiseman can stay healthy and get comfortable in the right environment, Begley thinks he can really re-establish himself in the league. But he doesn't know whether a team defending a title can afford to have that third-center spot be a question mark — or whether it needs the safer option.

And then the smartest part of his testimony. Going into February, the Knicks may look at what they have and decide they need to trade for a big anyway. In that world, Begley said, he'd take Eubanks over Wiseman, simply because Eubanks is more of a sure thing to bridge the gap.

**Cross-examination.** Let me put Begley's argument in one sentence, because it's coherent: if the 14th man is just insurance until a February trade, you want the most reliable insurance — not the most exciting. That's a real argument. And notice how it fits with everything we've covered this fall — the January pivot, the trade deadline, the possibility of a deal for a big man like Ryan Kalkbrenner. Begley is thinking about the 14th spot as a bridge, not a destination.

The counterpoint? It assumes the Knicks are planning to trade for a center. If they aren't — or if the right deal never comes — then "safe" becomes "stuck." And a third center who can't change a game is a problem in a playoff series.

## WITNESS THREE: THE ROTATION ITSELF

And now the witness that can't be spun, can't be misquoted, and can't be biased. The rotation.

**Game one, in Philadelphia.** Wiseman had eight points, five rebounds, and a block. He went two-of-six at the free-throw line, which kept the night from being even better, but he was active and mobile. Eubanks had one point and one rebound. One outlet's player grades gave Wiseman a B-plus and Eubanks a B-minus, with the note that Eubanks just didn't move the needle.

**Game two, against Washington at the Garden.** The Knicks lost 111–109. But look at who played center. Towns and Drummond combined for 39 minutes. After that, exactly one other center got minutes: James Wiseman. Nine minutes. Jose Alvarado threw him several alley-oops, and he finished a drop-off feed with a dunk. Tony Bradley didn't play. Drew Eubanks didn't play.

**Cross-examination.** This is the most important piece of evidence in the entire trial. Not because nine minutes is a lot — it isn't. It's because of who got ZERO. When a coach is evaluating three players for one spot and only one of them gets on the floor, that tells you something about the order in his head.

And it gets more interesting when you look at who was left out. Tony Bradley is the player Brown spoke about most warmly when the Knicks signed him — the veteran who has hurt the Knicks in the past, the box-out specialist. Eubanks is the most naturally physical of the three, and physicality is exactly the trait Brown has been demanding since day one. And yet, in a game where the coach wanted to see his bench compete, neither of them played. That doesn't end their chances. But it does suggest the staff has already seen what it needs to see from those two in practice — and is using the games to test Wiseman.

There's also a clear basketball reason. Wiseman gives the Knicks something no other option does: the vertical lob threat. That's exactly what Mitchell Robinson used to provide. Drummond rolls to the rim, but he doesn't threaten above it. Towns pops out to the perimeter. A center who catches lobs in the pick-and-roll forces the defense to collapse — and that opens the corners for shooters like Shamet, Bridges, and OG. That's a real, specific role. And on Thursday, Alvarado was already feeding it.

## A CORRECTION ON THE INJURY TIMELINE

Quick on-air correction before we go on, because accuracy matters on this channel. In a recent video, I described Wiseman's Achilles injury as happening last December. The more accurate timeline: Wiseman tore his Achilles in October 2024, in his very first game with the Indiana Pacers. He had knee surgery earlier in his career too. Last season, he had brief stints with Indiana before being released, and he signed his Exhibit 9 deal with the Knicks in mid-September. The bigger point stays the same: he's played a limited number of games across six seasons, and his health is the central question in this trial. But you deserve the right timeline.

## THE HEALTH QUESTION: BEGLEY'S STRONGEST POINT

Let me be fair to Begley here, because this is his strongest argument and it's real. Wiseman has been hit hard by injuries — a knee surgery early in his career, then that torn Achilles in his Indiana debut. Those aren't small things, and a team defending a title has to factor them in.

**Cross-examination.** But the gap in risk isn't as big as the "safe play" label makes it sound. Eubanks has his own injury history — he's coming off surgery to repair a torn ligament in his left thumb, the injury that ended his 2025–26 season with Sacramento. And Eubanks is the veteran with the lower ceiling. If he's healthy and good, he's a solid end-of-bench big. If Wiseman is healthy and good, he's a rotation player who can actually change a game. When the downside risks are closer than they look, the smart bet leans toward the higher upside.

And then there's the Mike Brown factor. Brown coached Wiseman in Golden State. He's been openly supportive of him. And now he's playing him while leaving the other two candidates on the bench. That's not a guarantee — Brown has told everyone nothing is guaranteed. But it's the clearest signal anyone has seen.

## THE CONTRACT DETAIL THAT SETTLES THE ARGUMENT

Now here's a detail that could bridge Begley's view and the evidence from the games — and I think it's the key to this whole trial.

Camp players who make the roster usually sign standard minimum contracts that don't become fully guaranteed until early January, when the league-wide guarantee date arrives. So if the Knicks keep Wiseman on a non-guaranteed deal, they get to test his health and his play for the first two and a half months of the season — and they still have the option to waive him before his salary locks in.

Think about what that means. That gives the Knicks most of the safety Begley is asking for, while keeping the upside. If Wiseman breaks down or struggles, they can move on in January — right around the time Begley expects them to be thinking about a trade for a big anyway. If Wiseman thrives, they've found a rotation center at a minimum salary. I can't tell you for certain that's how they'll structure it. But it's a common way teams handle exactly this kind of decision. And it's why "Wiseman or safety" might be a false choice.

## WITNESS FOUR: BRUCE BROWN, AND WHAT THE APRON DID TO HIM

Now the most emotional testimony of the trial. Bruce Brown sat down with the New York Post and was very open about his summer.

Asked why he ended up on a camp deal, he said the second apron was the reason teams kept giving him. "I heard that all summer," he said. And the number of teams that were interested? Not many. He also said: you've got to prove yourself every year. "I've been on eight teams in nine years. Every year you've got to prove yourself. Prove you've gotten better." And on his role, he was realistic. The first and second units are pretty set, he said, so he'd have to provide a winning attitude. "I'm fighting for a job right now. So any role they give me, I'd be happy to do."

**Cross-examination.** There's something quietly important in that answer. The second apron isn't just a Knicks problem. It's reshaping the entire league. Teams are scared of it. And the players getting squeezed are exactly the ones like Bruce Brown — solid veterans on mid-sized or minimum deals who used to be the glue of every contender.

Look at his résumé. Last season, he made about $3 million with Denver and averaged 7.9 points, 3.9 rebounds, and 2.1 assists, shooting 38.5 percent from three. That's a useful NBA player. A champion. And he still had to take a camp deal. That's the same force sitting at the center of the Karl-Anthony Towns negotiation — the same apron that has the Knicks holding at $200 million. From the star at the top of the roster to the veteran at the bottom, the apron is writing this team's story.

And he's playing like a man fighting for his career. In both preseason games, he's played more minutes than any other newcomer. On Thursday, he defended well enough that Washington passed out of his matchup. He drew a foul instead of giving up an uncontested layup. He forced a late turnover that turned into a breakaway. He also airballed a late shot. A messy, effort-filled night — exactly the kind of night Mike Brown says he wants.

## THE PROBLEM FOR THE WINGS

But here's the honest problem for Bruce Brown — and Begley's arithmetic is right about this. With one spot available and three bigs in the mix, the Knicks will almost certainly keep a center unless they make a trade. For Bruce Brown to make the roster, the Knicks would need to open a second spot — most likely by trading or salary-dumping a player like Pacôme Dadiet.

And if you watched our Buy, Sell, Hold video, you know exactly how that could work: Dadiet's guaranteed $2.98 million, a second-round pick or two from the Knicks' stash of at least 11, and a team with cap space or a trade exception — Memphis, Charlotte, New Orleans, the Clippers, or Sacramento. Moving Dadiet's salary would create the room for a second veteran minimum under the apron. It's real. But it isn't guaranteed.

The other wing in the race, Ochai Agbaji, is in an even tougher spot. In the opener, he played eight minutes and went zero-of-three from deep. His case for the roster has always rested on his shot — a lottery pick who's bounced around and might find another level in a winning system, a Landry Shamet-style project. But the Shamet path requires the shot to fall, and so far it hasn't. With only one spot available and the bigs ahead of him in line, Agbaji needs a couple of very good shooting nights in the remaining games to change the conversation.

## CLOSING ARGUMENTS

So let's sum up the case.

**For Eubanks:** the safe insurance policy. Eight years of experience, physical, a reliable bridge if the Knicks plan to trade for a big in February. Begley's logic is sound.

**For Wiseman:** the evidence. The only third center to play in game two. A B-plus in game one. Alley-oops from Alvarado. The vertical lob threat nobody else on the roster has — the same weapon Mitchell Robinson gave Brunson. A coach who knows him and believes in him. And a contract structure that lets the Knicks protect themselves until January.

**For Bradley:** the coach's warm words when he signed — but no minutes on Thursday.

**For Bruce Brown:** the most minutes of any newcomer, real defensive impact, a championship résumé — but he needs a second spot to open.

**For Agbaji:** the shot. It has to fall, and it has to fall soon.

## THE VERDICT

Here's the NBA Tunnel verdict. The 14th spot is Wiseman's to lose. Begley's argument is smart if the Knicks see the 14th man as a placeholder until February. But the evidence from the first two games says the coaching staff is treating Wiseman as more than a placeholder. And with the January guarantee date as a safety net, the Knicks don't have to choose between upside and caution — they can have both.

And for Bruce Brown, my verdict is: case continued. If the Knicks find a way to move Dadiet before opening night, I think Brown gets the second spot. If they don't, he'll be one of the best players looking for a job — and a perfect example of what the second apron is doing to good NBA veterans.
