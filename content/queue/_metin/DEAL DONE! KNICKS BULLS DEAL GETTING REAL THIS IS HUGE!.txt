WHY EUBANKS CAME TO NEW YORK: THE MATH OF A CHOICE

Start with the arrival itself, because it tells you something about the man before we ever look at a stat. When the Knicks unleashed that September burst of Exhibit 9 signings, Eubanks was part of the very first wave — inked before Bradley was even on the radar, before the Wiseman fandom reports, before the center battle had a narrative at all. And here's what an Exhibit 9 actually means for a player in Eubanks' position: no guaranteed money, no promised seat, a contract the team can shred before opening night at zero cost — and, if he gets hurt in camp, a one-time payment of $15,000. Fifteen thousand dollars. That's the total financial commitment the defending champions made to Drew Eubanks. A nine-year veteran with a career 60% field-goal mark looked at that paper and said YES.

Why would he? Because of the same gravity this channel has documented all offseason: New York doesn't have to recruit anymore. A veteran big man with options doesn't sign a non-guaranteed deal with a stacked champion unless he believes two things — that the opportunity is real, and that winning a roster spot HERE is worth more than a guaranteed spot somewhere irrelevant. Remember the Konchar choice, picking the hardest rotation in basketball over easier paths in Miami and Orlando? Same species of decision. Eubanks chose the gauntlet. And for a player who spent last season on a Sacramento team that went 22-60, the motivation is easy to read: after a year buried on one of the worst rosters in the league, the man wants to matter in a building where basketball matters. That hunger is not a footnote, folks. In a three-man camp battle decided by margins, hunger is a weapon.

THE JOURNEYMAN'S MAP: A CAREER THAT EXPLAINS THE PLAYER

To understand what Eubanks offers, you have to understand the road he took to get here, because it's the road of a craftsman, not a prospect. Undrafted out of Oregon State in 2018 — no lottery hype, no promise, nothing handed to him. He fought his way onto a roster, then another, then another, building an NBA career on the unglamorous parts of the job: setting hard screens, rolling on time, finishing what he's given, protecting the paint without gambling. His peak arrived in Portland in 2021-22, when injuries opened the door and he started 22 games — and he walked through it with 14.5 points and 8.5 rebounds a night. Read that number again, Tunnel fam: when Drew Eubanks has been given a real starter's opportunity, he has produced like a real starter. That's not a projection. That's a receipt.

Since then, the minutes shrank — and this is where the honest critics land their punches. Last season in Sacramento: 42 games, 11 starts, about 13 minutes a night, roughly five points per game, before a torn UCL in his left thumb required surgery and ended his season. The cold read says: if he couldn't carve out a bigger role on a 22-60 team, what does he offer a champion? It's a fair question, and we'll answer it honestly below. But let me offer the context the cold read skips: bad teams don't distribute minutes on merit — they distribute them to development projects and trade-deadline showcases. A veteran backup center on a tanking roster is the LAST person who gets minutes, because his minutes don't serve the rebuild. Sacramento's season tells you about Sacramento's priorities more than Eubanks' ability. Put him on a contender that needs fifteen honest minutes from a professional big, and the math flips completely.

THE SKILL FILE: WHAT EUBANKS DOES THAT THE OTHERS DON'T

So what does he actually bring that Wiseman and Bradley don't? Three things, and each one maps directly onto how this offense works.

Skill one: SCREENING — the craft this entire system is built on. Brunson's whole game depends on his screeners: the angle of the pick, the timing of the slip, the decision to re-screen when the defender goes under. Eubanks is a screener by trade — it's the job that has kept him in the league for nine seasons. A good screen doesn't show up in a box score, but it shows up in every Brunson pull-up that starts with a defender stuck on a hip. The Finals MVP has had elite screeners his whole Knicks career. The backup center who sets picks the way he likes them earns his trust faster than any athletic upside ever could.

Skill two: the SHORT ROLL — and this is the one I'd circle in red. When defenses trap Brunson, and they will trap him every night of his life, the ball goes to the big man rolling into the space in the middle of the floor, and that big man has about one second to make a decision. Eubanks' file says he's built for exactly that second: a reliable floater, a 47% mark on short mid-range shots that sits in the 73rd percentile among bigs, and the feel to find the cutter or the corner shooter when the help comes. Wiseman's profile is the lob. Bradley's profile is the glass. Eubanks' profile is the DECISION — and against a blitzing defense, the decision is what wins the possession.

Skill three: PROFESSIONAL RIM-RUNNING. A career 60.3% field-goal percentage doesn't happen by accident. It happens because a player knows exactly what shots he can make and takes nothing else. No forced post-ups, no ill-advised jumpers, no ego possessions. On a team where every touch belongs to Brunson, KAT, OG, or Bridges, the ideal backup center is a man who never wastes the ball he's given. That's Eubanks in one sentence.

And picture the defensive fit for a second, because it's cleaner than people assume. Mike Brown's scheme asks its backup center to do three things: hold the drop coverage, communicate the switches, and protect the rim without fouling. Eubanks has spent nine seasons in drop coverage across multiple systems. He knows where to stand when a guard comes off a screen, when to step up and when to stay home, how to wall up at the rim without leaving his feet early. Camp analysts have called him a solid interior defender, and that word "solid" matters more than it sounds. A third center doesn't need to block four shots a night. He needs to not give up layups, not pick up silly fouls, and not blow coverages that leave a shooter open in the corner. That's where nine years of experience become a real advantage over a player who has barely played in two seasons.

THE HONEST RISK FILE — BECAUSE WE ALWAYS DO IT

Now the other page, stated plainly, because this channel doesn't do one-sided cases. Risk one: THE THUMB. The left-thumb UCL surgery is the biggest question mark in his file, and it's not a small one. A screening, catching, finishing big lives and dies by his hands — every pocket pass caught in traffic, every rebound secured through contact, every floater released off a bump. If the thumb isn't fully trustworthy, the entire skill file above is compromised. Camp and preseason are the only way to know.

Risk two: THE REBOUNDING CRITIQUE. One camp analysis we covered flagged him as a below-average rebounder for his position — and on a team that lost Mitchell Robinson's offensive glass, that matters. Drummond and Hart can carry a lot of that burden, but a third center who doesn't win his share of boards is a third center the staff has to compensate for.

Risk three: THE TURNOVERS. His passer turnover rate sits in the 18th percentile over the last three seasons. The feel is real, but sometimes the feel outruns the hands — and in a short-roll role, the passes are exactly the ones that get picked off. And risk four, the one everyone repeats: THE CEILING. At 29, Eubanks is what he is. He won't develop into a starter. He won't surprise anyone in March with a new skill. The analysis line that keeps getting quoted is the sharpest version of it: he doesn't change this team, he stabilizes it.

But here's my pushback on that last point: stability is exactly what a title defense needs at the fourteenth roster spot. You don't need the fourteenth man to change your team. You need him to NOT break it on the night he's forced into the rotation.

He doesn't have to win the storyline. He has to win the stopwatch, the film session, the coach's trust. And the paths to that seat are more real than anyone admits.

Path one: WISEMAN'S HEALTH. The convergence is real, the coach's fandom is real, the national-team tape is real. But the three tests we've defined all month still stand, and the first one is health. Wiseman has played a handful of games in two years. Remember the coach's own doctrine: no favorites, no politics, production wins. The Wiseman relationship is real, but Brown has told us loudly he doesn't hand out minutes on history. Here's an angle that connects two of this week's storylines in a way I haven't seen anywhere. Tyler Kolek just told the Post that his biggest problem last season was becoming too passive, and that his fix is attacking the paint with real intent. Now think about who sets the screens for Kolek when he runs the second unit. A guard on an aggression mission needs a screener who frees him cleanly AND a roller who punishes the help when the drive collapses the defense. That's literally the Eubanks job description. A Kolek-Eubanks pick-and-roll in bench minutes is the kind of pairing that could revive both careers at once: the guard gets the clean angles he needs to attack, the big gets the short-roll touches where his feel shines. And remember the tightrope we described for Kolek: both of his futures, staying or being traded, depend on looking aggressive and productive. The big man setting his screens matters more than people think. If preseason shows that pairing working, it's a quiet argument for Eubanks that no insider is tracking.

THE KAT CONNECTION: PROTECTING THE CORNERSTONE

And one more connection, this time to the biggest story of camp. The case for paying Karl-Anthony Towns rests on a simple fact we laid out with the Wembanyama numbers: KAT is irreplaceable. The supply of floor-spacing seven-footers who make elite rim protectors ordinary is basically two players on the planet, and one of them plays in Denver. Which means the most important job in the Knicks' frontcourt this season isn't finding a star backup. It's PROTECTING the irreplaceable one.

There's also the uncomfortable human angle the analysis raised: KAT is playing this season without an extension, and it's human nature for a player in that position to think about health and risk. Every minute the backup centers can reliably absorb is a minute KAT doesn't have to grind through in November. A dependable third center, one who can take 12 to 15 honest minutes on the nights Drummond needs rest or foul trouble hits, lowers the workload on the franchise's most important negotiation. That's not Wiseman's upside or Bradley's grit. That's reliability — and reliability is Eubanks' entire résumé. You don't need your third center to be exciting. You need him to keep your star fresh until June.

WHAT TO WATCH FOR TONIGHT

Since preseason is finally here, let me give you the Eubanks-specific watch list, because his audition looks different from the others. Watch his HANDS first: every catch in traffic, every pocket pass from Brunson or Kolek, every rebound grabbed through contact. That's where the thumb tells the truth. Watch his SCREENS second: are the guards getting clean separation, or are defenders sliding through? Count the possessions that start with an Eubanks pick and end with a good shot. Watch his SHORT ROLLS third: when the defense traps, does he catch, survey, and make the right play, or does the ball die in his hands? And watch the TURNOVER column, because that 18th-percentile passing number is the one weakness that could sink him fast. If after three exhibitions his hands are sure, his screens are freeing shooters, and the ball keeps moving through him, then the narrative everyone has already written will need a rewrite.
