## THE HONEST HEADLINE

Quick clarity, the NBA Tunnel way. There is no "massive trade." Nothing has happened, and nobody has reported talks. ## WHY KOLEK IS THE KEY

Let's start with why this matters.

**What Kolek has done:** He took over as backup point guard while Alvarado was out. Over three games, he put up 21 assists and zero turnovers in roughly 44 minutes. In Chicago, he hit three-of-four from deep, was plus-13, and threw that viral behind-the-back pass to KAT. He was on the floor for the game-deciding fourth-quarter run against Brooklyn. And in the Bulls blowout, he dished out four assists in the second quarter while Brunson rested.

**What happened next:** When Alvarado came back, Kolek didn't give the spot back. Brown kept Kolek in the backup role, with Alvarado playing late or in smaller doses.

**Why it changes the math:** For months, Deuce McBride has been the name in trade conversations. Now, for the first time, Alvarado could realistically be part of those discussions too. More tradable salary means more buying power. And for a team with almost no room under the second apron, buying power is everything.

## THE CAP RULE YOU NEED TO KNOW

Here's the rule that drives everything. According to Daily Knicks, the Knicks have only about $370,000 of room under the second apron, and James Dolan has said he won't cross that line.

So in any trade, the Knicks can't take back meaningfully more salary than they send out. If they want a $7 million player, they need to send out roughly $7 million. If they want an $11 million player, they need $11 million going out. No cap room, no exceptions to absorb salary, and only one tradable first-round pick, in 2033.

That's why the Calculator works in salary tiers. Let's build it.

## TIER 1: THE $3 MILLION PACKAGE

**What goes out:** Pacôme Dadiet, about $3 million on what's now effectively an expiring contract, after the Knicks declined his fourth-year option.

**What it could bring back:** Players on minimum-level deals. Last video, we went through two examples: Charles Bassey of Golden State, at about $2.45 million, and Karlo Matković of New Orleans, at about $2.3 million. Both fit.

**What it costs the Knicks:** Almost nothing on the court. Dadiet has barely played this season, though he did score four points in the Bulls blowout.

**The catch:** At this price, you're only shopping at the bottom of other teams' rosters. You might find a useful backup big, but not a difference-maker.

**My grade:** ✅ Low risk, low reward. It's the most likely type of move.

## TIER 2: THE $7.4 MILLION PACKAGE

**What goes out:** Dadiet, about $3 million, plus Jose Alvarado, about $4.4 million.

**What it could bring back:** A player making around $7.4 million, or a little more thanks to the $370,000 cushion. One of Favale's examples fits almost perfectly: **Jaylin Williams** of Oklahoma City, at about $7.7 million. He's the stretch big with the rare combination of defensive rebounding and three-point shooting.

**A correction:** Last video, I said a Jaylin Williams trade was difficult because Dadiet plus Deuce adds up to only about $6.9 million. That's true for that package. But with Alvarado instead of Deuce, the numbers get much closer, roughly $7.4 million out plus the small apron cushion. On paper, the math is very close to working. The bigger obstacle is that OKC, with Chet Holmgren out eight to 10 weeks, has every reason to keep him.

**What it costs the Knicks:** Alvarado. And that's emotional. He's a Brooklyn native, he played a real role in last year's title run, he re-signed with the Knicks this summer, and he's "Grand Theft Alvarado." Full-court pressure, deflections, charges, chaos.

**The timing:** Alvarado can't be traded until December 15th because he re-signed this offseason.

**My grade:** 🟡 Interesting, but only if the return is a real upgrade at center, and only if Kolek keeps proving himself.

## TIER 3: THE $11.4 MILLION PACKAGE

**What goes out:** Dadiet, Alvarado, and Deuce McBride, for a combined total of about $11.4 million.

**What it could bring back:** Now you're talking about legitimate rotation players. Favale's examples in this range:

| Player | Approximate salary | What he'd bring |
|---|---|---|
| Day'Ron Sharpe | $10M | Frontcourt size and rebounding |
| Ty Jerome | $9.4M | Ball handling and shot creation |
| Jalen Smith | $9.4M | Frontcourt help (he had 17 and 11 against the Knicks last week) |
| Dean Wade | $9M | A versatile forward with floor spacing |
| Tre Jones | $8M | Another ball handler |

Again, these are salary examples, not reported targets.

**What it costs the Knicks:** A lot of backcourt depth. Deuce brings three-point shooting and perimeter defense, and he's heading into free agency. Alvarado brings pressure and toughness. Losing both would leave Kolek, Clarkson, and Shamet as the main backup guards behind Brunson.

**My grade:** 🔴 Risky. You'd be trading two playoff-tested guards for one player. Unless that player clearly fixes the backup center problem, I'd pass.

## TIER 4: THE $37.5 MILLION PACKAGE

And then there's the big one: Mikal Bridges. This is the "massive trade" people keep talking about, so let's put it in the Calculator too.

**What goes out:** Bridges, in the first year of a four-year, $150 million extension, about $37.5 million per year on average.

**The idea:** The New York Post's Stefan Bondy raised the question of whether trading Bridges could be the quickest way for the Knicks to get comfortably below the second apron and create flexibility for future contracts, especially for Karl-Anthony Towns and Josh Hart.

**The problem:** The Knicks gave up five first-round picks to get him. His numbers are at a career low, with 6.1 points per game on 30.8 percent shooting through seven games, so his trade value is too. And the Knicks can't attach draft picks to sweeten a deal, because those picks went to Brooklyn in the Bridges trade.

**My grade:** 🔴 Not now. We've covered this in detail in our last few videos, and my verdict hasn't changed. Selling a player like this at his lowest point is the opposite of smart asset management. If he's still struggling in January, we'll revisit it.

## THE BIG QUESTION: CAN KOLEK SUSTAIN IT?

Everything in Tiers 2 and 3 depends on one thing: Kolek holding up over a full season. So let's be fair about the risks.

**The history:** We've seen Kolek show promise before and then lose his place in the rotation when his weaknesses became more noticeable. During last season's playoff run, the only games where he played at least 10 minutes were blowouts.

**The turnovers:** Ball security has been a concern in the past. That's why the 21-assist, zero-turnover stretch was so impressive. If you're asking a young guard to run the second unit for a team with title expectations, protecting the ball isn't optional.

**The defense:** It's never been his calling card. He's held his own against some backcourts, like Orlando's, but against elite, physical guards, teams will test him.

**The sample:** It's still only a handful of games. The league adjusts. Scouting reports catch up.

That's why the timing helps. Alvarado can't be traded until December 15th. That gives Mike Brown about five more weeks to answer the biggest question: is this the real Tyler Kolek?

## THE OVERLAP PROBLEM

There's another layer. The Knicks' backcourt and wing rotation is crowded:

- **Landry Shamet** has played his way into a "sixth starter" role. He scored 22 against Brooklyn and started against Chicago with Hart out.
- **Jordan Clarkson** leads the team in plus-minus.
- **Deuce McBride** is coming out of his early slump.
- **Kolek** holds the backup point guard job.
- **Alvarado** is just back from the ankle.
- **Dalton Knecht** is a two-way wing who just had 10 and seven in his debut.

There are only so many minutes to go around. Shamet's emergence overlaps with what Deuce does, and Kolek's emergence overlaps with Alvarado. That overlap is exactly what creates trade flexibility. It's a good problem to have, but it's still a problem.

## THE ALVARADO FACTOR: WHAT YOU'D REALLY BE TRADING

Let's slow down on Alvarado, because his value is easy to underrate on a spreadsheet.

There's a nice irony here. Alvarado himself was a midseason addition. The Knicks got him from New Orleans last February, and he became one of the finishing pieces of a championship team. Leon Rose has a long history of in-season moves that matter: Bojan Bogdanović and Alec Burks at the deadline three years ago, P.J. Tucker in March two years ago, and Alvarado last season. Now Alvarado might be the salary that helps make the next one happen.

What would the Knicks lose? The things that don't show up in the box score. Full-court pressure that drains the shot clock. Deflections. Charges. The sneaky steal from behind that earned him the "Grand Theft Alvarado" nickname. Kolek is the better playmaker and the better shooter. He shot 39 percent from three last season, compared to Alvarado's 33. But Alvarado is clearly the better disruptor on defense. In a playoff series, those details can swing a game.

So if the Knicks move him, they need to get back something that adds toughness, not just talent. Otherwise, they're trading one problem for another.

## THE DEUCE FREE-AGENCY ANGLE

Deuce's situation is different, and it's more about timing. He's on an expiring contract worth about $3.9 million and heading into free agency. SNY's Ian Begley has said he finds it hard to believe McBride and the Knicks will agree on a new deal that leaves everyone happy. That creates a classic dilemma: do you keep him for a title push and risk losing him for nothing next summer, or do you move him while he still has trade value?

Here's my view. A defending champion doesn't trade a 40 percent three-point shooter and elite perimeter defender just to avoid losing him in free agency. Last season, he shot 40.3 percent from deep and was a key part of the title run. He's also starting to find his rhythm, with two threes and two steals against Brooklyn, and a transition three plus a four-point play against Chicago. If he's helping you win, you keep him and deal with the contract in the summer. If the Knicks trade him, it should be because the return clearly makes them better this season, not because of his contract status.

## MY PREFERRED APPROACH

Here's what I'd do, and this is my opinion, not a report.

**Step one: wait.** Don't trade anyone before December 15th. Let Kolek's sample grow. Let Alvarado get his legs back. Let the team settle in.

**Step two: identify the real problem.** Is it backup center? A ball handler? A versatile forward? Favale makes this exact point: the Knicks don't have to decide today. Watch Drummond and Wiseman. Wiseman's 8 points, 8 rebounds, and 3 blocks against Chicago might change the picture.

**Step three: start small.** A Tier 1 Dadiet deal is the natural first move. It costs nothing in the rotation.

**Step four: go to Tier 2 only for a clear upgrade.** If a real center becomes available at around $7 to 8 million and Kolek is still holding strong, moving Alvarado makes sense, even though it would hurt.

**Step five: avoid Tier 3 and Tier 4 this season.** Unless something dramatic changes.

The smartest move is waiting until you know exactly what problem you're trying to solve.

## THE DEADLINE CALENDAR

Here are the dates that matter:

- **December 15th:** Players who signed this offseason, including Alvarado, become trade-eligible. Most trade activity starts here.
- **January:** Teams out of the race start selling. That's when bargains appear.
- **The February deadline:** It has been reported for February 11th. Last chance.

And a reminder about the market: Oklahoma City, with about $20.6 million under the second apron and extra first-round picks, might be shopping for bigs too after Holmgren's injury. If the Knicks and Thunder want the same center, OKC can outbid them. That's another reason to be patient and creative.
