## ACCOUNTABILITY AND CONTEXT

Before the numbers, some honesty. I predicted a bounce-back win in Boston. It didn't happen — the Knicks are now 0–2, and tonight against Orlando, they're trying to avoid an 0–3 start. And to be clear about the title: the "move" here isn't a signing or a trade. It's the next move in the most important negotiation of the season — the KAT extension — and the number that will shape it.

## WHERE THE TALKS STAND

Let's reset. A few weeks ago, the Knicks publicly tabled extension talks with all eligible players. KAT was upset: "Very disappointed, to say the least. Let's state the obvious: it's business. But there is business on the court for us to handle too." According to Brett Siegel, since then there have been no more extension conversations between KAT and the Knicks, and there's been no word on whether his representatives will keep talking behind the scenes or wait for the front office to come back to them.

But KAT has also made his intentions clear. He told ESPN's Malika Andrews he's willing to take less than the max — "nowhere in my mind a max was even thought of" — because he wants this team to stay together. And Jalen Brunson's response was two words: "Sign a contract."

## THE NUMBER: $225 TO $240 MILLION

Here's the new information. Siegel reports that the word on the street over the last few weeks is that KAT wants something in the range of $225 million to $240 million on a four-year extension. That's about $56 million to $60 million per year.

Siegel also pointed to a useful comparison: Evan Mobley's extension with Cleveland — a five-year deal reported at up to $269 million, with an average annual value of roughly $54 million. It wouldn't be surprising if that $54 million-plus range is a target for KAT. And Siegel added that while KAT is willing to take a discount, he still wants to be paid like one of the top centers in the league, and he wants long-term security — because at 31 in November, this could be the last major contract of his career.

## THE MOBLEY COMPARISON: FAIR OR NOT?

Let's dig into that Mobley benchmark, because it tells you how KAT's camp is probably thinking. Evan Mobley is a former Defensive Player of the Year and an All-NBA selection, and he's still only 25. His extension averages about $54 million a year.

The case for KAT at that level: he's a six-time All-Star, he was the offensive hub of a championship team, and he's one of the most productive big men in the league — remember, the betting market had him second, behind only Nikola Jokić, to lead the NBA in rebounding this season. On offense, he does things very few centers can do: shoot, pass, and run an offense from the top of the key.

The case against: Mobley is six years younger and an elite defender, while KAT turns 31 in November and has struggled with foul trouble. Teams pay for the future, and Mobley has more of it.

So the honest answer? A Mobley-like average of around $54 million to $56 million a year for KAT is defensible. Sixty million a year is harder to justify for a team with apron limits. That's why the middle of the range feels like where this ends.

## THE GAP IS SMALLER THAN YOU THINK

Now, here's why this is actually encouraging news. Let's line up the numbers.

**The max:** about $276 million.
**KAT's reported range:** $225 million to $240 million.
**The Knicks' reported offer:** about $200 million.

When this standoff started, people framed the gap as roughly $76 million — the distance between the Knicks' offer and the max. But KAT was never asking for the max. If his real range starts at $225 million, the gap between the two sides is somewhere between $25 million and $40 million over four years. That's about $6 million to $10 million a year. In NBA contract terms, that's a negotiation — not a war.

And remember the executives Stefan Bondy talked to, who put KAT's open-market value around $40 million to $50 million a year. That supports the Knicks' side. But KAT's title-season production — 20.1 points, 11.9 rebounds, and 3.0 assists — and his status as the offensive hub of a champion support his side. The truth is somewhere in the middle.

## THE BRIDGE: A DECLINING CONTRACT

So how do they meet? We've talked before about a structure that could work — an extension that starts at KAT's max first-year salary of about $61.66 million and then declines each year. Let's run the math, NBA Tunnel style, with different decline rates.

**Declining 8 percent a year:** $61.66 million, then $56.73 million, then $52.19 million, then $48.01 million. Total: about $218.6 million.

**Declining 7 percent a year:** $61.66 million, then $57.34 million, then $53.33 million, then $49.6 million. Total: about $221.9 million.

**Declining 6 percent a year:** $61.66 million, then $57.96 million, then $54.48 million, then $51.21 million. Total: about $225.3 million.

Look at that last one. A deal that starts at KAT's max and declines 6 percent each year lands at about $225 million — the very bottom of KAT's reported range. And it gives the Knicks what they want too: a salary that shrinks every season, right as Jalen Brunson's projected massive extension arrives around 2028–29 and the cap keeps rising.

To be clear, this is analysis, not a reported offer. But it shows how close these two sides really are. The bridge exists. Somebody just has to walk across it. And notice who benefits from a declining deal: KAT gets the biggest paychecks while he's still in his prime, and the Knicks get relief in the years when the apron pressure peaks. That's the kind of structure that lets both sides tell their fans they won the negotiation.

## THE INSIDERS SAY A DEAL GETS DONE

And the insiders agree. According to Brett Siegel, many around the NBA anticipate the Knicks and KAT agreeing to an extension sometime in the middle of this season. And Ian Begley made it his bold prediction for the Knicks' season: an extension with Towns is going to get done. In Begley's words, there's enough common ground to figure it out.

That lines up with Jake Fischer's reporting that KAT is likely to decline his player option so talks can continue during the season, with conversations likely to resume after the trade deadline.

## THE RISK IF IT DOESN'T

But Siegel also laid out the other side. If no deal happens and KAT enters next offseason without an extension, there's a belief he would opt out of his $61 million player option to become an unrestricted free agent. And we know the suitors that have been mentioned: the Bulls and Clippers with the cleanest paths, plus the Warriors, Hawks, Nets, and Mavericks. That's why the Knicks can't let this drift forever. The leverage cuts both ways.

## WHAT EACH NUMBER COSTS THE ROSTER

And here's where every Knicks fan needs to pay attention, because the exact number decides who else stays.

**At $240 million — about $60 million a year:** If the Knicks pay KAT at the top of his range, they likely don't have the room to bring back Josh Hart, and Deuce McBride is almost certainly gone. Brian Windhorst has already warned that paying KAT near his number could cost the Knicks Hart and Deuce. At that price, the core changes next season.

**Around $220 to $225 million — about $55 to $56 million a year:** This is the middle ground. At this level, the Knicks have a much better chance of keeping Josh Hart, whose team option is around $22 million for 2027–28. Deuce is still probably the odd man out — all the reporting suggests the Knicks won't be able to re-sign him — which is why there's a real chance he's moved before the deadline rather than lost for nothing.

**At $200 million — the Knicks' offer:** This keeps the most flexibility, but KAT has already said that's too big a discount.

So the difference between $220 million and $240 million might be the difference between keeping Josh Hart and losing him. That's what's at stake in this $20 million.

## HOW THE 0–2 START AFFECTS THE NEGOTIATION

Here's something people aren't talking about: the early losses matter for this negotiation too. KAT has been fighting foul trouble — in the opener, he picked up his second foul just over three minutes in and played only 24 minutes. If that becomes a season-long storyline, it gives the Knicks a reason to hold their number. As the reporting has noted, KAT is heading toward what could be his last major contract, and teams factor in things like foul trouble when deciding what to pay.

But it works the other way too. If KAT bounces back and dominates — staying on the floor, leading the league in rebounding, carrying the offense as the hub — his price goes up, and the Knicks' leverage shrinks. That's why this contract year matters so much. Every game is part of the negotiation.

## WHY THE PAUSE WAS SMART — AND RISKY

Looking back, the Knicks' decision to table talks makes more sense with this number in mind. If KAT's camp was asking for $225 million or more, and the Knicks were at $200 million, there wasn't going to be a quick handshake before the season. Pausing kept the focus on basketball and gave both sides time to see how the season plays out.

The risk is the relationship. KAT found out about the pause publicly and was "very disappointed." Carmelo Anthony said the Knicks dropped the ball on the communication. And if talks don't restart until after the trade deadline, the Knicks give up their chance to trade him this season — meaning they're betting heavily that a deal gets done before free agency. Based on the insiders' predictions, that's a bet they're likely to win. But it's still a bet.

## THE DOMINO ORDER

And think about the sequence the front office is managing. KAT is the first domino. After KAT comes Josh Hart. After Hart, eventually, Jalen Brunson — with a projected extension around $290 million. And after that, OG Anunoby. Every one of those decisions gets easier once KAT's number is set. That's why the Knicks paused talks: they need to get the first domino right. And that's also why Leon Rose has been stocking the pipeline with cheap young players — Dink Pate, Jaden Akins, Dalton Knecht, Tyler Nickel, Mohamed Diawara, Tyler Kolek, James Wiseman. When the apron forces painful choices, those are the players who'll fill the gaps.

## TONIGHT: AVOIDING 0–3

Now, the urgent part. The Knicks are 0–2 after losses to Philadelphia and Boston. Remember the history: the Knicks haven't started 0–2 since 2020–21, the last Knicks team to start 0–2 and still win a playoff series was the 1998–99 Finals team, and the last champion to start 0–2 was the 1990–91 Bulls. An 0–3 start would be a much louder alarm.

Tonight, they face the Orlando Magic. The betting market has the Knicks as solid favorites — about minus-290, with Orlando at plus-235. The main reason: Paolo Banchero is out with a high ankle sprain and is expected to miss the next few weeks.

But Orlando is still dangerous. Desmond Bane is a sniper from three — leave him open and he'll make you pay, and the Knicks' perimeter defense has been shaky early. And Franz Wagner is a problem inside: if he gets into the paint against a smaller defender, he'll take advantage. The Knicks need to match his size and make the right help-and-recover reads.

## THE KEYS FOR TONIGHT

**Contributions from all five starters.** That means Mikal Bridges too. Whether he's the third option or the fifth, he needs to impact the game on both ends — he scored four points on two-of-eight shooting in the opener.

**Lock down Bane.** No clean looks. Run him off the line.

**Size on Franz Wagner.** Don't let him feast on mismatches inside.

**KAT stays on the floor.** He's been in foul trouble to start the season. He needs to operate more from the top of the key and avoid cheap whistles.

**Manage Drummond and use Wiseman.** Drummond faded in the second half of the opener after heavy minutes. A few Wiseman minutes each half could keep the bigs fresh.

**Move the ball.** The right shots, the right reads, the right intensity on both ends. Against a Magic team missing its best player, this is a game the champs need to take — and a chance to show the first two nights were just a slow start.

## WHY THIS GAME MATTERS BEYOND ONE NIGHT

And don't underestimate what a win tonight would do. It would stop the early-season noise before it grows. It would give KAT a clean, positive night in the middle of contract headlines. It would let Mike Brown settle into a rotation instead of experimenting under pressure. And it would remind everyone — the betting market included — that the defending champions are still the team that rallied from double-digit deficits four times on the way to a title.

A loss, on the other hand, would put the Knicks at 0–3 with Philadelphia and Boston already ahead of them in the Atlantic Division race. It wouldn't be the end of anything in October. But it would make every storyline louder: the foul trouble, the bench shooting, Deuce McBride's shrinking role, and yes, the contract talks.

## THE FAN'S GUIDE TO THE NUMBERS

One last thing, because contract numbers can get confusing. Here's a simple way to think about it per year:

**$200 million over four years** is $50 million a year — the Knicks' offer.
**$220 million over four years** is $55 million a year — the middle ground we keep coming back to.
**$225 million over four years** is about $56 million a year — the bottom of KAT's reported range.
**$240 million over four years** is $60 million a year — the top of KAT's reported range.
**$276 million over four years** is $69 million a year — the max, which KAT says was never on his mind.

When you see it that way, the real negotiation is between $50 million and $56 million a year. That's a gap that can be closed with creativity — a declining structure, a little give on each side, and two parties who both say they want the same thing.
