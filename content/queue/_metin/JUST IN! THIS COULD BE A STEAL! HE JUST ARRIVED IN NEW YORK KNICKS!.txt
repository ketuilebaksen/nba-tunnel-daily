THE VALUE RANKINGS: A CHAMPIONSHIP BUILT ON BARGAINS

NO. 1: JALEN BRUNSON — THE CONTRACT THAT CHANGED EVERYTHING. You know the origin story, but the numbers deserve a fresh reading. July 2024: Brunson signs a four-year, $156.5 million extension, leaving roughly ONE HUNDRED THIRTEEN MILLION DOLLARS on the table versus his five-year max. That single act of sacrifice rebuilt New York's entire financial architecture — it's the flexibility that made the KAT trade possible and locked in OG long-term. And now the payoff line: in 2026-27, the reigning Finals MVP will earn UNDER $40 million. A man who averaged 32.6 points in the championship series. A man whose 31.2% share of his team's Finals scoring is something NO point guard had produced in a winning Finals run in at least EIGHT DECADES — for perspective, Clyde Frazier accounted for roughly 16% of the scoring in each of the 1970 and '73 title runs. Brunson literally doubled the greatest guard in franchise history's offensive load... while playing for a discount he chose himself. Best value contract in the sport. It's not close.

NO. 2: OG ANUNOBY — THE "OVERPAY" THAT BECAME A MASTERPIECE. Acquired for RJ Barrett, Immanuel Quickley, and a second-rounder, re-signed to five years and $212.5 million — and then he delivered one of the most efficient postseasons ever recorded: 21.2 a game in the Finals on 52.5% shooting, 50% from three, and an NBA RECORD 72.1% true shooting for any player attempting 150-plus postseason shots. His +6.1 on/off led the entire roster in the regular season, and among every player in the league earning $30 million or more, his contract graded out as the FOURTH most efficient — trailing only Shai Gilgeous-Alexander, Nikola Jokić, and Kawhi Leonard. Oh, and he authored THE moment: Game 4, playoff career-high 33 points, and the immortal tip-in of Brunson's miss with under two seconds left to cap the 29-point comeback. Two hundred million well spent.

NO. 3: DEUCE McBRIDE — THE PUREST BARGAIN ON THE ROSTER. $3.96 million this season — the EIGHTH-highest salary on the team — for the man who posted a team-leading +12.6 net rating across combined regular season and playoff minutes. And the stat that should be tattooed on the trade-deadline conversation: paired with Brunson last season, the Knicks were +16.2 per 100 across 829 shared minutes — the most productive two-man pairing involving the star all year. Every insider calls his expiring deal one of the league's premier trade chips; these numbers are WHY. Replicating this production at this price simply does not exist on the market.

NO. 4: KARL-ANTHONY TOWNS. Just under $60 million in the final year of the Minnesota extension — big money, but the production justifies it: 20.1 and 11.9 in the regular season, then a Finals where he stood toe-to-toe with Victor Wembanyama and never blinked. The $61 million player option for 2027-28 makes his extension THE front-office priority of the coming months — we've covered that saga and will keep covering it. NO. 5: LANDRY SHAMET, four years, $24 million total — and the man set an NBA PLAYOFF RECORD by shooting 91.7% from three in a single series, going 11-of-12 in the Conference Finals sweep of Cleveland. NO. 6: JOSH HART at just under $21 million, whose quiet importance screamed in the Finals: New York was +30.9 per 100 possessions better with him on the floor than without. NO. 7: MIKAL BRIDGES at $33.5 million per — real money for a 17.3% usage rate, among the lowest of any player earning that figure, but that's precisely the point: he's paid to defend, connect, and sacrifice. NO. 8: JOSE ALVARADO, three years under $14.4 million total, +16.4 net rating in his Finals minutes. Then the depth tier — Diawara, Clarkson, Drummond replacing Mitch at $3.9 million, Kolek, and Dadiet still hunting a role. Step back and absorb the pattern: a champion where nearly EVERY contract outperforms its number, anchored by a superstar who volunteered for the biggest discount in the sport. That's not luck. That's culture, compounding.

And put this payroll in league-wide context, because the contrast is the whole story. Across the NBA, contenders are CHOKING on their own books — superstars devouring 35% of the cap, second options demanding max money, benches funded with pocket lint. Philadelphia just stacked five stars and will spend all season discovering what that does to a salary sheet. Boston paid so heavily into its core that Mitchell Robinson became their splurge. Meanwhile the champion's three most impactful playoff performers — Brunson, OG, and Deuce — collectively earn less than some single supermax players. THAT's the invisible superpower: while rivals negotiate against the apron, New York negotiates against nobody, because its best players already volunteered for less. Every discount compounds into another depth signing, another kept asset, another year of the window staying open. Championships are won in June and funded in July.

KOLEK'S CONFESSION: THE BRUNSON BLUEPRINT

Now to the story I find most fascinating, because raw honesty from a young player is rare. Tyler Kolek, 25 years old and entering a pivotal third season, spoke at a House of Sports event and said the quiet part out loud: he knows he's NOT in the rotation when camp opens, he knows exactly why, and he named his own flaw — "too passive in the second half of last season." Coaches LOVE that kind of public self-diagnosis. It's the sound of professional maturity.

And the data behind his confession is genuinely wild. Here's the paradox of Tyler Kolek: he ranked SECOND on the entire roster — behind only Brunson himself — in drives per 75 possessions. The man attacks the paint constantly. But once he gets there? Only 31.5% of his drives ended in a shot attempt — the FOURTH-LOWEST rate among all 171 players league-wide with that driving volume. He does the hard part — beating his man, collapsing the defense — and then declines the reward, kicking it out at exactly the moments when a finish would hurt most. And the cruelest twist: the passing-up isn't justified by his shooting! He hit 38.1% on spot-up threes and converted 65% AT THE RIM when he actually attacked — a figure that MIRRORS Brunson's own rim numbers. The touch is there. The mechanics are there. What's missing is pure WILLINGNESS.

And there's a beautiful symmetry in WHO he's studying. Remember Dirk Nowitzki's confession from last week — even the greatest Maverick ever couldn't see Brunson's franchise-player ceiling while he deferred in Dallas. Brunson himself lived Kolek's exact purgatory: the skilled, undersized guard the league had already labeled, waiting behind a superstar for a real chance. The difference between the Brunson who backed up Luka and the Brunson who owns New York was never talent — it was the day he decided to be RELENTLESS. Nobody on this roster can teach Kolek that lesson better, because nobody else has lived both sides of it. The blueprint isn't just tactical. It's biographical.

Which is why his chosen template is perfect. Kolek pointed directly at Brunson's mid-range game — the floaters, the wrong-footed layups, the paint scoring — as the skill he must absorb. And understand why that's the right study: Brunson's dominance was never built on athleticism; he's built on relentless AGGRESSION — the refusal to settle, the demand for his own shot regardless of coverage. Kolek doesn't need Brunson's body. He needs Brunson's mentality. Because here's the domino logic: a point guard's playmaking — and Kolek is arguably the best PURE passer on this roster — only reaches its ceiling when defenders fear his scoring. The moment they sag off, the passing windows close and his superpower dies. Add real scoring pressure, and suddenly the vision becomes lethal. McBride, Alvarado, and Shamet stand between him and minutes — a brutal queue on a champion. But if the Deuce domino falls the way every insider predicts, a door opens midseason... and the version of Kolek who takes his own advice walks through it. Contract-year-of-the-soul season for the kid. Watch it closely.

MILIČIĆ: THE NEW DETAILS

And a quick follow-up file on yesterday's signing, because fresh reporting from Karol Wesik of Interia Sport added layers worth knowing. First, the mechanics: Miličić's Exhibit 10 carries a bonus of up to $91,000 on top of his G League salary IF he's waived before the season and spends at least 60 days with Westchester — the standard structure that makes these deals win-win, and a strong hint at the intended path: audition in camp, develop in Westchester alongside Mascari, stay one call-up away. Second — and this is the detail I love — the SYSTEM fit: his final college season came under Rick Barnes at Tennessee, in a motion offense built on quick ball movement, purposeful off-ball activity, and read-and-react principles... which overlaps almost exactly with Mike Brown's offensive framework. Players who arrive pre-programmed with those concepts absorb NBA schemes dramatically faster — priceless on a team whose coaching bandwidth is focused on the title defense, not on teaching camp bodies. Add his Germany line getting even better on inspection — 18 points, 8 boards, THREE blocks on 6-of-9 shooting with 3-of-5 from deep in 38 minutes — and the wing-inventory pattern around him (Mascari traded for, Konchar sniffed at, three two-ways still open), and the picture sharpens: New York is stockpiling long shooters with IQ and letting the gauntlet decide. And one fun kicker: if Miličić ever debuts in orange and blue, he joins a short, proud lineage of Polish players in league history — from Cezary Trybański and Maciej Lampe (a Knicks second-rounder himself!) through the great Marcin Gortat, to Poland-born Jeremi Sochan. A new chapter for Polska at the Garden? The kid controls that pen.
