## STORY ONE: OKC'S INJURY CRISIS GETS WORSE

**The news:** Oklahoma City announced that Chet Holmgren has a small patellar tendon tear in his right knee. He underwent a biologic treatment last Friday, October 30th, and the team expects a full recovery and a return in about eight to 10 weeks.

**The context:** This comes right after the Thunder lost Jalen Williams, with a dislocated left ankle and a fractured fibula, and Cason Wallace, with a torn labrum, for most if not all of the season. That's three key players out at once.

**The human side:** As always, we wish Chet a full and fast recovery. Patellar tendon injuries are tricky, and he's had lower-body injuries before. The initial treatment is being described as successful, which is good news.

**Who's left?** The Thunder still have Isaiah Hartenstein at center, plus backup big Jaylin Williams. Quick note: that's Jaylin Williams the big man, not Jalen Williams the injured star. Different players, and a lot of people are mixing them up this week. So OKC isn't desperate. But Holmgren gives them something nobody else can: elite length, rim protection, and the ability to play next to another big. Without him, their lineups change.

## THE ROSTER-SPOT TELL

One detail from Daily Knicks caught my attention. The Thunder were reportedly expected to use their final roster spot on Ochai Agbaji, a name Knicks fans will remember from training camp. After Holmgren's injury, they reportedly reconsidered that plan to look at other options.

That doesn't mean they're making a trade. But it suggests they're keeping a door open, and if they go shopping for a big man, they're going to the same store the Knicks might need.

## WHY THE KNICKS NEED A BIG, TOO

Let's be honest about New York's side. The backup center spot has been a question mark all season.

- **Mitchell Robinson** left for Boston this offseason.
- **Andre Drummond** has had good moments, with 11 rebounds in Chicago and nine against Brooklyn, but there are real questions about how much the Knicks can lean on him against elite playoff teams.
- **James Wiseman** has played about 10 minutes in the first five games, and Mike Brown has gone small rather than use him.
- **KAT** has had foul trouble, and when he sits, the paint gets thin.
- **Mike Brown** criticized the team's paint defense after the Philly loss.

So yes, a backup center upgrade makes sense. The problem is what it would cost.

## THE WALLET COMPARISON

Here's where it gets uncomfortable. Let's line up the two front offices side by side, using Daily Knicks' numbers.

| | Oklahoma City Thunder | New York Knicks |
|---|---|---|
| **Room under the second apron** | About $20.6 million | About $370,000 |
| **First-round picks** | All of their own, plus at least three extra from other teams | Only one tradable first-rounder right now, in 2033 |
| **Trade exceptions** | Yes. They can absorb a player without sending salary back, and they're expected to get disabled player exceptions for the Williams and Wallace injuries. | None useful at this scale |
| **Salary flexibility** | Can take on money | Has to send out at least as much as it takes in |

That's a massive gap. If both teams identify the same available center, OKC can put together a much stronger offer without hurting its future. The Knicks simply don't have that luxury.

## WHAT THE KNICKS COULD ACTUALLY OFFER

So if Leon Rose wants a center, what's in his pocket?

**Pacôme Dadiet:** His option was declined, so his roughly $3 million contract is now effectively expiring. He's useful as salary filler, maybe with some second-round picks attached. On its own, though, that's probably not enough for a meaningful upgrade.

**Second-round picks:** The Knicks picked up two future seconds in the Sergio de Larrea draft-night deal. Useful sweeteners.

**Deuce McBride:** Here's where it hurts. Deuce makes about $3.9 million on an expiring deal, and he plays real defensive minutes. He's a natural trade candidate, and we've discussed him before as a fit for OKC's disabled player exception.

**Jose Alvarado:** He re-signed this offseason, so he can't be traded until later in the season. Once he's eligible, he becomes another option.

And here's the second-apron catch. With only about $370,000 of room, the Knicks can't take back meaningfully more salary than they send out. Every deal has to be close to salary-neutral, or cheaper.

## WHAT A REALISTIC TARGET LOOKS LIKE

I'm not going to throw out specific names, because I don't want to invent rumors. Plenty of channels will tell you exactly which center the Knicks are "targeting," with zero reporting behind it. Instead, here's the profile a realistic target has to fit, given the cap math:

- **A small salary.** Probably a few million dollars or less, so the Knicks can match it with Dadiet's deal without crossing the second apron.
- **An expiring or short contract.** That protects next summer's flexibility for Brunson, KAT, and Hart.
- **A specific job.** Rebounding, screening, rim protection, and staying vertical without fouling. Not a scorer. The Knicks have enough offense when KAT is on the floor.
- **Playoff toughness.** Someone who can handle the physical bigs in the East, the kind of matchup Mitchell Robinson used to win.
- **A seller with a reason.** Most likely a team out of contention that wants to save money or collect second-round picks.

Notice what's not on that list: a big name. The "dream player" in this market is a boring one, a backup big who does the dirty work. That's what the Knicks need, and it's what they can afford.

## MY TAKE: DON'T OVERPAY FOR A BACKUP CENTER

I'll be honest: I'm not excited about losing backcourt depth just to bring in a backup center. Deuce brings defense and energy. Alvarado brings pressure and toughness. Those are exactly the players you want when playoff basketball gets physical.

But we can't pretend the Knicks don't need help in the paint either. So here's my preference, as analysis, not reporting:

- **Be patient.** Holmgren is expected back in eight to 10 weeks. OKC's need for a center could be temporary, which might take them out of the market by the deadline.
- **Use Dadiet and second-round picks first.** Find a cheap, physical big on a small or expiring contract who doesn't cost a rotation player.
- **Develop what you have.** Wiseman has length and athleticism. If Brown gives him a defined role, even five or six minutes a night, he might grow into it.
- **Don't panic-trade because a rival needs the same position.** This team has invested too much in its window to throw away key rotation players for a short-term fix.

## STORY TWO: THE BRIDGES "ESCAPE HATCH"

Now to the second story, which is more controversial. Stefan Bondy of the New York Post raised a question: what if trading Mikal Bridges is one of the clearest ways for the Knicks to get out of their cap squeeze?

Let me be very clear: the Knicks have not said they're trading Bridges. There's no report of talks. This is a columnist's argument, not news. But it's the second outlet in a week raising Bridges' future. Last video, we put the SI Knicks column's OKC trade idea on trial. So the conversation is getting louder.

## THE ARGUMENT

Here's Bondy's logic, in plain terms:

- **The money:** Bridges is owed about $150 million over the next four years on his extension, and that deal includes a player option around $41.5 million in 2029–30. That's a big commitment for a team with about $370,000 under the second apron.
- **The core's future:** The Knicks have to plan for Brunson's extension, projected around $286.5 million over four years starting next summer. They also have to plan for KAT's next contract (his camp is reportedly looking for $225 to $240 million), OG's long-term deal, and Hart's team option. Those are the players who define this team.
- **Who's most expendable?** Among the big contracts, Bridges might be the easiest to move financially, especially after his slow start.
- **The replacement:** The Knicks have other options at shooting guard. Shamet just scored 22 against Brooklyn. Deuce and Clarkson give Brown different looks. None of them replaces everything Bridges does, but together they can cover a lot.
- **The plan:** Trade Bridges for an expiring contract before the February deadline. That creates future flexibility and makes it easier to extend and manage the expensive core.

## THE COUNTERARGUMENT

And here's why this is so complicated.

**The price.** The Knicks gave up five first-round picks to get Bridges from Brooklyn. Moving him for an expiring contract, mostly cap relief, would mean getting back a fraction of what they paid. That's a tough pill to swallow.

**The champion factor.** Bridges was part of the foundation of a title team. That starting five posted a plus-19.9 net rating in the Finals. You don't casually break up a championship core.

**The timing.** He's in a slump right now: about six points per game, zero free-throw attempts through six games, and one-for-seven against Brooklyn. Trading him now would be selling at his lowest value.

**The defense.** Bridges is still one of the better perimeter defenders in the league. Shamet, Clarkson, and Deuce can replace some of his scoring, but not his size and length on the wing.

**The domino effect.** Trading Bridges for an expiring contract makes the Knicks cheaper, but also weaker this season. Is that a trade you make while defending a title?

## THREE WAYS THIS COULD PLAY OUT

Here's how I see it, and this is my analysis.

**Scenario one: Bridges bounces back.** The most likely outcome. He finds his shot by December, the Knicks start winning, and this conversation goes away. A player with his track record usually comes out of slumps.

**Scenario two: a "keep, then decide in summer" approach.** Bridges stays, the Knicks chase a repeat, and they revisit the money next summer, when Brunson's extension window opens. That's when the real cap decisions will be made.

**Scenario three: the escape-hatch trade.** If Bridges is still struggling in January and the Knicks are hovering around .500, Rose explores moving him for an expiring contract plus whatever assets he can get. I'd put that at maybe 15 to 20 percent.

## HOW THE TWO STORIES CONNECT

Here's the bigger picture. Both stories come down to one question: how far is this front office willing to go to stay in contention?

The OKC story shows the Knicks are short on resources. They don't have extra first-round picks, they don't have cap room, and they have almost no flexibility under the second apron. The Bridges story shows the only big lever they have: one of their expensive contracts.

That's the reality of building a champion. You spend the picks and the money to win. Now the Knicks have to keep winning without much left to spend.

But here's the thing. This front office has always found creative angles. The draft-night de Larrea move. Second-round picks like Kolek and Diawara. Buy-low veterans like Clarkson and Shamet. If anyone can work inside a $370,000 margin, it's Leon Rose and Brock Aller. They've turned tight cap situations into championship pieces before, and they'll have to do it again this winter.
