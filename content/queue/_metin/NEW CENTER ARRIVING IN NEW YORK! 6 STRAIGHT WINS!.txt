THE HONEST HEADLINE

Quick clarity, the NBA Tunnel way. No new center is arriving in New York. The name in this video, Karlo Matković of the Pelicans, comes from a Bleacher Report trade proposal — a hypothetical, not a reported negotiation. The six straight wins are real, though. Let's start there.

THE 39–17 QUARTER

The stakes: This was the third meeting with Cleveland this season. The Cavs won the last one 115–107 at the Garden. And it mattered in the NBA Cup — a loss would have knocked the defending champions out of contention for the Cup title. Instead, the Knicks picked up their first group-play win and stayed alive.

The first three quarters: This didn't look like a blowout at all. Cleveland came out hot with a 34-point first quarter. The Cavs led 61–56 at halftime and were up by 11 more than once. After three quarters, the Knicks still trailed 85–82.

The fourth quarter: Then the Knicks outscored Cleveland 39–17. Here's how it happened:

Two straight threes from Jose Alvarado early in the quarter gave New York a 90–85 lead.
At the 9:21 mark, Alvarado stripped Donovan Mitchell and set up Pacôme Dadiet for a layup.
Dadiet added consecutive baskets in transition, and suddenly the Knicks had an 11-point cushion.
James Wiseman scored on back-to-back possessions, and the Knicks had opened the quarter on a 25–9 run to lead 107–94.

The game was over long before the final buzzer.

THE ALVARADO GAME

Let's give Grand Theft Alvarado his flowers.

The line: A season-high 21 points off the bench, with five three-pointers.

The moment: The strip on Mitchell. That's the play that defines him — sneaking up, getting a hand on the ball, and turning defense into instant offense.

The context: Remember where Alvarado was a few weeks ago. He missed seven of the Knicks' first 11 games because of an ankle injury and coach's decisions. Tyler Kolek took his backup point guard job. People were talking about Alvarado as a trade chip once he became eligible on December 15th.

Then came the Toronto game, when Mike Brown picked him over Kolek in a faster matchup and he flipped the game with pressure and playmaking. And now this: 21 points and a game-changing fourth quarter against a playoff team. With Deuce McBride and Jordan Clarkson out, Alvarado stepped up when the Knicks needed him most.

The "Kolek or Alvarado?" debate isn't a debate anymore. It's a strength. The Knicks have two very different backup guards, and both are winning games.

THE YOUNG GUYS DELIVER

Pacôme Dadiet: Remember, the Knicks declined his fourth-year option. He's been the subject of trade talk all season. And in the biggest stretch of the game, he scored on three straight trips — a layup off the Alvarado steal and two transition baskets. Huge moment for a young player.

James Wiseman: The third-string center was everywhere in the fourth quarter. Three offensive rebounds, a jumper off an Alvarado pass, and a dunk. And after getting blocked at the rim, he kept coming back. That kind of effort from the third center on the depth chart says a lot about this group. And after weeks of us asking whether Wiseman deserved a real look, this is exactly the kind of answer you want.

THE SI KEYS — AND HOW THE KNICKS EXECUTED

Sports Illustrated's Knicks On SI laid out three keys for a win before the game. Let's see how they played out.

Key #1: Relax the coverage on Donovan Mitchell. According to the analysis, the Knicks have blitzed Mitchell more than any other team against any high-volume ball handler — and it's rarely worked. Last season, blitzing him produced 1.60 points per action for Cleveland. This season, the Knicks doubled their blitz rate to 38%, and he still produced 1.30 points per action. Softer coverages, like hedging or playing up at the level of the screen, held him to much lower numbers.

The result: Mitchell still got his — a game-high 30 points. James Harden added 28. Cleveland's backcourt did what everyone expected. But the difference was everything around them.

Key #2: Decide which shooters to help off. The idea was to pick which Cavs shooters to leave and which ones to chase off the line. Jarrett Allen had 10 points and five rebounds, but the rest of the supporting cast never took over. In the fourth quarter, Cleveland managed only 17 points as a team. That's where the game turned.

Key #3: Attack the cross-match. Cleveland has often put its big men on Josh Hart when Towns is on the floor. The plan was to feature Towns and make smaller defenders battle him inside and on the glass.

The result: KAT didn't have a big scoring night — 12 points — but he grabbed 10 rebounds and dished out seven assists. That extended his streak to 17 straight double-doubles to open the season. And Josh Hart was everywhere: 15 points, 12 assists, and seven rebounds.

THE GLASS

Rebounding was a big theme heading into this game. In the first two meetings, the Knicks grabbed 37% of their half-court misses in their win — and only 18% in their loss. Cleveland controlled the boards in that 115–107 defeat.

On Wednesday, the Knicks found their physicality when it counted. Wiseman's three offensive rebounds in the fourth quarter were a big part of that.

THE INJURY CONTEXT

Neither team was at full strength.

Cleveland played without Evan Mobley, who's out with a toe injury and is the anchor of its defense.
The Knicks were missing OG Anunoby (strained left calf), Deuce McBride, and Jordan Clarkson.

Mikal Bridges stepped into a bigger role and delivered a season-best 19 points with eight rebounds. After the buzzer-beater against Portland, that's another big step out of his slump. Landry Shamet chipped in 11. Six Knicks scored in double figures.

The season series: The Knicks now lead Cleveland 2–1. The Cavs have dropped to 9–10 and lost two straight. If these teams meet again in the playoffs, as many expect, Wednesday night is a useful blueprint.

BROWN'S QUOTE: DEFENSE AHEAD OF OFFENSE

Mike Brown has said recently that his team's defense is way ahead of its offense. This game showed both sides of that. The defense was shaky early — Cleveland scored 61 in the first half — but once the Knicks locked in, they allowed only 17 points in the final 12 minutes. That's the kind of stretch that wins games against good teams, and it's the kind of response a champion needs when the first half goes wrong.

THE ROUGH NIGHT FOR THE CAPTAIN

Now the one concern from the game. Jalen Brunson was listed as questionable earlier in the day with an illness. Once the game started, he labored through a six-of-22 shooting night. He still finished with 19 points and five assists and hit some big shots, but it was far from his usual level.

The good news: the Knicks won anyway, thanks to the bench. The bigger question: what happens on nights when the supporting cast doesn't bail him out — especially when Towns is on the bench?

THE BRUNSON SPACING EQUATION

That question was the focus of a sharp analysis from Daily Knicks' Dan Favale, published right before the Cavaliers game. His argument: the Knicks aren't doing Brunson any favors when Towns sits. And the numbers back him up.

Overall offense:

Brunson and Towns together: 1.17 points per possession
Brunson without Towns: 1.12 points per possession — Favale compared that to a below-average offense

Half-court offense:

Both stars: 0.98 points per play
Brunson without Towns: under 0.87 — which would rank 29th in the league

The sample isn't small. Brunson plays about 75% of the possessions the Knicks have without Towns. Brown is no longer staggering his two stars so that one is always on the floor. Brunson often leads the second unit, and the results haven't been pretty.

And here's the strange part: this is a new problem. Last season, the Knicks scored nearly 1.24 points per possession when Brunson ran the show without Towns. Favale noted that would have been the best mark in the entire league. So Brunson has proven he can carry a lineup. Something has changed — and according to the analysis, it isn't him.

VARIABLE #1: THE BACKUP CENTER

The first issue is the backup center spot. Andre Drummond has taken over that role, and more than 80% of Brunson's minutes without Towns have come alongside him.

The problems, per Favale:

Drummond is a strong rebounder, but a downgrade as a screener and finisher.
He has a habit of putting the ball up himself after grabbing offensive rebounds, which hurts efficiency.
Since the Knicks don't want the 33-year-old shooting threes, defenses can crowd the paint.
Brunson gets less space coming off screens, has no reliable lob threat, and often finds Drummond's defender sitting right in his driving lane.

The numbers:

Brunson's two-point percentage with Drummond on the floor: 38.8%
When Drummond sits: 48.2%

That's a massive swing for a player whose game lives on two-point shots.

VARIABLE #2: JOSH HART'S SHOOTING

The second issue is Hart's shooting. Nearly half of Brunson's minutes without Towns come with both Drummond and Hart on the court.

Hart this season: under 28% from three and under 44% on twos
In minutes without Towns: 40% on twos and under 17% from three

Defenses simply don't respect him enough to leave Brunson alone.

THE COMBINED EFFECT

Put Drummond and Hart together, and Brunson's shot profile suffers:

Situation	Brunson's shots at the rim
With Drummond and Hart	11.9%
Overall	16.3%
With Towns	19.1%

And on short mid-range jumpers — his floaters and push shots — he shoots just 30% in Drummond-Hart lineups, compared with 47.4% when neither is on the floor. Those are the shots that make Brunson so dangerous.

THE FAIR COUNTERPOINTS

Hart answered back on Wednesday: 15 points, 12 assists, and seven rebounds, and he was a huge reason the Knicks pulled away. That's the version of Hart the Knicks need. But Favale's point is that even when Hart makes more shots, defenses are unlikely to guard him differently. The spacing problem doesn't disappear after one good night.

The Knicks still win those minutes. Barely, but they do — mostly because of defense, where Brunson does his part. So these lineups aren't a disaster. They're just wasting one of the best offensive players in the league.

Favale's conclusion: The offensive drop-off reflects the talent around Brunson, not his ability to lead a one-star unit. Unless the Knicks upgrade the backup big spot or add a wing who can offset Hart's shooting swings, not much will change.

THE DRUMMOND INJURY

And Drummond's situation took a worrying turn. He left Wednesday's game with a head injury, and his status is now something to watch. If he misses time, the Knicks will lean even more on James Wiseman — who played great in the fourth quarter against the Cavs but has limited experience in a regular rotation role.

That might be the opportunity Wiseman has been waiting for. And based on Wednesday night, he might be ready.

SO WHY DOES MATKOVIĆ KEEP COMING UP?

And that leads straight to the trade idea.

The proposal: Bleacher Report's Zach Buckley floated this deal:

Knicks get: Karlo Matković
Pelicans get: Pacôme Dadiet and three second-round picks

Again, it's a hypothetical trade idea, not a reported negotiation.

The player:

6-foot-10, 231 pounds
A career 37.2% three-point shooter
The leaping ability to finish above the rim
About 6.2 points per game this season, shooting roughly 36% from deep
Buckley called him "quietly one of the more intriguing backup bigs on the market"

Why he fits the Favale problem perfectly: Look at what's missing in the Brunson-without-KAT lineups. A big who can pull his defender out of the paint, so Brunson has room to drive. A big who can catch lobs and finish above the rim. Matković offers both. He's exactly the skill set the Knicks don't currently have off the bench.

The history: This isn't the first time the Knicks have looked at him. Earlier this offseason, SNY's Ian Begley reported that the Knicks discussed a trade for Matković while searching for a third center behind Towns and Drummond. Those talks went nowhere. The Pelicans turned New York away, just as they reportedly did when the Knicks asked about Yves Missi. At the time, it looked like New Orleans wasn't interested in helping the champs fill their center spot. But things can change once the season gets going.

The cap math: Dadiet's roughly $3 million expiring deal is the matching salary, and with only about $89,000 of room under the second apron, that's exactly the kind of structure the Knicks need. The second-round picks are the sweetener — and the Knicks have plenty of them.

The catch — and it's a new one: Dadiet just had one of the best moments of his Knicks career in this game. Trading him right after he helped win a key fourth quarter is a tougher call than it was a week ago. It's not a deal-breaker, but it adds a wrinkle.

MATKOVIĆ VS. THE OTHER OPTIONS
Option	What it fixes	Cost
Karlo Matković	Floor-spacing big plus lob threat	Dadiet + 3 seconds (proposal)
James Wiseman (internal)	Lob threat, rebounding, energy	Nothing
Goga Bitadze	Rim protection, physicality	More salary to match
Moussa Cissé	Athletic rim-runner	Blocked until next summer

Matković is the only one on that list who stretches the floor. That's why he fits the Favale problem so well.

MY CALL

Here's my take. Alvarado's 21-point night keeps him in the rotation through the deadline — the Knicks aren't trading him now. Wiseman gets real backup minutes while Drummond deals with the head injury, and he plays well enough to change the trade conversation. The Knicks call New Orleans again about Matković after December 15th, and this time there's a real chance — a Dadiet-plus-seconds structure works for both sides. The win streak hits seven. And Brunson? One six-of-22 night while sick doesn't worry me. He leads the league in clutch scoring for a reason.
