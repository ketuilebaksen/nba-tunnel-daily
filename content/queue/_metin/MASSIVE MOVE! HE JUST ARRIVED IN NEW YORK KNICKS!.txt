THE UPDATE: TWO MILLION DOLLARS THAT CHANGE EVERYTHING

Here's the news, via Dan Favale's reporting: the NBA raised next year's salary cap forecast by $2 million. I know — two million sounds nanoscopic in a league of $60 million salaries. But for a franchise TAP-DANCING around the second apron the way New York is, every single penny is oxygen. Run the numbers: instead of roughly $7.2 million of space beneath the 2027-28 second apron, the Knicks now project to have $9.2 MILLION of runway — and crucially, that figure only accounts for ten contracts currently on the books. And it gets better: if and when Karl-Anthony Towns signs the extension we've mapped for weeks — the structure that pays him LESS in year one in exchange for long-term security — that runway mushrooms by a considerable margin on top.

Now connect the dots to the conclusion Favale himself reached, because it's the sentence of the day: anyone still assigning INEVITABILITY to a McBride trade is now officially exaggerating. The wall we kept describing — "keeping Deuce at market price means crossing the apron" — just grew a door. The Knicks may genuinely have the flexibility to pay their longest-tenured player AND duck the second apron after all. Two million dollars from the league office, and the entire calculus shifts.

And understand WHY the forecast moved, because it's a tell about where the whole league is heading. Cap projections rise when league revenue projections rise — the new media money settling in, gate receipts booming, and yes, the expansion fees looming on the horizon that we'll get to later in this video. The league doesn't nudge these numbers casually; teams build three-year budgets off them. So when the office says "there's more money coming than we told you in July," every capologist in every front office re-runs every scenario that was previously marked impossible. And in New York's specific spreadsheet, the scenario that just moved from the "impossible" column to the "workable" column has a name and a number 2 on his jersey. Small forecast, massive consequence — that's how the apron era works. The margins ARE the game now. We've been telling you all summer that this front office tap-dances on a ledge measured in hundreds of thousands of dollars; well, somebody just widened the ledge by two million.

THE RULEBOOK: EXTEND HIM AND KEEP THE CHIP

And here's the part that makes this genuinely brilliant for the front office — the CBA fine print, again via Favale. New York can extend McBride right now, well after August 11th, AND STILL KEEP HIM TRADE-ELIGIBLE — as long as the deal meets three conditions. One: the extension can't exceed three additional seasons on top of 2026-27 (he's eligible for four, so they'd deliberately leave a year on the table). Two: the annual salary can't increase or decrease by more than 5% year over year. Three: he can't earn more than 120% of the league's average salary — which, at his realistic price point, was never going to be an issue anyway.

Absorb what that means: the fabled either/or we've debated all summer — "extend him and lose the trade chip, or keep the chip and risk losing him for nothing" — was a FALSE choice all along, if the contract is structured right. Sign the compliant three-year extension, and the Knicks get every branch of the decision tree: Deuce locked in long-term if the season goes to plan, Deuce as a MORE valuable trade asset if February demands a center deal (a controlled multi-year contract trades better than a scary expiring), and zero risk of the Mitchell Robinson nightmare — walking for nothing — under any scenario. That's not picking a door. That's buying the whole hallway.

And let me linger on that Robinson comparison for a second, because it's the ghost haunting this entire front office. Remember the sequence: New York let Mitch's situation drift, the injuries stacked, the market cooled, and a homegrown, title-winning center walked out the door for NOTHING — no pick, no prospect, no salary relief, just an empty locker and a hole at the five that this team has spent the ENTIRE summer scrambling to patch. The Hukporti gamble, the Cissé offer sheet Dallas matched, the Richards saga that ended in Miami, the Exhibit 9 pile, the Liam Robbins flier — every single one of those stories is downstream of ONE asset mismanagement. You think Leon Rose hasn't internalized that lesson? The compliant extension is precisely the anti-Robinson insurance policy: whatever happens with Deuce — he stays, he's traded, the roles shift — New York gets VALUE out of it. The one outcome the rulebook now guarantees against is the only truly unforgivable one: watching another homegrown champion leave for free. Once bitten, twice armored.

Think about the trade-value mechanics too, because this is the part casual fans miss. An expiring contract at the deadline is a SCARY asset for the buying team — you're trading real players and picks for a guy who can walk in July. Teams discount for that risk; it's why expiring-contract returns always underwhelm. But a 25-year-old two-way guard locked into three more years at a controlled, CBA-capped number? That's the single most liquid asset type in modern NBA trade markets. The Pelicans, the Hornets, any big-man-rich team we've mapped all summer — they'd give up MORE for extended-Deuce than expiring-Deuce, not less. So the extension doesn't shelve the February scenarios we've been tracking. It APPRECIATES them.

THE HONEST ROLE CONVERSATION

Now, the fair question underneath the money: what IS Deuce's role on this team anymore? Let's handle it straight. Landry Shamet took the sixth-man torch last season — 51 games after the early shoulder issue, nine points on 39% from deep, real defense, and the trust that carried him into massive Conference Finals and Finals minutes. Heading into camp, Shamet is the favorite to keep that role. But Deuce's counter-argument is sitting right there in the numbers we've quoted all summer: 12 points, 41% from three, a steal a game, elite point-of-attack defense — production that is brutally hard to replicate at ANY price, let alone his. The likely picture: Alvarado runs the backup point, Deuce slides to the two, Shamet mans the wing, Diawara and (my prediction holds) Bruce Brown fill out the flanks, Drummond anchors. That's not a demotion for Deuce — that's a loaded second unit where his 3-and-D profile fits like a glove. Sixth Man of the Year might be an internal competition this season. Championship problems, folks.

And here's the camp subtext nobody's saying out loud: this role battle is ALSO a negotiation. Every October minute Deuce wins, every preseason stretch where his point-of-attack defense wrecks an opposing backcourt, is leverage at the extension table — for HIM. And every Shamet heater is quiet leverage for the FRONT OFFICE to hold its number. That's not cynicism; that's how smart organizations and smart agents both operate when the talks are live and the season is about to hand out fresh evidence. Which makes the first month of this season appointment viewing on two levels: the banner defense on the scoreboard, and the contract case being built possession by possession underneath it. THE EXPANSION WRINKLE: THE CHESS GETS THREE-DIMENSIONAL

And one more layer, because the future just got bigger than the cap sheet. It's become increasingly clear the NBA is EXPANDING — Vegas and Seattle are coming, likely sooner than people think. And expansion means expansion DRAFTS: existing teams protect a limited list of players, and the new franchises pluck from the rest. Now think about what that does for a team like New York. First, it's a relief valve: a contract that doesn't fit the apron math someday could simply be... taken off your hands. Second, it forces protection chess: the Knicks are loaded with exactly the young, cheap, proven role players expansion teams crave — Deuce, Alvarado, Diawara, the whole doctrine shelf. At least one beloved name could get plucked. Third — and here's the cold-blooded nuance — letting a player you extended walk unprotected into an expansion draft is BAD business; trading him just before the draft for actual assets is GOOD business, and Leon Rose is perpetually a step ahead on exactly these calendar games. So yes, I'll do the full "who do the Knicks protect" breakdown in a future video — the debates are juicy. Picture the war room for a second: Brunson's obviously protected, KAT, Bridges, OG, Hart... and then the list gets short and the sweating starts. Deuce or Alvarado? The young bigs or the vets? Somebody beloved goes unprotected — that's just arithmetic. For today, just register the strategic upgrade: expansion gives this front office one more escape hatch, one more leverage point, and one more reason an extension now doesn't box them in later.
