## THE DECISION: BROWN CHOSE ANSWERS OVER WINS

Let's start with the call Mike Brown made before tipoff, because it explains everything that followed. The coach decided the preseason opener in Philadelphia would NOT be about winning. His starting five got a good chunk of the first half together, just enough to build some rhythm. Then he opened the floodgates. By the end of the night, TWENTY of the twenty-one players under contract had played. The only one who didn't was Jose Alvarado, held out to rest a minor bone bruise in his knee — another deliberate choice to protect a key piece in a game that doesn't count.

Now, why would the defending champions basically hand away a game against the team the oddsmakers see as their biggest East rival? Because Brown cares about different things in October. Remember his doctrine from media day: no slippage in the process, and he WANTS adversity early, so he knows his group can handle it. And remember what Begley reported before camp: the regulars would play fewer preseason minutes so the staff could get an extended look at everyone else. Brown did exactly what he said he'd do. He traded a scoreboard for information.

And the contrast with Philadelphia tells you why. The Sixers went through a massive makeover this summer — only three players who suited up Monday were on the team last season: VJ Edgecombe, Jabari Walker, and Justin Edwards. A brand-new team NEEDS preseason wins to build chemistry. The Knicks' starters have played close to 200 games together. They don't need a win in October. They need answers about the bench, the camp battle, and the center rotation. Brown's decision was shocking only if you think the score matters. If you understand what he's actually doing, it was the smartest call of the night.

## WHAT THE DECISION REVEALED

So what did twenty players in one night actually show the staff? Some great news first. Jalen Brunson looked like himself in his first game since the wrist surgery: 15 minutes, 12 points, two threes, three assists, zero turnovers. Mohamed Diawara attacked closeouts and made a chase-down block in transition that this roster badly needs. James Wiseman flashed the size and skill that makes him the camp favorite. Jaden Akins made eight minutes count with three assists. And on the other side, Ochai Agbaji missed wide-open corner threes and finished without a field goal.

But the biggest lesson was about size. When you play the deep bench for long stretches against a team hungry to win, you find out where the depth is thin — and for New York, it's still the center spot. Since Mitchell Robinson left for Boston and Ariel Hukporti for Philadelphia, the frontcourt behind Towns and Drummond is built on camp invites and question marks. Monday night didn't create that problem. It just put it under bright lights. And then came the injury that made the whole picture even more complicated: Hukporti, now a Sixer, went down with a non-contact Achilles injury in the first quarter. Our best wishes to him. But it means Philadelphia now has the same hole the Knicks do — and they'll be shopping in the same market.

Which brings us to the name that could fix that hole better than anyone on the current roster.

## THE BACKSTORY: A DRAFT NIGHT THAT STILL STINGS

Let's start with the history, because it explains why this name hits different for New York. On draft night two years ago, the Knicks' front office had real interest in two young big men: Kel'el Ware and Yves Missi. Both of them came off the board before New York's pick. Miami grabbed Ware. New Orleans grabbed Missi. And the Knicks went with Pacôme Dadiet — a pick that, as we covered in the "no trade value" autopsy, simply hasn't worked out, to the point that New York now has to attach picks just to move his contract.

Think about how that night still echoes through this roster. Missi became the Pelicans prospect New York chased all summer and couldn't pry loose. Ware became one of the more interesting young centers in the league. And the Knicks' own pick from that night became a salary-dump candidate. Three paths from one draft — and two of the players New York wanted most are now names on its trade board. Sometimes the NBA gives you a second chance at a mistake. The question is whether you're willing to pay for it.

And quick correction to our own records, since accountability is the house rule: a couple of videos ago we listed Ware as "Miami's young shot-blocker." That's outdated. When Giannis Antetokounmpo landed in Miami three months ago, Ware was part of the package sent to Milwaukee. He's a Buck now — and that changes everything about how available he might be.

## QUESTION ONE: IS HE ACTUALLY AVAILABLE?

The honest answer: maybe — and that "maybe" got a lot more real this week. Kevin O'Connor wrote that Ware and Miles Turner could help Milwaukee figure out what comes next, OR they could help somebody else win a championship. That's analyst language for "these two could be moved." And O'Connor named the teams he sees as fits: the Celtics, the KNICKS, the Lakers, the Pistons, the Sixers, the Bulls, the Mavericks, and the Magic. That's a crowded list, which is both good and bad — good because it confirms New York is in the conversation, bad because it means a bidding war if Milwaukee ever opens the doors.

Now the important caution: the reporting suggests the Bucks are more motivated to trade TURNER than Ware. That makes sense. Turner is the veteran on a bigger contract; Ware is the young, cheap, improving piece a rebuilding team would usually want to keep. But nothing stops Milwaukee from listening on both. And let's be realistic about the Bucks' situation: after the Giannis trade, they don't have a championship roster. They may not even have a playoff roster in a deep East. Tyler Herro is a good player, but this doesn't look like a team built to win now. If Milwaukee struggles early, the question becomes: why keep a young big man's best years for a season that's going nowhere? Midseason sellers flip assets. That's how this league works.

And there's one more interesting detail. Ware said he actually likes Milwaukee's calmer pace of life — it's "more my speed," he said. That's not a trade demand. If anything, it's the opposite. So this isn't a player forcing his way out. It's a team deciding what it wants to be. Availability depends entirely on Milwaukee's patience, not Ware's.

## QUESTION TWO: IS HE GOOD ENOUGH?

This one is easier. Ware is coming off a genuinely solid season: 11 points and 9 rebounds a game in about 22 minutes, a block per game, and — this is the number that matters for New York — 39% from three. He only missed three games all season. That's durability, production, and a shooting stroke from a young seven-footer, all in one profile. Compared to his rookie year, the three-point shot became better and more frequent, and he simply looked more comfortable and confident playing the game. That's the trajectory you want from a young big: steady growth, healthy, adding a skill each season.

But there's a catch in his file, and it's worth taking seriously. Ware never fully earned the trust of Erik Spoelstra in Miami. And when one of the best coaches in the league doesn't fully trust a young player, you have to ask why. Was it effort? Consistency? Defensive discipline? The honest answer is we don't know the details from the outside. O'Connor's framing is fair: it'll be fascinating to see what Ware does with a longer leash under Milwaukee's new head coach, Taylor Jenkins. If he thrives with more freedom, his trade value goes up — and so does his price. If he struggles, he gets cheaper, but the question mark gets bigger too. Either way, the production says he's good enough to help a contender. The Spoelstra question says the Knicks should do their homework.

## QUESTION THREE: DOES HE FIT THE KNICKS?

Here's where it gets exciting. Ware fits two different holes on this roster at once. Hole one: shot-blocking. Since Mitchell Robinson left for Boston, the Knicks don't have a true rim protector behind Towns. Ware averages a block per game in limited minutes and has the length and timing to protect the paint. Hole two: floor spacing from a big. And this is what makes him different from almost everyone else on the target list. Wiseman, Eubanks, Bradley, Edey — none of them shoot threes like Ware. A big man who blocks shots on one end and hits 39% from deep on the other is rare.

Now picture the lineups. Ware next to KAT: two seven-footers who can both shoot, pulling opposing centers away from the rim and opening the whole floor for Brunson's drives. Defensively, Ware takes the rim-protection job so KAT doesn't have to. Ware next to Drummond: a stretch big paired with a glass-eater, the two halves of what Robinson and Towns used to give this team. And Ware in a frontcourt with KAT and OG: length, switching, shooting, and size everywhere, a lineup that could punish opponents on both ends. Ware can also slide to the four in bigger lineups, which gives Mike Brown flexibility he simply doesn't have right now.

And compare him to the player he would really be replacing: Mitchell Robinson. Mitch was an elite offensive rebounder and a great shot-blocker, but he never shot from outside, and opponents could hack him at the free-throw line in big moments. Ware gives you some of the rim protection, a lot less offensive rebounding, but something Mitch never had at all: real spacing. In a playoff series, that means opponents can't leave him alone to help on Brunson's drives. Different player, different strengths — but in some ways a better fit for an offense built around KAT at the elbow and Brunson attacking the paint.

That's why Brian Windhorst said back in June — before the Finals ended, before the Giannis trade happened — that if Ware ever got shipped to Milwaukee, he'd be on the phone. Well, Ware got shipped to Milwaukee. Windhorst's logic is the same as ours: a big who stretches the floor, plays with high energy, and blocks shots is the kind of player plenty of teams should be calling about.

## QUESTION FOUR: WHAT WOULD IT COST?

And now the hard part, because this is where the dream meets the second apron. Let's be honest: getting Ware would be a long shot. Not impossible, but expensive. A young, cheap, improving center with a 39% three-point stroke is exactly the type of player every team wants in this CBA era. Milwaukee won't give him away for second-round picks.

So what does New York have? The salary pieces are the same ones we've discussed for weeks: Dadiet's $2.9 million and Deuce McBride's $4 million expiring deal, plus Tyler Kolek's rookie contract. The war chest of up to twelve second-round picks helps. But the real problem is first-round capital. The Knicks are extremely limited there — the picks they sent out to build this championship roster mean they can't trade a first-round pick before 2033. That's a long way away, and it's a pick you have to think very carefully about. Some fans say it's worth it for a player with Ware's potential, and honestly, I understand that argument. A 2033 first for a young starting-caliber big who fits perfectly next to KAT? That's a conversation worth having. But Milwaukee would probably want more than one distant pick, and that's where Deuce becomes the uncomfortable question again.

Here's my line, and it's consistent with everything this channel has said: for a BACKUP center, Deuce is too expensive. For Ware, though, the math is different. Ware isn't a backup-only player — he's a young big who could start for some teams and grow into a long-term piece. If the Knicks ever traded Deuce, Ware is the kind of return that would actually justify it: a player who solves the center problem now AND for years to come, on a cheap contract that fits under the apron. I'm not saying do it today. I'm saying if the phone call ever gets serious, Ware is one of the very few names on the board worth that conversation.

## THE COMPETITION: WHO ELSE IS CALLING MILWAUKEE?

Let's look at O'Connor's list team by team, because the competition tells you how hard this would be. Boston already added Mitchell Robinson this summer, so their need is smaller, but the Celtics always have assets and always shop. The Lakers are a big-market team that never stops hunting and could offer young players New York doesn't have. Detroit is fascinating: if the Jalen Duren standoff goes badly, the Pistons might suddenly need a young center, and they have picks to spend. And then there's Philadelphia — and this one just changed. As we covered in the preseason report card, Ariel Hukporti went down with an Achilles injury in the first exhibition game. The Sixers signed him to be their backup center. If he's out for the season, Philly suddenly has the exact same hole New York has, plus more trade flexibility. That makes them a dangerous new rival for every big on the market, Ware included. Chicago, Dallas, and Orlando round out the list, each with their own reasons.

What does that mean for the Knicks? Simple: New York can't win an auction. They don't have the young players or the first-round picks that Detroit, the Lakers, or Philly can offer. Their only real edge is speed and clarity — knowing exactly what they need and being ready to move the moment Milwaukee is willing to listen. That's the same lesson from the "trade early" video, and Ware makes it even more urgent.

## WHAT A REALISTIC PACKAGE COULD LOOK LIKE

Let me sketch what a deal could look like, and I want to be clear: this is my own analysis, not a reported offer. Version one, the "no Deuce" version: Dadiet's expiring salary, Tyler Kolek's rookie deal, several second-round picks, and maybe a pick swap. The salaries can work, and Milwaukee gets a young guard in Kolek plus draft capital for its rebuild. The problem: it might not be enough for a player with Ware's upside, especially if other teams bid. Version two, the "serious" version: add the 2033 first-round pick. That's a real price, but it's a pick so far away that its value is uncertain, and for a rebuilding team, future firsts are exactly what they want. Version three, the "all-in" version: Deuce McBride plus picks. That's the expensive one, and as I said, I'd only consider it for a player like Ware — someone who can be a long-term starter, not just a backup.

My preference is version two. It keeps Deuce, it gives Milwaukee real future value, and it lands the Knicks a young two-way big who fits perfectly beside KAT. Would Milwaukee say yes? Maybe not right away. But if the Bucks are struggling in December, that offer starts to look a lot more attractive.

## QUESTION FIVE: WHEN IS THE RIGHT TIME?

And this ties directly to the strategy we laid out in the "trade early" video. The Knicks know exactly what they need: a big man. Milwaukee doesn't know what it is yet. That's an advantage. If the Bucks start slowly, they become sellers. And if New York waits until December 15th, when trade season really opens, they'll be competing with every team on O'Connor's list — Boston, the Lakers, Detroit, Philly, and more. The best window might be early: as soon as Milwaukee shows it's not going anywhere, before the bidding war starts.

There's also a usage angle. If Ware gets a bigger role under Jenkins and starts putting up big numbers, his trade value could explode. A young big having a breakout season on a losing team is exactly the kind of player sellers flip at the deadline for a big return. So New York has to time it carefully: early enough to beat the competition, but with enough information to know what they're getting.

One more timing factor that often gets ignored: player development under a new coach. Taylor Jenkins is brand new in Milwaukee, and new coaches usually want to see what they have before they agree to move young players. That means the first month or two of the season is probably a "wait and see" period for the Bucks. The sweet spot for New York could be right after that — late November or early December, when Milwaukee's record and Jenkins' evaluation both start to come into focus.

## THE WISEMAN UPDATE: STILL THE FAVORITE

Now, none of this means the camp battle doesn't matter. Stefan Bondy reported that James Wiseman has impressed people who've watched camp and should be the favorite for the open spot — with an important caution: there's still preseason to play. That matches everything we saw in the first exhibition, when Wiseman showed real flashes. And here's the beautiful part: a Ware trade and a Wiseman signing aren't either/or. Wiseman would be the third or fourth center, a depth piece who can grow. Ware would be a real rotation upgrade. The best version of this season might include both.

## THE KONCHAR DOOR AND THE ROSTER MATH

One more roster note. The Knicks want to expand their final roster spots from one to two, and there are a few ways to get there. One option: trade for a big man, then use the open spot on a wing. Another: trade for a wing, then sign one of the big men outright. And speaking of wings — remember John Konchar, who was waived recently to deal with personal matters. If his situation allows, it's at least possible the Knicks could revisit that door down the line. Bruce Brown and Ochai Agbaji are still in camp too. The front office likes its wing options. What it still needs is size — and that's exactly why a name like Ware keeps coming up.

## THE BOTTOM LINE

So let's score the five questions. Is Ware available? Maybe — the Bucks seem more likely to move Turner, but a slow start could change everything. Is he good enough? Yes — 11 and 9 with a block and 39% from three, plus durability, with one question mark about why Miami's coach never fully trusted him. Does he fit? Perfectly — shot-blocking and spacing, next to KAT, Drummond, or OG. What would it cost? A lot — probably first-round capital the Knicks barely have, maybe even a conversation about Deuce. And when? Early, if New York wants to beat the crowd. It's a long shot, Tunnel fam. But it's the rare long shot that could fix the center position for years and correct a draft-night what-if at the same time. That's worth at least one phone call to Milwaukee.
