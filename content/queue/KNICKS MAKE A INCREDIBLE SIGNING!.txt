## THE HEADLINE, HONESTLY

Quick clarity, the NBA Tunnel way: the Knicks haven't made a new signing. Nobody has been added to the roster. The "incredible signing" angle in this video is about a player they signed in the offseason, Jordan Clarkson. He's been one of the best values on the team when he plays, and that's the problem. He barely plays. So no, there's no breaking news. There's a coaching decision worth questioning, and that's what we're doing today.

## WHERE THE KNICKS STAND

Let's set the scene. The defending champions are 2–4 and sit 12th in the Eastern Conference. Philadelphia has beaten them twice in less than two weeks: 106–100 on opening night at the Garden, the night the championship banner went up, and 98–93 on Friday, when LeBron James and Tyrese Maxey closed the door late.

There's plenty of blame to go around. The stars haven't consistently looked like themselves. Mistakes have been costly, and close games keep slipping away. Josh Hart even admitted the team is feeling fatigue. So when a team is searching for answers, the first place to look is simple: who's actually producing? The answer points straight at Clarkson.

## FIX NUMBER ONE: PLAY JORDAN CLARKSON

**The raw numbers:** At first glance, his numbers don't jump off the page: 5.2 points per game. But that figure hides how little he's been on the floor.

- **Minutes per game:** 8.6
- **Games over 10 minutes:** one
- **Detroit game:** a coach's decision, DNP
- **Outside Chicago:** just 25 total minutes across four games

**The impact numbers:**

- **Plus-minus:** plus-32, the best on the roster. Landry Shamet is next at plus-20.
- **Net rating with Clarkson on the floor:** plus-38.1 in his 43 minutes.
- **Net rating with Clarkson on the bench:** minus-7.9 in about 245 minutes.

For a team that has struggled to score and find its rhythm, that gap is hard to ignore.

## THE HONEST CAVEATS

Let's be fair, because these numbers have limits.

**The sample is tiny.** Forty-three minutes is less than one full game. One great stretch can swing net rating wildly.

**One game is doing a lot of the work.** A big part of his total comes from the Chicago comeback.

**Opponents and game situations matter.** Some of his minutes came against weaker lineups or at moments when the game had already shifted.

So no, I'm not telling you Clarkson is secretly the Knicks' best player. But even with all those caveats, the eye test backs up the data. Every time he checks in, something good seems to happen.

## THE EVIDENCE: CHICAGO AND PHILLY

**Chicago, Wednesday:** The Knicks started slowly against a Bulls team they were expected to handle and trailed by as many as 19. In the second half, Clarkson changed the game. He scored 18 points on seven-of-11 shooting in about 16 minutes, made key defensive plays including timely steals, and helped push the Knicks to a 133–127 comeback win. Brunson led the way with 38, but Clarkson's bench scoring was a huge reason they climbed back.

**Philadelphia, Friday:** The Knicks trailed by 14 in the third quarter. Clarkson came in and brought energy again. He finished plus-five in about nine minutes, and his presence helped swing the momentum. Then he sat back down. The Knicks briefly took the lead on Brunson's three with 1:32 left but couldn't close it out.

The obvious question: would the result have been different if Brown had turned to him sooner and trusted him longer? We'll never know. But on a night when Anfernee Simons outscored the entire Knicks bench, leaving your hottest bench scorer at nine minutes is a choice worth questioning.

## THE NEW CLARKSON

What makes this even more puzzling is how much Clarkson has changed his game.

Earlier in his career, he was known as a high-volume shot creator: a bench scorer who could pile up points but didn't always fit a team concept. Every possession was a chance for Clarkson to get his.

In New York, he has reinvented himself as more of a role player who plays within the system. He moves the ball, takes what the defense gives him, and fits next to the starters. But he still has that old switch. When the offense stalls, he can still take over a stretch on his own.

That combination, a team player who can create when everything breaks down, is exactly what a struggling team should be looking for. And this offense has broken down a lot. Remember the end of the first half in Philly, when a 22-second possession produced no shot? That's a Clarkson moment.

## WHY BROWN'S OWN LOGIC POINTS TO CLARKSON

Here's the interesting part. Mike Brown has said that the early struggles are changing his priorities. He said he'd move away from his plan to give young forward Mohamed Diawara more playing time because the team needs to get back on track.

That makes sense. When a defending champion starts 2–4, development takes a back seat to winning. But that same logic should apply to Clarkson. If winning games right now is the top priority, keeping a productive veteran glued to the bench is hard to explain.

And there's a fatigue angle too. Hart said the whole team is feeling it. Brunson went one-for-five in the final stretch in Philly. A guy who can give you 15 to 18 productive minutes of offense takes pressure off Brunson's legs. Clarkson's minutes are a fatigue solution.

## THE OTHER SIDE: WHY BROWN MIGHT BE DOING IT

To be fair, let's think about Brown's side. This is my analysis, not something he's said.

- **Defense.** Clarkson has never been known as a stopper. Against teams with big, physical wings, Brown might prefer a stronger defender.
- **The guard logjam.** Brunson, Deuce, Kolek, Shamet, and Alvarado once he's healthy. There are only so many backcourt minutes to go around.
- **Kolek's emergence.** With Kolek playing so well as the backup point guard, Clarkson's role naturally shrinks.
- **Matchups.** Brown adjusts constantly. Some nights, the matchup just doesn't call for Clarkson.

Those are all real reasons. But Brown has also told everyone that minutes are earned, and that only five rotation spots are locked. Clarkson has earned more than nine minutes a night.

## MY CLARKSON PROPOSAL

Here's what I'd do. Give him a consistent role: 15 to 18 minutes a night as the second-unit scorer, playing next to Kolek. Kolek runs the offense and creates; Clarkson scores and breaks down defenses when possessions stall. That's a natural fit. And when Alvarado comes back, his defense can cover some of Clarkson's weaknesses on that end.

A coach looking for answers should start with the players who are already giving him results. Right now, Clarkson is one of them, and the Knicks can't afford to waste a hot hand while they're climbing out of a hole.

## FIX NUMBER TWO: UNLOCK KAT, THE PLAYMAKER

Now the second fix, and it might be even bigger. Karl-Anthony Towns has been one of the Knicks' most reliable players through six games, with six straight double-doubles. But there's a strong case that the Knicks still haven't tapped into everything he can do as a playmaker.

**The key: how often he attacks the basket.**

| Span | Drives per 36 minutes |
|---|---|
| Last regular season | More than 9.4 |
| The championship run | Just under 9 |
| Early this season | Around 7 |

That's a meaningful drop. And it matters because driving isn't just a scoring tool for Towns anymore. It's become one of his best ways to set up teammates.

## THE PLAYOFF TRANSFORMATION

This is the part that gets overlooked. Towns became a different player during the title run.

From Game 4 of the first-round series against Atlanta through the NBA Finals, Towns passed the ball on more than 39 percent of his drives. Before that stretch, his highest mark over a full season was 26.6 percent.

That's a huge change. Instead of putting his head down and forcing shots, he started collapsing the defense and finding teammates spotting up on the perimeter or cutting to the rim. Towns went from scorer to playmaker when it mattered most.

And he's carried that habit into this season. He's passing on nearly 30 percent of his drives and recording an assist on roughly one out of every nine. It's a small sample, but if those numbers hold, they'd be the best of his career.

So the problem isn't that Towns has stopped making the right reads, or that he's forgotten what made him so dangerous last spring. It's that he isn't getting enough chances to make them.

## THE TOUCHES PARADOX

There are signs the Knicks are leaning on him in other ways. Towns has more frontcourt touches than last season, his time of possession is up, and he's making more passes per game, all while playing fewer minutes. That shows the coaching staff trusts him to handle the ball and run the offense from the elbow and the high post.

But touches at the top of the key aren't the same as attacking downhill. When Towns catches the ball and stands still, defenses can stay home. When he puts it on the floor and drives, they have to react. That's when the floor opens up.

**Why his assist numbers aren't higher:**

- **Some passes are high risk.** He's attempting tough reads, and some of those turn into turnovers.
- **The team is in a shooting slump.** When teammates miss the open shots Towns creates, those passes don't show up in the box score. Remember the nine-of-38 night against Orlando.

The bigger picture: outside of garbage time, the Knicks rank 20th in points per possession, according to Cleaning the Glass. That's not the offense anyone expected from the defending champions.

## THE DETROIT BLUEPRINT

The good news? We've already seen what this looks like. In the win over Detroit, the Knicks finally played through Towns the way they did in the playoffs. He finished with 27 points and 20 rebounds and stayed on the floor without foul trouble. He scored on drives, made plays out of screen actions, and gave the Knicks a clear focal point.

That was the Knicks' first win of the season, and it wasn't a coincidence that it came on a night when Towns was at the center of everything.

Foul trouble has been the one real blemish on his start. When Towns has to sit, the Knicks lose their best interior option, and the offense often stalls. Staying on the floor is a big part of unlocking his full impact.

## HOW TO GET MORE OUT OF KAT

So how do the Knicks get more out of him? Two ideas.

**Option one: shift some touches his way.** Brown has experimented with giving OG Anunoby a bigger offensive role, and we've talked about OG's growth as a passer. That idea has merit. But some of those possessions could be better used by putting the ball in Towns' hands.

**Option two: run more ball screens for him.** Use Towns as the ball handler in pick-and-roll. That gives him a running start toward the basket and forces defenders into quick decisions: switch, help, or recover. Every one of those choices creates a passing lane or a scoring chance.

Towns has the tools to make this work. He has the strength to bully smaller defenders and the mobility to get past bigger ones. Very few centers in the league can handle the ball and pass the way he can. The Knicks just need to put him in positions to use those skills more often.

## THE CONTRACT BACKDROP

There's also an off-court backdrop. Towns' extension talks were tabled before the season, and many people, including fans and maybe Towns himself, expected a deal to be done by now. Instead, he's the most consistent starter on a 2–4 team while playing for his next contract.

That cuts both ways. Every big night strengthens his case for the number he wants, reportedly in the $225 to $240 million range according to Brett Siegel. And if the Knicks build the offense around his playmaking and it works, it'll be very hard for James Dolan to justify not paying him. For the Knicks, unlocking KAT isn't just about winning in November. It's about how much they'll pay to keep him.

## HOW THE TWO FIXES CONNECT

Here's why these two fixes belong together. Both are about using players who are already producing, more and better.

Clarkson gives you scoring when the offense stalls. KAT's driving gives you playmaking that breaks defenses down. Put them together and the Knicks get exactly what they've been missing: easier shots, more ball movement, and less dependence on Brunson creating everything.

Here's one lineup I'd love to see for a few minutes a night, and this is just my idea: Kolek, Clarkson, Bridges, OG, and Towns. Kolek organizes. Clarkson scores. Bridges and OG defend the wings and space the floor. And KAT gets the ball at the top with room to attack downhill, while Brunson rests and saves his legs for the fourth quarter. It's a group with passing, shooting, size, and a center who can create. On paper, it checks a lot of boxes.

And neither fix requires a trade, a signing, or a front-office move. It's all in Mike Brown's hands.
